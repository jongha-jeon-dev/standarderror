# Batch Normalisation Normalises the Batch, Not the Example

Batch normalisation is usually described in one sentence, which is the sentence under test: *batch normalisation normalises each example*, rescaling its activations to a standard range. A practitioner meets it in almost every convolutional network, and relies on it whenever a normalised model is trained in small batches, fed batches sorted by label or source, or accidentally served in training mode. If the sentence is false, and the statistics used to normalise an example come from the other examples around it, then an example's prediction depends on its companions in the batch, and the network computes different functions during training and at inference.

This chapter first defines the layer and its two modes. It then takes a trained network and measures how often one test digit's predicted label changes when the digit is placed in different random batches, at several batch sizes and with companions drawn from a single class. It explains the extreme case of a batch of two exactly. It then trains networks at each batch size, with and without normalisation, and shows that small batches make the normalised network worse than an unnormalised one. Finally it describes what the common alternatives change.

## A layer with two modes

Chapter 23 compared optimisers on networks with nothing between their layers but activations. Most modern networks have a *normalisation layer* there as well. *Batch normalisation* (Ioffe and Szegedy), the first to be widely used, operates on each unit separately. For a unit whose values on the $B$ examples of a minibatch are $x_1, \dots, x_B$, it computes the batch mean and variance,

$$
\mu_B = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} x_i, \qquad \sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} (x_i - \mu_B)^2,
$$

normalises each value, and then applies a learned scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$:

$$
\hat x_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y_i = \gamma \hat x_i + \beta,
$$

where $\epsilon$ is a small constant that prevents division by zero. Training with the layer is faster and less sensitive to the initial scale of the weights, the problem Chapter 21 measured.

The mean and standard deviation in these formulas are not the example's. They are the batch's. During training, the layer is in *training mode*: an example's output is computed from statistics of the other examples it happens to be batched with. The layer also keeps *running averages* of $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$, exponentially weighted averages updated at every training step. At inference it switches to *inference mode* (also called evaluation mode) and uses those running averages instead, so that a prediction depends on the example alone. The two modes compute two different functions, and the question is how different they are.

## One digit, many batches

The network is fully connected, 64-128-128-10 (64 pixel inputs, two hidden layers, ten outputs), with batch normalisation before each ReLU activation. It is trained on half the digits with SGD (momentum 0.9, learning rate 0.05) in batches of 64 for 20 epochs. Then 100 test digits are taken, and each digit's prediction is computed in training mode inside 200 different random batches of other test digits. The running averages are frozen during this evaluation, so that evaluating does not change the network.

The block prints the network's accuracy on the 100 digits in inference mode and, for each batch size, how often a digit's training-mode label differs from its inference-mode label, together with the training-mode accuracy. Its last line repeats the batch-of-16 case with all 15 companions drawn from one class.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

r = nets.batchnorm_dependence()   # 100 test digits, 200 batches each
print(f"inference mode: accuracy {r['sample_eval_acc']:.3f}")
for row in r["rows"]:
    print(f"training mode, batch of {row['batch']:2d}: label changes in "
          f"{row['flip']:5.1%} of batches, accuracy {row['accuracy']:.3f}")
print(f"batch of 16 from one class: same label as inference mode in "
      f"{r['skewed_agree']:.1%}")
```

```text
inference mode: accuracy 0.990
training mode, batch of  2: label changes in 45.3% of batches, accuracy 0.540
training mode, batch of  4: label changes in 20.5% of batches, accuracy 0.786
training mode, batch of 16: label changes in  3.2% of batches, accuracy 0.958
training mode, batch of 64: label changes in  0.6% of batches, accuracy 0.984
batch of 16 from one class: same label as inference mode in 77.4%
```

In inference mode the network gets 0.990 of these digits right. In training mode, inside random batches of 2, a digit's label differs from its inference-mode label in **45.3%** of batches, and every one of the 100 digits changes label in at least one of its batches. In batches of 64, the size the network was trained with, the label changes in 0.6% of batches. The figure below shows how the rate of change falls with the batch size, and the effect of single-class companions.

## The companions are an input

The dependence falls with batch size because the batch mean and variance are estimates of the population mean and variance, and like any sample estimates they settle towards the population values as the sample grows. In batches of 64 the batch statistics should be close to the running averages, and training mode computes nearly the same function as inference mode. With 16 examples the estimates are noisier: in a batch of 16 the label still changes in 3.2% of batches, and 46% of the digits change label in at least one of their 200.

### A larger batch is not enough; it must be a random sample

The dependence does not fall merely because the batch is large. It falls because a random batch is a *random sample* of the population, so its statistics estimate the population's. The last line of the output keeps the batch at 16 but draws the 15 companions from a single class, as happens when data arrive sorted by label, by date or by source. The digit's label now differs from its inference-mode label in **22.6%** of batches, 7 times as often as with random companions. The batch statistics describe the companions; the companions are all 3s, or all 7s; and the network normalises the digit against them, as if it were being compared with a population made entirely of one digit. A batch statistic is only a good stand-in for the population statistic when the batch is a representative sample, and no batch size repairs a biased sample.

### A batch of two keeps only an ordering

Batches of 2 show the limiting case, and it can be worked out exactly. For two values $a$ and $b$ of a unit, the batch mean is their midpoint and the standard deviation (with the $1/B$ divisor above) is half their distance:

$$
\mu_B = \frac{a + b}{2}, \qquad \sigma_B = \frac{|a - b|}{2}.
$$

Normalising gives

$$
\hat a = \frac{a - (a+b)/2}{|a-b|/2} = \frac{a - b}{|a - b|} = \pm 1,
$$

and $\hat b = -\hat a$. Before the learned scale and shift, then, every unit is $+1$ for one example and $-1$ for the other, whatever the two values were, unless they were almost equal, when $\epsilon$ in the denominator dominates. The layer passes on only which of the two examples was larger on each unit; all information about the magnitudes is gone. A network that has to classify from that is classifying a pair, not a digit.

## Training in small batches

That last observation matters most during training, because training always runs in training mode. A network trained in batches of 2 learns to classify from the ±1 patterns of pairs. At inference the layer uses running averages instead, a normalisation that no example ever received during training, so the network is asked to compute a function it was never trained to compute.

The second experiment trains the same architecture with and without batch normalisation at each batch size, with Adam at a learning rate of 1e-3 for 20 epochs, over seeds 0 to 2, and scores it on the full test half. For the normalised network it reports accuracy in inference mode and, in parentheses, in training mode on test batches of the same size.

```python
for row in nets.batchnorm_batch_size():
    print(f"batches of {row['batch']:2d}: plain {row['plain']:.3f}  "
          f"batch norm {row['bn']:.3f}  "
          f"(training mode {row['bn_train_mode']:.3f})")
```

```text
batches of  2: plain 0.972  batch norm 0.863  (training mode 0.580)
batches of  4: plain 0.971  batch norm 0.966  (training mode 0.866)
batches of 16: plain 0.970  batch norm 0.975  (training mode 0.953)
batches of 64: plain 0.967  batch norm 0.975  (training mode 0.972)
```

Without normalisation, accuracy is about the same at every batch size, 0.967 to 0.972. With it, the network trained in batches of 2 scores **0.863** in inference mode, and 0.580 when evaluated in training mode on test batches of 2: normalisation cost it 0.109. In batches of 4 the cost is 0.006. From batches of 16 up, normalisation helps, by 0.005 and 0.008, in the direction it is known for. Over three seeds, though, the smaller gain amounts to 4.7 of the 899 test digits, and the spread across seeds was not measured, so the size of the gain is not established. In batches of 64 the two modes agree to within a point, 0.975 and 0.972. That agreement is the condition under which the running averages are a faithful stand-in for the batch statistics. ## What the alternatives change

Three alternatives address the dependence in different ways. *Batch renormalisation* (Ioffe) corrects the batch statistics towards the running averages during training, so that the function trained is closer to the one used at inference; it was designed for small and non-independent batches. *Layer normalisation* and *group normalisation* (Wu and He) compute the mean and variance over the units of one example, or over groups of them, instead of over the examples of one batch. The output then never depends on the companions, and the two modes are the same function. Transformers use layer normalisation, and are free of the problem.

The practical failures of batch normalisation follow directly from the measurements:

- A model left in training mode at serving time gives answers that depend on what else is being served with them.
- A batch assembled from one class, one customer or one day normalises every member against an unrepresentative sample.
- Gradient accumulation over many tiny batches, used to fit a large model in memory, still normalises over each tiny batch, not over the accumulated one.
- A fine-tuning run in small batches can shift the running statistics away from the population the network was trained on.

Batch normalisation is one layer whose behaviour changes between training and inference; dropout, the subject of Chapter 25, is another.

## In practice

- **Check the mode at serving time.** Confirm that every normalisation layer is in inference mode when the model serves predictions; a training-mode model's answers depend on the rest of the batch.
- **Shuffle before batching.** Make sure batches at training time are random samples. Data sorted by label, date or source give batch statistics that describe the sort, not the population.
- **Avoid batch normalisation with very small batches.** Below a batch size at which training-mode and inference-mode accuracy agree, consider layer or group normalisation, or batch renormalisation (none was tested here).
- **Compare the two modes on held-out data.** Evaluate a trained network in both modes, inside random batches of the training size. Close agreement is evidence that the running averages are trustworthy.
- **Do not rely on gradient accumulation to enlarge the normalisation batch.** It enlarges the gradient's batch, not the statistics'.
- **Watch the running statistics when fine-tuning.** Freeze them, or check them against the original data, when fine-tuning in small or unrepresentative batches.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Fully connected layers on small images.** In convolutional networks the statistics are pooled over all spatial positions as well as over the batch, so a batch of 2 images contributes many values per channel, and the dependence is likely weaker than here; that was not measured.
- **Frozen running statistics in the first study.** Their momentum was set to zero so that the evaluation did not change the network. In a real training-mode deployment the running averages would also drift with every batch.
- **One architecture, one data set.** The batch size at which normalisation turns from hurting to helping, between 4 and 16 here, depends on the network and on how much the batch statistics vary, and is not a general constant.
- **Adam at one learning rate.** The batch-size study holds the learning rate at 1e-3 for every batch size. Chapter 22 showed, for plain SGD, that the best rate moves with the batch, and Adam's rate is unlikely to be immune, so the plain network's flat line across batch sizes is partly a matter of where 1e-3 happens to sit.

## Key points

1. Batch normalisation normalises with the batch's statistics. In training mode one digit's label changed with its random companions in 45% of batches of 2 and 0.6% of batches of 64.
2. The companions must be a random sample. With 15 companions from one class, the label changed in 23% of batches of 16, against 3.2% with random ones.
3. In a batch of 2 every normalised unit is ±1 before the learned scale and shift: the layer keeps only which example was larger. Trained that way, the network scored 0.863 in inference mode, against 0.972 without normalisation.
4. From batches of 16 up, normalisation scored 0.5 and 0.8 points higher over three seeds, and at 64 the two modes agreed to within a point.
5. Before trusting batch normalisation, check that batches are large enough and mixed enough that their statistics are the population's.
