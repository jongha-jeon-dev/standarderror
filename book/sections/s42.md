# Stacking Learns to Pick the Best Model, Given Honest Predictions

Chapter 41 ended with an average of models dragged down by a member that was too weak or too sure of itself. The obvious repair is to stop weighting members equally and let the data decide how much each one counts. Wolpert called this *stacked generalisation* in 1992: fit a second model, the *meta-model*, whose inputs are the base models' predictions and whose target is the label. Stacking is a standard ingredient of competition-winning pipelines, and it is sold with the sentence this chapter tests: *stacking learns the best way to combine models.* If the sentence is false, a stack can quietly hand its decisions to the worst model in the pool, or cost several times the compute of a single model for no gain.

The chapter fits a meta-model in two ways, on the base models' in-sample predictions and on their out-of-fold predictions, and compares both with the members. It then reads the meta-model's weights to see what each version trusted, asks whether the honest version actually combines or merely selects, and sets it against the plain average. It ends with the two baselines a stack has to beat and the price of fitting it honestly.

## Two ways to build the meta-model's inputs

The task is the simulated one from Chapter 4: two Gaussian classes in 10 dimensions with a curved Bayes boundary and a Bayes error of 0.268, the lowest error any classifier can achieve. Training sets have 100, 300, 1,000 and 3,000 rows (20, 10, 6 and 3 data sets of each size), and every model is scored on the same 20,000 separate test rows.

There are four base models: logistic regression, an RBF support vector machine with Platt-scaled probabilities, a random forest of 200 trees, and 1-nearest-neighbour (1-NN). The meta-model is a logistic regression with a very weak penalty (C = 10,000) whose inputs are the four base models' predicted probabilities of the positive class. The question is which predictions it is fitted on.

- **In-sample predictions.** Each base model is fitted on the training rows and then predicts those same rows. This is quick, and it is what a naive implementation does.
- **Out-of-fold predictions.** The training rows are split into five stratified folds. For each fold, a copy of each base model is trained on the other four folds and predicts the held-out fold, so every training row's prediction comes from a model that never saw that row. The base models are then refitted on all rows to produce test-time inputs.

The code below runs both versions at each training size and prints the accuracy of the SVM, of 1-NN and of the two stacks.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

for r in combine.stacking_study(with_1nn=True):
    acc = r["acc"]
    print(f"{r['n']:5d} rows: SVM {acc['SVM']:.3f}, 1-NN {acc['1-NN']:.3f}"
          f" | stacks {acc['stack, in-sample']:.3f} (in-sample), "
          f"{acc['stack, out-of-fold']:.3f} (OOF)")
```

```text
  100 rows: SVM 0.597, 1-NN 0.566 | stacks 0.566 (in-sample), 0.589 (OOF)
  300 rows: SVM 0.672, 1-NN 0.577 | stacks 0.577 (in-sample), 0.670 (OOF)
 1000 rows: SVM 0.702, 1-NN 0.594 | stacks 0.594 (in-sample), 0.702 (OOF)
 3000 rows: SVM 0.718, 1-NN 0.600 | stacks 0.600 (in-sample), 0.718 (OOF)
```

On in-sample predictions the stack's accuracy equals 1-NN's at every size, to the last digit: 0.594 at 1,000 rows, the worst of the four models, while the SVM scores 0.702. On out-of-fold predictions the stack scores 0.702, the SVM's accuracy. The same procedure, fed two kinds of input, became two different single models, as the figure below shows.

![On in-sample predictions the stack became 1-NN (0.594 at 1,000 rows); on out-of-fold ones, the SVM (0.702 against 0.702).](ml142-f0-stack.png){alt="The in-sample stack's line lies exactly on the 1-NN line, lowest of all; the out-of-fold stack's line lies on the SVM's from 300 rows on; the average lies between."}

## The meta-model rewards whichever model has seen the answers

### In-sample, memorisation looks like skill

The meta-model's weights explain the result. A 1-NN classifier predicts each training row from its nearest neighbour in the training set, and each training row is its own nearest neighbour. On in-sample predictions 1-NN is therefore right about every training row, and its probabilities, like the single tree's in Chapter 41, are all 0 or 1. The forest, whose trees are grown deep, is nearly as perfect on its own training rows.

The meta-model thus sees one input that predicts the label without error. Because that input separates the training labels perfectly, the unpenalised likelihood would push its weight towards infinity, as Chapter 6 showed for logistic regression on separable data; the weak penalty keeps it finite, at +12.9. On test rows the meta-model then reproduces 1-NN, which there is the weakest model of the four. Breiman made the point for regression in 1996: the inputs to the meta-model, which he called the level-one data, must come from predictions the base models did not fit, or the combination rewards overfitting.

The mechanism is not specific to 1-NN. Replace 1-NN with 25-NN, which averages over 25 neighbours and cannot memorise its training rows, and the same mistake picks the next best memoriser: the in-sample meta-model gives the forest a weight of +28.5. Its log loss, 1.089 at 1,000 rows, is nearly twice the SVM's 0.570 and worse than any member's. The meta-model is not learning which model is good; it is learning which model has seen the answers.

### Out-of-fold, the weight goes to the best model

On out-of-fold predictions every base model is judged on rows it did not see, so its inputs to the meta-model are as noisy as its test predictions will be. The weights are then logistic regression −0.24, SVM +4.56, forest +0.80 and 1-NN −0.18. Almost all the weight is on the SVM, and 1-NN gets nothing. The table below sets the in-sample and out-of-fold weights side by side.

Out-of-fold fits put about 4 on the SVM and little elsewhere.](ml142-f1-weights.png){alt="A four-row table of meta-model weights. In-sample fits put weight 13 on 1-NN or 28 on the forest; out-of-fold fits put about 4 on the SVM and little elsewhere."}

## Honest stacking is mostly selection

Out-of-fold stacking avoids the trap. Whether it *combines* the models, producing something better than any of them, is a separate question. The next block uses the pool with 25-NN in place of 1-NN, so that no member is certain, and compares the out-of-fold stack with the best member at each size (the one with the highest test accuracy) and with a plain average of the four members' probabilities.

```python
for r in combine.stacking_study(with_1nn=False):   # 25-NN instead
    acc = r["acc"]
    best = r["best"]
    print(f"{r['n']:5d} rows: best member {best} {acc[best]:.3f}"
          f" | out-of-fold stack {acc['stack, out-of-fold']:.3f}, "
          f"average {acc['average']:.3f}")
```

```text
  100 rows: best member forest 0.602 | out-of-fold stack 0.590, average 0.606
  300 rows: best member SVM 0.672 | out-of-fold stack 0.669, average 0.660
 1000 rows: best member SVM 0.702 | out-of-fold stack 0.701, average 0.694
 3000 rows: best member SVM 0.718 | out-of-fold stack 0.718, average 0.710
```

### From 300 rows, the stack selects the SVM

From 300 rows up, in both pools, the out-of-fold stack is within 0.0035 of the best member and never more than 0.001 above it. The meta-model has learned to pick the SVM, which choosing the model with the best validation score would also have done.

### At 100 rows, estimating the weights costs more than it gains

At 100 rows the stack is worse than the best member: 0.589 against the forest's 0.602 in the pool with 1-NN. That comparison flatters the best member slightly, since a practitioner would have had to identify the forest by validation, with its own noise. But the stack's loss has a clear source. With 100 rows the out-of-fold predictions are noisy, each made by a model trained on four fifths of them, and the meta-model's four weights are estimated from those same 100 rows. The stack pays for estimating the combination.

### A plain average costs nothing to estimate

The plain average shows the other side of that trade. In the pool without 1-NN, at 100 rows, it scores 0.606, above every member and 0.016 above the stack, because equal weights cost nothing to estimate. From 300 rows on, logistic regression and 25-NN fall well behind the SVM, and equal weights let them drag the average below it, 0.694 against 0.702 at 1,000 rows, the dilution Chapter 41 described.

Forecasters know this pattern as the *forecast combination puzzle*: a simple average of forecasts often beats the weights estimated to combine them optimally. Smith and Wallis traced the puzzle to the estimation error in the weights, exactly the cost visible at 100 rows here. The table below collects the comparison at all four sizes.

## When stacking earns its place

Stacking improves on its best member when the members are good in different places: one model better in one region of the inputs, another elsewhere, and enough rows for the meta-model to learn where. On this task the SVM is best almost everywhere, so the right combination is close to "use the SVM", and the stack found it. On tasks where diverse models of similar quality each capture part of the structure, as in many competition data sets, out-of-fold stacking can beat every member; the measurement here says what to compare it with before believing that it has.

The comparison has two baselines, not one. The stack should beat the best member chosen by validation, or it is an expensive way of choosing. And it should beat the plain average, or its weights cost more to estimate than they gain.

Honest stacking also has a price that is easy to forget. With five folds, every base model is fitted once per fold and once more on all the rows, six fits instead of one. And the stack's own score needs data that played no part in building it; here that was 20,000 separate test rows. A stack scored on the same folds that produced its inputs would add to everything measured above the optimism Chapter 35 measured, where the best score in a search reflects the search's luck as well as the model's quality.

Comparing a stack with its best member, or any two models with each other, raises the question of how such a comparison should be tested. Before turning to it in Chapter 44, Chapter 43 looks at a model whose combination of evidence is built into its assumptions: naive Bayes.

## In practice

- **Fit the meta-model on out-of-fold predictions only.** In-sample predictions reward memorisation; the meta-model will hand its decisions to whichever base model fitted its training rows most closely.
- **Print the meta-model's weights.** If they concentrate on one model, the stack is selecting, and that model alone is the baseline to beat. A large weight on 1-NN, a deep tree or a deep forest can be a sign of leaked in-sample predictions.
- **Keep two baselines.** Score the stack, the base model with the best validation score and a plain average on the same held-out data. Keep the stack only if it beats both.
- **Be sceptical of stacks on small data.** With few rows, the noise in the weights can cost more than the combination gains; a plain average or a single validated model may do better.
- **Budget for the cost.** Count the extra fits, and keep a test set that neither the base models nor the meta-model ever saw.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One task with one dominant model.** The SVM's lead from 300 rows on is large, which makes selection the right answer. With members of equal quality and complementary errors, the out-of-fold stack has more to gain and the average is harder to beat.
- **A linear meta-model on probabilities.** Meta-models on log-odds, with non-negativity constraints on the weights (as Breiman recommended), or with the original features as extra inputs, behave differently; the in-sample trap is the same for all of them.
- **Few repetitions at large sizes.** There are three data sets at 3,000 rows, and differences of a thousandth there are within noise. The in-sample stack's exact match to 1-NN is not.
- **Accuracy and log loss only.** A stack tuned for AUC or for a cost-weighted metric would choose differently, by the same mechanism.

## Key points

1. Fitted on in-sample predictions, the meta-model gave its largest weight to the model that had memorised the most: 1-NN (weight +12.9), whose accuracy it reproduced exactly, or the forest when 1-NN was absent.
2. Fitted on out-of-fold predictions, it chose the SVM and matched it to within 0.0035 from 300 rows on, never more than 0.001 above it; at 100 rows it lost 0.013 to the best member.
3. A plain average beat every member at 100 rows when no member was certain, and trailed the best member once the weak members fell behind.
4. Honest stacking on this task was selection; whether it combines depends on members that are good in different places.
5. Fit stacks on out-of-fold predictions only, and keep a stack only if it beats both the best single model and the plain average on data neither has seen.
