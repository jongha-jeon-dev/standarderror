# Four Simulated Verdicts, Retried on Real Data

This book has leaned on simulations, deliberately. A simulation knows the truth, so a method's error can be measured exactly, and it can isolate one mechanism at a time: a planted shortcut, a known cost, a rare class with a known rate. Where scikit-learn's bundled data allowed, chapters replicated their findings on real data, but some verdicts were measured only on simulations. A practitioner reading them is entitled to ask whether *a finding measured on a simulation tells you what will happen on real data.* If it does not, the numbers in those chapters are properties of the simulated worlds and say little about any data set the reader owns.

This chapter takes four such verdicts and repeats each on real data with the original chapter's models and settings. For each it asks two separate questions: whether the direction of the effect replicates, and whether its size does. It closes with what the pattern of answers says about how to use a simulation's results.

## What a replication of a method can test

Morris, White and Crowther's guide to simulation studies makes the case for simulations and states their limit: the results apply to the data-generating processes simulated. A simulation shows what a method does to data of a particular kind; it cannot say how common that kind is.

Replication has a cautionary history. The Open Science Collaboration's attempt to replicate a hundred psychology studies found effects that were, on average, about half the size of the originals. The question here is narrower, because the four verdicts are about methods, not about the world: does the method behave on real data the way it behaved on simulated data?

The four were chosen because each was measured only on a simulation and each can be re-run on the bundled data with the original chapter's own code. They are resampling for a rare class (Chapter 5), the optimism of a tuned score (Chapter 35), the threshold for unequal costs (Chapter 46) and the model with the best AUC (Chapter 47). Each is repeated over 20 random splits.

## Rare classes: replicated, smaller

### The simulated verdict

Chapter 5 studied three common remedies for a rare positive class. *Oversampling* duplicates positive rows until the classes are balanced; *SMOTE* (synthetic minority oversampling) creates new positive rows by interpolating between existing ones and their nearest positive neighbours; *class weights* leave the rows alone and weight the positives more heavily in the loss. At a 2% positive rate in a simulation, all three left a logistic model's AUC unchanged and multiplied its predicted probabilities about elevenfold.

The AUC is unchanged because it depends only on how the model ranks rows, and for a logistic model rebalancing mainly shifts the intercept, which moves every score the same way and leaves the ranking nearly intact. The probabilities inflate because the model learns the prevalence it is trained on: balancing the classes tells it that positives are common.

### The real version

The real version asks whether a handwritten digit is an 8, about 10% of the digits, with logistic regression on standardised pixels: 1,000 images to train and 797 to test. The block below runs five splits and prints each method's AUC, mean predicted probability, the actual rate and the *log loss*, the average of $-\log$ of the probability given to the true class, which penalises confident wrong probabilities. The figures quoted afterwards are over all 20 splits.

```python
from standarderror.ml import deploy

r = deploy.replicate_imbalance(reps=5)
for m, v in r.items():
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {v['auc']:.4f}  mean predicted {v['mean_p']:.3f}"
          f"  (actual rate {v['rate']:.3f})  log loss {v['log_loss']:.3f}")
```

```text
plain         AUC 0.9701  mean predicted 0.098  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.120
oversample    AUC 0.9694  mean predicted 0.171  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.178
SMOTE         AUC 0.9704  mean predicted 0.157  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.157
class weights AUC 0.9702  mean predicted 0.182  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.184
```

Over the 20 splits, the AUC moved by at most 0.0009, paired across splits. The plain model's mean prediction matched the actual rate (0.098 against 0.101); the resampled ones predicted 1.6 to 1.9 times too much, and their log loss was 30% to 53% worse.

Both the mechanism and its direction carried over. The size did not, for a reason the simulation predicts. Balancing a 10% class moves the training prevalence by a factor of 5; balancing a 2% class moves it by a factor of 25. The inflation grows with that shift, though not in proportion to it, so a less rare class gives a smaller inflation.

## Tuning: replicated, a fifth of the size

Chapter 35 measured the *optimism* of hyperparameter search: the amount by which the best cross-validated score among many settings overstates how the chosen model performs on fresh data. Each setting's score is an estimate with noise, and picking the maximum picks, in part, the setting whose noise happened to be most favourable. This is the *winner's curse*. Chapter 35 searched 64 SVM settings by cross-validation on 200 simulated rows and found the best score 0.023 optimistic, with the tuned model no better on fresh data than the default.

On 200 breast-cancer tumours with the same grid, the best cross-validated score was 0.974, the tuned model scored 0.969 on the other 369 tumours, and the default also scored 0.969. The optimism was 0.005 (standard error 0.002), and tuning bought +0.000.

The direction replicated exactly: the search's best score overstated what it found, and what it found was the default. The size was a fifth, because the breast-cancer data are easy. The winner's curse needs spread among the settings' scores to exploit. When every reasonable setting scores near 97%, there is little spread. The simulation's task was chosen to be hard, with an error floor of about a quarter, which is where the winner's curse bites. Real problems lie at both ends, so the chapter's warning holds and its number does not travel. The table below summarises the four replications.

## Thresholds and AUC: half, and weaker

### The cost-derived threshold

Suppose a miss costs $r$ times as much as a false alarm. A row with calibrated probability $p$ of being positive should be flagged when the expected cost of clearing it, $p \cdot r$, exceeds the expected cost of flagging it, $1 - p$, that is, when

$$
p > \frac{1}{1 + r}.
$$

Chapter 46 set a miss to cost ten false alarms, so the threshold is $1/11$. In simulation it found that flagging at 0.5 cost 2.9 to 4 times as much as simply flagging everyone, while the threshold $1/(1 + r)$ came within 0.003 of the best possible for a recalibrated model.

On breast cancer, with 300 tumours to train, 269 to test, malignancy as the positive class and a missed malignancy costing ten false alarms, the cost-derived threshold lowered every one of the six models' costs. Logistic regression's fell from 0.330 to 0.147 per tumour. But at 0.5, five of six models were already cheaper than flagging everyone (0.623); only 15-nearest-neighbours was not. And the recalibrated model's cost at $1/(1 + r)$ was 0.158 against 0.112 at the best threshold in hindsight, a gap of 0.046, though a threshold chosen in hindsight on 269 tumours flatters itself, for the same winner's-curse reason as above.

The advice replicated; the drama did not. A task where the models are 97% accurate leaves little room for any threshold to be catastrophic. The figure below shows each model's cost at both thresholds against the cost of flagging everyone.

### The best AUC and the cheapest model

Chapter 47 found, in simulation, that the model with the best AUC was never the cheapest when misses cost ten times more. On breast cancer, the AUC winner was boosted trees in 7 of 20 splits and the cheapest at $1/(1 + r)$ only 2 times; logistic regression, the AUC winner in 4, was cheapest in 11. The same model won both in 6 of 20 splits. Choosing by AUC would usually have chosen a model that was not the cheapest, as the simulation said, but not always.

The mechanism is the one Chapter 47 described. The AUC averages a model's ranking over every possible threshold, while a 10:1 cost ratio cares only about the region where malignant tumours might be cleared, and the model that ranks best overall need not rank best there. On a task this easy the top models' AUCs differ in the third decimal, so which one wins varies from split to split. Across 20 splits the cheapest was still most often logistic regression, which won on AUC only 4 times.

### The pattern

All four verdicts kept their direction, or part of it, and every effect was smaller or weaker on these data. Both bundled data sets are easy, and on easy data the failures the earlier chapters measured have less room to do damage. ## In practice

- **Trust a simulation for whether and why a method misbehaves.** All four verdicts carried over in direction, at least in part.
- **Do not quote a simulation's effect size as a forecast for your data.** Re-measure how much on data like yours; every size here shrank.
- **Expect effects to depend on difficulty and rarity.** Tuning optimism and threshold costs were small on an easy task; probability inflation was smaller at a 10% than at a 2% positive rate.
- **Still apply the safe remedies**: report probabilities from an unresampled or recalibrated model, choose thresholds from costs, and select models on the cost you will incur, not on AUC.
- **Write up a failed replication.** A direction that fails to hold on your data is the more important finding.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Two bundled data sets.** The digits and the breast-cancer data are clean, small and easy. Most real problems are harder, which on the evidence here would move the replications towards the simulations' larger effects, not away from them.
- **Four verdicts of many.** These were chosen because they could be re-run with the original chapters' own code. Verdicts about time, people and selection (Chapters 36, 37 and 39) need data with that structure, which the bundled sets lack.
- **Twenty splits of a small data set.** The splits share most of their rows, so the twenty results are not independent, and their spread understates how a new data set of the same kind could differ.
- **The same choices on both sides.** The replications reuse the original chapters' choices of models and settings; an independent replication would choose its own.

## Key points

1. Resampling for a rare class left the AUC unchanged on real digits and inflated the probabilities 1.6 to 1.9 times, against eleven in the simulation at a rarer prevalence.
2. Tuning 64 settings on 200 real tumours was 0.005 optimistic and bought nothing over the default; the simulation's optimism was 0.023.
3. A cost-derived threshold cut every model's cost on real data, but 0.5 was not the disaster the simulation found; the best-AUC model was the cheapest in 6 of 20 splits.
4. Directions replicated; sizes shrank on easy, less imbalanced data.
5. Trust a simulation for whether and why a method misbehaves. Re-measure how much on data like yours before quoting a number.
