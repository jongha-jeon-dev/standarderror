# Naive Bayes Counts Correlated Evidence Twice, and Only Its Probabilities Notice

Chapter 41 combined models by averaging their probabilities and found that the most confident member decides. Naive Bayes combines too, but inside a single model: it treats each feature as a separate, independent piece of evidence about the class and adds up what each one says. That is exactly right if the features are independent within each class, and wrong otherwise, and they almost never are. The sentence that keeps the method in every textbook is its defence: *naive Bayes works well even though its independence assumption is wrong.* A practitioner meets it whenever naive Bayes is used as a fast baseline, a text classifier or a component whose scores feed later decisions. If the defence covers less than it seems to, those scores can be wildly overconfident while the accuracy looks respectable.

This chapter tests the defence on a data set where the assumption is about as wrong as it gets. It first compares naive Bayes with logistic regression on accuracy and on the quality of its probabilities. It then copies every feature several times, which scales the model's evidence without changing its direction, and copies only one group of features, which changes the balance of the evidence. Finally it tries three repairs.

## How naive Bayes combines evidence

For a two-class problem, naive Bayes predicts with the *log-odds*, the logarithm of the ratio of the two classes' posterior probabilities. Under the assumption that the features $x_1, \dots, x_d$ are independent given the class, Bayes' rule gives

$$
\log\frac{P(y=1 \mid x)}{P(y=0 \mid x)} \;=\; \log\frac{P(y=1)}{P(y=0)} \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^{d} \log\frac{p(x_j \mid y=1)}{p(x_j \mid y=0)} .
$$

The first term is the prior; each term of the sum is that feature's *log-likelihood ratio*, its evidence for one class over the other. In *Gaussian* naive Bayes each class-conditional density $p(x_j \mid y)$ is a normal distribution with its own mean and variance, estimated separately for each feature. The predicted probability is the logistic function of the log-odds.

The formula adds each feature's evidence as if no other feature had already reported it. If two features measure the same thing, the same evidence enters twice.

## On correlated features, naive Bayes is accurate but overconfident

The data are the Wisconsin diagnostic breast-cancer set bundled with scikit-learn: 569 tumours and 30 features. Ten measurements of each tumour's nuclei are recorded three times each, as a mean, a standard error and a worst value, and three of the ten, radius, perimeter and area, measure the same thing, size, in different units. The comparison model is logistic regression on standardised features, which fits one weight per feature jointly and so accounts for overlap between them: if two columns carry the same information, the fit can split the weight between them.

The code scores both models by five repetitions of five-fold cross-validation and reports accuracy, log loss (the mean negative log probability given to the true class) and the share of errors made with confidence above 0.99.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

b = combine.nb_baseline()      # breast cancer, 5 x five-fold CV
for name in ("nb", "logistic"):
    r = b[name]
    print(f"{name:9s} accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}, log loss {r['nll']:.3f}, "
          f"errors made at > 0.99 confidence {r['confident_errors']:.0%}")
```

```text
nb        accuracy 0.940, log loss 0.611, errors made at > 0.99 confidence 67%
logistic  accuracy 0.981, log loss 0.073, errors made at > 0.99 confidence 9%
```

Naive Bayes is four points less accurate, 0.940 against 0.981, which is the kind of gap that keeps it in use as a fast baseline. Its log loss is 8 times larger, and that is a different kind of gap. Log loss punishes confident mistakes severely, and 67% of naive Bayes' mistakes were made with more than 99% confidence, against 9% of logistic regression's.

## Copying every feature scales the evidence

### Why the ranking survives

Domingos and Pazzani explained in 1997 why naive Bayes classifies well despite the assumption. Classification needs only the sign of the log-odds, and ranking needs only their order; dependence between features distorts the *size* of the log-odds far more than their sign.

The cleanest test of that explanation copies the features. With every one of the thirty features present $k$ times, every log-likelihood ratio in the sum appears $k$ times, so the sum is multiplied by $k$. The prior is added once, whatever $k$ is. The log-odds are therefore multiplied by $k$, apart from the prior term. The block below fits naive Bayes on the copied data for several $k$ and reports accuracy, the *AUC* (the probability that a randomly chosen malignant tumour is scored above a randomly chosen benign one) computed both from the log-odds and from the predicted probabilities, and log loss.

```python
for r in combine.nb_duplicates():   # every feature copied k times
    print(f"k = {r['k']:2d}: accuracy {r['acc']:.4f}, AUC log-odds "
          f"{r['auc_log_odds']:.4f}, AUC probs {r['auc']:.4f}, "
          f"log loss {r['nll']:.3f}")
```

```text
k =  1: accuracy 0.9395, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9876, log loss 0.611
k =  2: accuracy 0.9385, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9830, log loss 0.982
k =  3: accuracy 0.9385, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9807, log loss 1.191
k =  5: accuracy 0.9381, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9734, log loss 1.479
k = 10: accuracy 0.9385, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9549, log loss 1.789
```

Multiplying log-odds by a positive number does not change their order, and the AUC computed from the log-odds is 0.9876 at every $k$. It agrees to within 3 millionths, the difference being one near-tie resolved differently, by rounding or by the prior term that does not scale with $k$. Accuracy barely moves, from 0.9395 to 0.9385; the small change again comes from the prior term no longer keeping pace with the rest. On order the textbook defence holds exactly, and on sign up to the prior term.

### Why the probabilities do not

The probabilities are another matter. Scaling the log-odds by $k$ pushes every probability towards 0 or 1, and log loss climbs with every copy, to 1.789 with ten copies. At that point 80% of the predicted probabilities are exactly 0 or 1 in floating point: once the log-odds reach a few dozen, the probability rounds to exactly 1 in double precision, and sufficiently negative log-odds underflow to exactly 0.

That rounding shows up even in a ranking metric. The AUC computed from the probabilities falls to 0.955, because tumours that differ in log-odds all tie at a probability of 1.0 and can no longer be ordered. The figure below shows the flat accuracy and log-odds AUC beside the falling probability AUC and the climbing share of saturated probabilities.

![At k = 10 the AUC of the log-odds is still 0.9876; the AUC of the probabilities is 0.955, because 80% of them are exactly 0 or 1.](ml143-f0-copies.png){alt="Accuracy and the AUC of the log-odds are flat lines; the AUC of the probabilities drifts down; the dashed share of probabilities that are exactly 0 or 1 climbs from about 0.14 to 0.80."}

So the defence is right about what it claims and silent about the rest. The real breast-cancer features are not ten copies of each other, but the size features come close, and the model counts them as separate witnesses saying the same thing.

## Copying some features reweights the evidence

Copying every feature scales the evidence uniformly. Copying only one group, or *family*, of features changes the balance between families, and that does change the model's verdicts. The next block copies either the size family (the nine features derived from radius, perimeter and area) or the texture family (three features) $k$ times, leaving the rest as they are.

```python
for r in combine.nb_family():       # one family copied k times
    print(f"{r['family']:7s} x{r['k']:<2d}: accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}, "
          f"AUC of log-odds {r['auc_log_odds']:.4f}, "
          f"log loss {r['nll']:.3f}")
```

```text
size    x2 : accuracy 0.933, AUC of log-odds 0.9864, log loss 0.924
size    x5 : accuracy 0.922, AUC of log-odds 0.9803, log loss 1.931
size    x10: accuracy 0.914, AUC of log-odds 0.9746, log loss 2.639
texture x2 : accuracy 0.945, AUC of log-odds 0.9895, log loss 0.549
texture x5 : accuracy 0.950, AUC of log-odds 0.9905, log loss 0.461
texture x10: accuracy 0.936, AUC of log-odds 0.9854, log loss 0.771
```

The nine size features copied ten times push the model towards judging tumours by size alone: the AUC of its log-odds falls to 0.9746, so the ranking itself has changed, and accuracy falls to 0.914. The three texture features copied five times push it the other way, and accuracy *rises*, to 0.950, with lower log loss than the original model (0.461 against 0.611).

That second result is a reminder of what the real data already do. Naive Bayes on the original features is not a neutral model with an independence flaw; it is a model that weights each group of correlated features by how many columns the group happens to occupy. Size occupies nine columns and texture three: each statistic of size is counted three times, once per unit it is measured in, and each statistic of texture once. Upweighting texture helped at two and five copies, which is consistent with the original features overcounting size; at ten copies texture was overcounted in turn, and accuracy fell back to 0.936. The table below collects these runs with the repairs of the next section.

## Three repairs, and one that failed

Three repairs follow from the mechanism.

**Recalibrate the log-odds.** The first repair accepts the order and fixes the scale. A logistic regression is fitted with naive Bayes' log-odds as its single input, using out-of-fold log-odds inside each training set so that the recalibration is learned on predictions the model did not fit; this is Platt scaling (Chapter 34). The fitted map is increasing, so the ranking is untouched; accuracy stays at 0.940, while log loss falls from 0.611 to 0.149 and the share of errors made at above 0.99 confidence from 67% to 2%. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana found naive Bayes among the models that gain most from calibration.

**Remove the redundancy.** The second repair keeps only three weakly related features, mean radius, mean texture and mean smoothness. It gives log loss 0.199 with accuracy 0.911: better probabilities, fewer correct answers.

**Decorrelate first.** The third repair looks principled and failed. Decorrelating the features with *whitened principal components*, which rotates the data onto uncorrelated axes and rescales each axis to unit variance, gave accuracy 0.850. Two things may explain it, neither isolated here. Whitening removes the correlation across all tumours, not within each class, so it does not make the features independent given the class, which is the only independence naive Bayes assumes. And it inflates the smallest components, mostly noise, to the same variance as the informative ones.

None of the three beats logistic regression, which solves the double-counting problem by fitting the weights jointly. The case for naive Bayes is speed, tiny data and text, where it remains a strong baseline; the case against it is any use of its probabilities as probabilities.

This chapter and the two before it compared models by their scores on held-out data. Chapter 44 asks how such comparisons should be tested when the scores come from cross-validation folds.

## In practice

- **Do not read naive Bayes probabilities as probabilities.** Check log loss and the share of predictions above 0.99 or below 0.01, and count the errors among them.
- **Use it for what the defence covers.** Its sign and order survive correlated features; it is a reasonable fast baseline for classification and ranking.
- **Recalibrate before using its scores downstream.** Fit Platt scaling on out-of-fold log-odds; it repairs the scale without changing the ranking.
- **Look for families of redundant columns.** Compute within-class correlations and find groups that measure the same thing. Naive Bayes weights each group by its column count, so the feature set itself sets the weights.
- **Do not expect decorrelation to rescue it.** Whitening across all rows is not independence within classes.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One data set with heavy redundancy.** Breast cancer is close to the worst case for double-counting. With features that really are close to independent within class, naive Bayes' probabilities are much better, as the naive Bayes model on Chapter 34's simulated task, already close to calibrated, showed.
- **Gaussian naive Bayes only.** The multinomial and Bernoulli versions used for text double-count in the same way, through words that co-occur, and are calibrated no better.
- **Copies are extreme.** Exact duplicates make the scaling exact. Real correlated features double-count partially and unevenly, which is the reweighting case rather than the scaling one.
- **Five-fold cross-validation on 569 tumours.** Differences of a point of accuracy are within noise; the factor of eight in log loss and the share of confident errors are not.

## Key points

1. On breast cancer, naive Bayes was 0.041 less accurate than logistic regression and had 8 times its log loss; 67% of its errors came at above 0.99 confidence.
2. Copying every feature $k$ times multiplies the log-odds by $k$: the AUC of the log-odds was unchanged to four decimal places, and log loss rose to 1.789 at $k$ = 10.
3. Copying one family reweights the evidence and changes the answers: 0.914 with the size features copied ten times, 0.950 with texture copied five.
4. Recalibrating the log-odds cut log loss from 0.611 to 0.149 with accuracy unchanged.
5. Use naive Bayes for ranking and quick baselines; recalibrate its log-odds before reading them as probabilities, and remember that its weights are set by how many columns each kind of evidence occupies.
