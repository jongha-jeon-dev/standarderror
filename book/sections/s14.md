# Boosting Overfits Its Probabilities Long Before Its Accuracy

Gradient boosting builds a model one tree at a time, each new tree fitted to what the current model still gets wrong. Run that process long enough and the model will fit the training data perfectly, noise included. Hence the standard warning, repeated in tutorials and library documentation: *boosting overfits if you run it long enough*, so the number of rounds must be tuned or stopped early. A practitioner meets this sentence whenever a number of rounds is chosen, an early-stopping rule is configured, or a boosted model's validation curve is read to decide whether it has overfitted. Whether the sentence is true depends on what "fits" means. If the curve being watched is the wrong one, early stopping may never trigger, and the model shipped will be the one whose probabilities are worst.

The chapter first defines the two ways a classifier's output can be scored, by its decisions and by its probabilities, and runs one boosted model for 2,000 rounds while scoring it both ways. It then examines shrinkage, the usual remedy, and measures how the learning rate moves the best round and the best achievable score. Next it asks what an early-stopping rule picks depending on the metric it watches, and what the trade between the two candidates is. Finally it adds label noise, which makes the error rate turn up as well, though far less than the probabilities degrade.

## Two ways to score a classifier

### Gradient boosting in brief

*Gradient boosting* builds an additive model. For binary classification the model produces a score $F(x)$ on the log-odds scale, and the predicted probability of the positive class is $p(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-F(x)})$. Starting from a constant, each round $m$ fits a small regression tree $h_m$ to the *pseudo-residuals*, the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current scores, which for log loss are simply $y_i - p(x_i)$. The tree's prediction is added with a scaling factor $\nu$, the *learning rate* or *shrinkage*:

$$
F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu\, h_m(x).
$$

Each round moves the scores in the direction that most reduces the training loss. Nothing in the procedure stops it from continuing to reduce that loss once the training data are fitted; it can keep pushing the training scores further from zero.

### Decisions and probabilities

A classifier makes two kinds of output. One is a decision, the predicted class, here whether $p(x)$ is above or below 0.5; it is scored by the *error rate*, the share of rows on which the decision is wrong. The other is the probability itself, scored by *log loss*, the average negative log-likelihood of the true labels:

$$
\text{log loss} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\Big],
$$

measured in *nats* when the logarithm is natural. Log loss rewards confident correct predictions and charges heavily for confident wrong ones.

The experiment uses scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier` with depth-3 trees on the breast-cancer data of the previous chapters, split in half, stratified by class, ten times with different seeds. At the common learning rate of 0.1 the model runs for 2,000 rounds, and its staged predictions are scored both ways on the held-out tumours at eight checkpoints. The block prints the averages over the ten splits.

```python
from standarderror.ml import trees as tr

# depth-3 trees, learning rate 0.1, 10 half splits, clean labels
c = tr.boost_curves(lr=0.1, flip=0.0)
for r, e, ll in zip(c["rounds"], c["error"], c["log_loss"]):
    print(f"round {r:>5,}  test error {e:.4f}  test log loss {ll:.3f}")
```

```text
round     1  test error 0.3719  test log loss 0.582
round     3  test error 0.0691  test log loss 0.470
round    10  test error 0.0561  test log loss 0.275
round    30  test error 0.0565  test log loss 0.161
round   100  test error 0.0498  test log loss 0.190
round   300  test error 0.0474  test log loss 0.348
round 1,000  test error 0.0463  test log loss 0.416
round 2,000  test error 0.0463  test log loss 0.416
```

### The error never turns up; the log loss does

By the error rate there is nothing to stop. The test error falls to 0.046 and is still at its lowest after 2,000 rounds. Apart from a tick of 0.0004 between rounds 10 and 30, under a third of a tumour per split, it never rises at any checkpoint. By that measure, over 2,000 rounds, the warning does not hold for this model.

By log loss the model peaked long ago. Of the checkpoints, the log loss is lowest at 30 rounds, at 0.161. Computed round by round within each split, the best round has a median of 46 across the ten splits. By the end the log loss is 0.416, 2.6 times worse than its best.

### Why the two scores disagree

The two numbers disagree because they look at different things. The error counts only which side of 0.5 each prediction falls. Log loss charges for confidence: a tumour predicted at 0.999 benign that turns out malignant costs $-\log(1 - 0.999)$, almost seven nats, while the same mistake made with little confidence costs a small fraction of that.

As the rounds go on, the model presumably pushes its training probabilities towards 0 or 1; the training loss was not tracked here, but that is the usual way a training log loss keeps falling once the training labels are already fitted. On test tumours the model mostly keeps the same answers, so the error stands still. The few it gets wrong, it gets wrong more and more confidently, and the rising log loss records exactly that. ## Shrinkage slows overfitting without stopping it

### Where the bottoms of the curves fall

The usual remedy is *shrinkage*: a small learning rate $\nu$ scales each tree's contribution, so the model approaches the training data slowly, in many small steps rather than a few large ones. It works in the sense Friedman intended when he introduced it: the curves in the figure above are smoother at smaller rates and their bottoms wider. But look at where the bottoms are:

- learning rate 1: best log loss 0.203, after about 3.5 rounds;
- learning rate 0.1: best 0.161, after about 46;
- learning rate 0.01: best 0.162, after about 400.

Each tenfold cut in the learning rate moves the best round about tenfold later. To a first approximation that is what the update rule suggests: with $\nu$ ten times smaller, each tree moves the scores about a tenth as far, so it takes about ten times as many trees to travel the same distance.

### What each cut buys

The two cuts differ in what else they do. The first, from 1 to 0.1, lowered the best log loss from 0.203 to 0.161, plausibly because at a rate of 1 each tree takes a full step, and the curve is past its bottom within a handful of rounds; the steps are too coarse to stop near the best point. The second, from 0.1 to 0.01, did not lower it at all: 0.161 against 0.162. Below 0.1, a smaller learning rate buys a wider window in which stopping is safe, and it costs proportionally more trees. It does not make running forever safe. At 2,000 rounds the model with learning rate 0.01 is already past its best, at a log loss of 0.272.

That is why early stopping is part of every serious boosting library, and why what it monitors matters. A validation set scored by accuracy would, here, never trigger it, and the model shipped would be the one with the worst probabilities of any checkpoint after the first few dozen. The figure below shows the error curves, which tell the opposite story to the log loss.

## What a stopping rule picks depends on what it watches

### Two candidate models

*Early stopping* takes the round at which a validation metric is best and keeps the model at that round. Which metric it watches decides which model is shipped. On the clean runs at learning rate 0.1, watching the error rate picks the last checkpoint, 2,000 rounds, because the error is still at its lowest there. That model's test log loss is 0.416. Watching log loss picks a model near the 30-round checkpoint (round 46 in the median split), with test log loss 0.161 and a test error of 0.056, which is 0.010 worse than the long run.

So the trade is real but lopsided: about a point of accuracy against a 2.6-fold difference in the quality of the probabilities. If the output is a decision at a fixed threshold, the long run is the better model here, though a point on about 285 test tumours per split is close to the noise (Chapter 1 shows how wide an error bar such a difference carries). If the output is a probability, and most deployed classifiers are read as risks, ranked, or combined with costs, the short run is better by a wide margin, and only a stopping rule on log loss will find it.

### The margin explanation

Why does the error keep improving while confidence runs away? The usual answer is the *margin* explanation of Schapire and colleagues, developed for AdaBoost and not tested here. The margin of a training point is, roughly, how far on the correct side of the decision boundary the model's score places it. On this account, later rounds mostly push correctly classified training points further from the boundary, increasing their margins, which changes probabilities a great deal and decisions very little. The boundary itself barely moves near the test points that sit on it, so the error holds; while on test points the model gets wrong, the pushed-out probabilities make each mistake more expensive.

## With noisy labels, the error turns up as well

### Flipping a tenth of the training labels

Clean labels flatter the error rate: with nothing to memorise but the truth, fitting the training data harder cannot cost many decisions. The next run flips 10% of the training labels at random, leaving the test labels untouched, so boosting has real noise to chase. The block prints the checkpoints from round 30 on.

```python
c = tr.boost_curves(lr=0.1, flip=0.1)   # 10% of labels flipped
for r, e, ll in list(zip(c["rounds"], c["error"], c["log_loss"]))[3:]:
    print(f"round {r:>5,}  test error {e:.4f}  test log loss {ll:.3f}")
```

```text
round    30  test error 0.0709  test log loss 0.240
round   100  test error 0.0804  test log loss 0.211
round   300  test error 0.0856  test log loss 0.248
round 1,000  test error 0.0891  test log loss 0.531
round 2,000  test error 0.0881  test log loss 0.690
```

Now the error turns up as well, from 0.071 at its best to 0.088, a rise of 0.017, less than two points over nearly two thousand extra rounds. The log loss goes from 0.211 to 0.690, more than three times worse. The flipped training labels are fitted as faithfully as the true ones, because to the algorithm a flipped label is just another residual to reduce. Every test tumour that resembles a flipped training tumour then inherits a confident wrong answer.

### Accuracy and probability are separate questions

Mease and Wyner made the accuracy half of this point in 2008: boosted classifiers often keep improving in error long after the statistical view says they should be overfitting. The probability half is the one that matters whenever the output is used as a risk, such as a credit default probability, a diagnosis likelihood or a click-through rate, because there the model's confidence is the product. ## In practice

- **Stop on the metric the output will be judged by.** If the probabilities are used, as risks, rankings or inputs to a cost calculation, early-stop on validation log loss or another *proper scoring rule*, a score that is optimised in expectation by reporting the true probabilities (the Brier score is another). Do not stop on accuracy.
- **Plot both curves.** For any boosted model, plot staged validation error and staged validation log loss against the number of rounds. If they bottom out far apart, the choice of stopping metric decides which model you ship.
- **Treat the learning rate as a speed, not a safeguard.** A smaller rate moves the best round later in proportion and widens the window around it, but the model still passes its best. Going below 0.1 bought nothing in the best log loss here.
- **Expect label noise to make matters worse.** With noisy labels, long runs cost accuracy as well, and probabilities much more.
- **Check calibration before using a boosted model's probabilities**, and recalibrate on held-out data if needed; Chapter 34 measures how many rows recalibration requires.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One dataset.** Breast cancer is easy, with a test error under 5%. On harder data, where many test rows sit near the boundary, the error can be expected to turn up sooner and further, as it did here once labels were flipped. That the ordering, log loss first and error later, carries over to other data is a reasonable expectation, not something measured here.
- **Probabilities can be repaired afterwards.** Calibrating a booster on held-out data, by *Platt scaling* (fitting a logistic regression to its scores) or *isotonic regression* (fitting a monotone step function), improved its log loss substantially in Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana's comparison, though the boosting they studied distorted probabilities towards the middle rather than towards 0 and 1, and recalibration was not run here. It needs a held-out set, and it repairs the symptom, not the training.
- **Checkpoints, not every round.** The curves are sampled at eight round counts, and the best log loss quoted is the lowest of those checkpoints on the curve averaged over splits. The best round is computed from every round, per split, and its median is reported. The two need not coincide: at a learning rate of 0.1 the best checkpoint is 30 rounds and the median best round 46.
- **Other boosters regularise more by default.** Libraries with leaf-weight penalties, row subsampling and minimum leaf sizes would be expected to reach the same place more slowly; none was run here. scikit-learn's classic implementation, with its defaults, is the plainest version of the algorithm.

## Key points

1. With clean labels, the test error of gradient boosting never turned up from round 30 to round 2,000, ending at 0.046.
2. Test log loss was lowest within the first few dozen rounds and 2.6 times worse at 2,000. The model's decisions held; its confidence ran away.
3. Each tenfold cut in the learning rate moved the best round about tenfold later. Cutting it from 1 to 0.1 lowered the best log loss; cutting it from 0.1 to 0.01 left it where it was. Shrinkage widens the window; it does not remove the need to stop.
4. With 10% label noise the error rose by under two points and the log loss more than tripled.
5. Stop boosting on the metric the output will be judged by. If the probabilities are used, that is log loss or another proper score, not accuracy.

## Further reading

- Friedman, "Greedy function approximation: a gradient boosting machine", *Annals of Statistics* (2001): the gradient boosting algorithm and the case for shrinkage.
- Schapire, Freund, Bartlett and Lee, "Boosting the margin", *Annals of Statistics* (1998): the margin explanation of why boosting's error keeps falling.
- Mease and Wyner, "Evidence contrary to the statistical view of boosting", *Journal of Machine Learning Research* (2008): boosted classifiers that keep improving in error long after they should overfit.
- Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, "Predicting good probabilities with supervised learning", *ICML* (2005): how well different learners' probabilities are calibrated, and recalibration by Platt scaling and isotonic regression.
