# A Test Set Answers Honestly Only the First Time

Chapter 1 put an error bar on a test score. Chapter 35 found that the best of many cross-validated scores reports the luck of the search as well as the quality of the model. Chapter 44 asked which tests can tell two models apart. Each assumed that the test set was used once. In practice it rarely is: a model is scored, adjusted and scored again, and a competition's public leaderboard is consulted thousands of times. The reassurance that makes this feel safe is the sentence under test: *a held-out test set gives an unbiased estimate, however often you look at it, as long as you never train on it.* If it is false, the score a team reports at the end of a project can be well above what the model will do on new data, even though no test row ever entered a training set.

This chapter wears out a holdout in three ways, none of which trains on it. The first is a deliberate attack on a leaderboard of coin-flip labels. The second is an analysis that looks careful: keeping only features that replicate on the holdout. The third is the most ordinary of all, an analyst choosing features by their holdout score. The chapter then states the common mechanism, adaptivity, and the two kinds of remedy.

## A leaderboard can be climbed without any signal

### The attack

The cleanest refutation of the sentence is Blum and Hardt's, from 2015. A competition leaderboard holds 1,000 *public* labels, whose score is shown after each submission, and 1,000 *private* labels, used only for the final ranking. Every label is a coin flip, so no submission can genuinely score above 0.5.

The attacker submits random guesses of 0 or 1 for every row, notes which guesses scored above 0.5 on the public labels, and finally submits the *majority vote* of those guesses: for each row, the label that most of the kept guesses assigned to it. The code runs the attack on 50 simulated leaderboards for increasing numbers of submissions and prints the vote's average public and private scores.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

for r in combine.leaderboard_attack():   # 50 leaderboards
    print(f"{r['queries']:5d} submissions: public {r['public']:.3f}, "
          f"private {r['private']:.3f}")
```

```text
    1 submissions: public 0.503, private 0.499
   10 submissions: public 0.524, private 0.500
  100 submissions: public 0.568, private 0.500
  300 submissions: public 0.624, private 0.499
 1000 submissions: public 0.713, private 0.497
 3000 submissions: public 0.839, private 0.500
```

After 1,000 submissions the vote scores 0.713 on the public labels and 0.497 on the private ones. Nothing was trained on anything.

### Why the vote climbs

Each submission asked the public labels one question, "is this guess better than chance?", and each answer leaked a little about them. A random guess that happens to score above 0.5 on the public labels agrees with them on slightly more than half the rows, so on each row it is slightly more likely than chance to carry the right public label. One such guess carries almost no information, but the majority vote of many of them adds these small tilts row by row, and the vote's agreement with the public labels grows as more guesses are collected. On the private labels the guesses were never selected, so their tilts point nowhere and the vote stays at chance. The figure below shows the two scores diverging.

![After 1,000 submissions the vote scored 0.713 on the public labels and 0.497 on the private ones.](ml145-f0-leaderboard.png){alt="The public score rises from 0.50 to 0.84 as submissions grow from 1 to 3,000; the private score stays at 0.50."}

## A careful-looking analysis leaks the same way

Nobody attacks their own holdout on purpose. Dwork and colleagues showed in 2015 that an ordinary-looking analysis does the same by accident. Their example, scaled down here, has 2,000 features of pure noise and coin-flip labels, with 1,000 rows each for training, holdout and fresh data. The analysis keeps the features whose correlation with the label is noticeably positive or negative on the training set *and* has the same sign on the holdout. That sounds like a careful way of keeping only features that replicate. It then classifies by a vote of the strongest kept features.

Asked for 100 such features (about 99 pass the filter on average), the classifier scores 0.687 on the holdout. On fresh data it scores 0.498, chance.

The holdout was never trained on. It was used to decide which features to keep, and the features it kept are the ones whose noise happens to agree with the holdout's particular noise. The holdout score is now a measurement of how well the model fits the holdout, which is exactly what the features were chosen for. The table below shows the holdout score rising as more selected features vote, while the fresh score stays at chance.

![The holdout was never trained on. It was asked which features to keep, and its answer is now part of the model.](ml145-f2-noise.png){alt="A four-row table: holdout accuracy rises from 0.56 to 0.69 as more selected features vote; fresh accuracy stays at 0.50."}

## An analyst who selects by holdout score

### Two analysts, one data set

The third case is the most ordinary. The data set has 100 standard-normal features, ten of them weakly informative, with 300 rows to train on and 300 held out; a further 20,000 fresh rows measure the truth. The analyst builds a logistic regression by *forward selection*: starting from no features, add at each step the one feature that most improves the model's score, and repeat. This analyst measures improvement by accuracy on the holdout, a natural thing to do with a holdout one has been told only not to train on. A second analyst runs the same procedure but chooses each step by five-fold cross-validation on the training rows, and leaves the holdout alone until the end.

The code prints, after 1, 5, 10 and 20 features, each analyst's holdout accuracy and fresh accuracy, and the share of the 20 chosen features that are real.

```python
h = combine.greedy_holdout(by="holdout")
c = combine.greedy_holdout(by="cv")
print("features | holdout-guided: holdout / fresh | CV-guided: same")
for step in (0, 4, 9, 19):
    print(f"{step + 1:8d} | {h['holdout'][step]:.3f} / "
          f"{h['fresh'][step]:.3f}{'':18s} | {c['holdout'][step]:.3f}"
          f" / {c['fresh'][step]:.3f}")
print(f"real features among 20 chosen: holdout-guided "
      f"{h['real_share'][19]:.0%}, CV-guided {c['real_share'][19]:.0%}")
```

```text
features | holdout-guided: holdout / fresh | CV-guided: same
       1 | 0.588 / 0.550                   | 0.525 / 0.543
       5 | 0.663 / 0.579                   | 0.564 / 0.561
      10 | 0.677 / 0.578                   | 0.560 / 0.563
      20 | 0.690 / 0.576                   | 0.579 / 0.580
real features among 20 chosen: holdout-guided 14%, CV-guided 16%
```

### The holdout-guided score beats the true model

The first analyst's holdout score rises at almost every step, to 0.690 with 20 features. That is above 0.676, the fresh-data accuracy of a model given exactly the ten real features, which is the best this procedure could honestly reach. On fresh rows the same models peaked at 0.579 after 5 features and ended at 0.576, and only 14% of the 20 chosen features were real. The holdout overstated the final model by 0.114. Each step chose among a hundred candidates by their holdout score, so each step was a small search over the holdout's noise, and the reported score is the search's best result rather than a fresh measurement.

### The cross-validation-guided score stays honest

The second analyst's holdout, consulted only to report the result, said 0.579 for a model that scored 0.580 on fresh rows, within 0.001. Cross-validation inside the training rows is subject to the luck of the search that Chapter 35 measured, and this selection picked noise too: only 16% of its 20 features were real. But its luck was spent on the training rows, not on the set used to report the score, so the report stayed honest about the model the analyst got. The figure below shows both analysts' curves.

![After 20 holdout-guided steps the holdout said 0.690; fresh rows said 0.576, and a model on the 10 real features scores 0.676.](ml145-f1-greedy.png){alt="The holdout-guided holdout score climbs steadily to about 0.69, above a dotted line at 0.676; its fresh-row accuracy levels off near 0.58. ## A test set's honesty is a budget spent by adaptivity

The common thread is that a test set's honesty is a budget, and it is spent by *adaptivity*: any decision made after looking at a test score, whether to keep a feature, a model, a hyperparameter or a submission, makes later scores on the same set depend on its particular noise. A score is unbiased only for a model fixed before the score was seen. Training on the test set spends the budget at once. Looking at it spends a little per look, and the amount depends on how much each look changes what happens next.

Two kinds of remedy follow.

**The procedural remedy** is the oldest: keep a final test set that is used once, after all decisions are made, and accept that its score, with the error bar of Chapter 1, is the report. Everything consulted before it, including any "validation" or "public" set, is part of training in the sense that matters.

**The algorithmic remedy**, from the same authors, limits what each look reveals. Blum and Hardt's *Ladder* reports a new leaderboard score only when a submission beats the previous best by a margin, so most submissions learn nothing new. Dwork and colleagues' *Thresholdout* answers each query with the training score unless the holdout score disagrees with it by more than a noisy threshold, so the holdout is consulted only when it has something to correct. Both slow the leak, at the price of less precise answers.

Benchmarks raise the same question at the scale of a field. Recht and colleagues rebuilt ImageNet's test set following the original procedure and found that published models lost 11 to 14 points of accuracy on it while keeping their order. They attributed the loss mainly to the new images being harder, rather than to years of adaptive reuse of the old test set. With large test sets and many genuinely different models, the leak can be slow; the three cases in this chapter show where it is fast.

## In practice

- **Keep one test set for one final report.** Decide everything first, score once, and report that score with its error bar.
- **Treat every set that informed a decision as training data.** A validation set, a public leaderboard or a holdout used to choose features, models, hyperparameters, thresholds or early-stopping points no longer gives an unbiased score.
- **Make selection decisions inside the training rows,** with cross-validation, so that the search's luck is not spent on the set used for reporting.
- **Count the looks.** Record each decision made after seeing a score on a held-out set. If the count is large and the set is small, expect optimism.
- **If a set must be consulted repeatedly,** limit what each look reveals, for instance by reporting only improvements beyond a margin, as the Ladder does.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Adversarial by construction.** The leaderboard attack and the noise selection are the worst cases, with nothing real to find. With strong real signal, the honest part of each score dominates and the adaptive part matters less.
- **Small holdouts.** The holdouts here have 300 to 1,000 rows. In Blum and Hardt's analysis the gain from such attacks shrinks roughly as one over the square root of the holdout's size, so large test sets tolerate more looks; they do not tolerate unlimited ones.
- **One analyst, one strategy.** Real model development mixes holdout-guided decisions with others, and counting how many decisions a test set has informed is rarely possible after the fact, which is the practical argument for a set used once.
- **Greedy selection is crude.** A more careful analyst would regularise and stop earlier; the mechanism, deciding by a score and then reporting that score, is the same.

## Key points

1. Coin-flip labels, never trained on: the majority vote of random guesses kept for beating 0.5 scored 0.713 on the public labels after 1,000 submissions and 0.497 on the private ones.
2. Features kept because they replicated on the holdout gave 0.687 there and 0.498 on fresh data.
3. Forward selection guided by the holdout reported 0.690 after 20 steps, above the true model's 0.676; fresh accuracy was 0.576. Guided by cross-validation instead, the holdout matched fresh rows to within 0.001.
4. A test set's honesty is spent by adaptivity: every decision made after seeing its score makes later scores on it depend on its noise, and tends to make them optimistic.
5. Treat every test score that informed a decision as part of training. Keep one test set for one final report, and count the decisions made with anything else.
