# Covariate Shift Charges for Misspecification

Chapter 28 ended with a network scored on digits moved off centre, inputs of a kind it had not been shown. That is the normal condition of a deployed model: the population it scores drifts away from the one it was trained on. The simplest version of this drift has a textbook name, *covariate shift*: the distribution of the inputs $x$ changes between training and deployment, while the conditional distribution of the outcome given the inputs, $P(y \mid x)$, does not. The sentence usually attached to it is the one under test: *covariate shift costs accuracy*. Practitioners meet it whenever a monitoring dashboard flags that input distributions have moved and the response is to retrain, reweight or simply expect worse performance. If the sentence is wrong about where the cost comes from, the remedy will be aimed at the wrong thing.

The sentence sounds obvious, but the setting makes it odd. If $P(y \mid x)$ is unchanged, the best possible classifier is unchanged too, and a model that had learned it would carry on being right. Whatever the shift costs, then, is not the shift's doing alone. This chapter measures the cost in a simulation where everything is visible, shows that it falls on a misspecified model and barely on a flexible one, examines importance weighting, the standard remedy, and measures the price the weights exact in effective sample size.

## A simulation in which only the inputs move

The *Bayes rule* is the classifier that, for each $x$, predicts the class with the higher true probability $P(y \mid x)$; its accuracy, the *Bayes accuracy*, is the best any classifier can achieve on a given input distribution. Under covariate shift the rule itself is the same in training and test: it labels each $x$ identically. Its accuracy can still change a little, because the test inputs may fall more or less often in regions where the outcome is uncertain.

In the simulation the input is a single number, drawn from $N(0, 1)$ in training and from $N(s, 1)$ in test, for shifts $s$ from 0 to 2 standard deviations. The label is 1 with probability

$$
P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma\big(3 \sin(1.5x)\big),
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. This is the same wavy curve everywhere, so the shift moves only the inputs. Two models are fitted to 500 training rows, twenty times over, and scored on 20,000 test rows per shift. The first is an ordinary linear logistic regression, which cannot follow the wave. The second is a logistic regression on cubic spline features, which can. *Spline features* represent $x$ by a set of piecewise-cubic basis functions joined smoothly at fixed points called knots, here 8 of them, so that a linear model on these features can trace a smooth curve; beyond the outermost knots the basis continues linearly. A model is *misspecified* when no setting of its parameters reproduces the true $P(y \mid x)$; the linear model is misspecified here and the spline model, over the range of the data, nearly is not.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

rows = nets.covariate_shift()        # 500 training rows, 20 repetitions
for r in rows:
    print(f"shift {r['shift']:.1f}  Bayes {r['bayes']:.3f}  "
          f"flexible {r['acc_flex']:.3f}  linear {r['acc_linear']:.3f}")
```

```text
shift 0.0  Bayes 0.838  flexible 0.834  linear 0.816
shift 0.5  Bayes 0.835  flexible 0.832  linear 0.802
shift 1.0  Bayes 0.839  flexible 0.831  linear 0.758
shift 1.5  Bayes 0.839  flexible 0.821  linear 0.666
shift 2.0  Bayes 0.832  flexible 0.805  linear 0.536
```

The Bayes accuracy stays between 0.832 and 0.839 at every shift: the task does not get harder. The flexible model stays within 0.018 of it up to a shift of 1.5. The linear model loses more at every step and ends at 0.536, not far above a coin. Covariate shift, in this setting, costs one model a great deal and the other very little.

## Why the line breaks and the spline does not

The figure above shows what the linear model did. Most training inputs lie between −1 and 1, where the wave climbs from its trough to its peak, so the best line through them rises. In the test region around $x = 2$ the wave has turned over and is falling. The line, still rising, predicts class 1 where class 0 is now the more likely label.

Nothing about the line was wrong *for the training distribution*. Maximum likelihood finds the line that best approximates the true curve, weighting each region of $x$ by how often training inputs fall there. It was the best linear approximation to the wave over $N(0, 1)$. The best linear approximation therefore depends on where the inputs are: when the inputs move, the best line moves, and the fitted one does not. That is the whole mechanism. Covariate shift hurts a model by exposing the places where it was approximating, and a misspecified model approximates everywhere.

The spline model follows the wave wherever it has data, so its errors in the test region are mostly the irreducible ones that the Bayes rule makes too. It does lose something at the largest shift, 0.027 of accuracy at $s = 2$. That is plausibly because the right tail of the test distribution reaches $x = 4$ and beyond, where the training data have almost no points and the spline extrapolates linearly; the mechanism was not isolated. Shift far enough and every model is extrapolating. The difference between the two models is how early the cost starts, and for the line it starts at the first step.

Calibration says the same thing more loudly. The *expected calibration error* (ECE) groups predictions into bins of predicted probability and averages, weighted by bin size, the gap between each bin's mean predicted probability and the observed frequency of the positive class. The linear model's ECE at $s = 2$ is 0.44: its probabilities are confidently pointed the wrong way. The spline's is 0.08. ## Importance weighting aims the model at the test inputs

The standard remedy is *importance weighting*: weight each training row by how much more likely its input is under the test distribution than under the training one,

$$
w(x) = \frac{p_{\text{test}}(x)}{p_{\text{train}}(x)},
$$

and fit by minimising the weighted training loss $\sum_i w(x_i)\,\ell(x_i, y_i)$. Because $P(y \mid x)$ is the same in both populations, the weighted average of the training loss is an unbiased estimate of the expected loss on the test population. The fitted model therefore aims at the best model *for the test inputs* rather than for the training inputs. For two normal distributions with unit variance the ratio has a closed form, $w(x) = \exp(sx - s^2/2)$, which grows exponentially in $x$: rows in the right tail of the training data get very large weights. Here the ratio is known exactly; in practice it has to be estimated, which is a second source of error this simulation leaves out.

The next block prints, for the linear model, accuracy and *log loss* (the average negative log probability assigned to the true label) without and with weights, and the effective sample size of the weights, defined in the next section.

```python
for r in rows:
    print(f"shift {r['shift']:.1f}  accuracy {r['acc_linear']:.3f} -> "
          f"{r['acc_linear_iw']:.3f}  log loss {r['nll_linear']:.3f} -> "
          f"{r['nll_linear_iw']:.3f}  effective n {r['ess']:.0%}")
```

```text
shift 0.0  accuracy 0.816 -> 0.816  log loss 0.550 -> 0.550  effective n 100%
shift 0.5  accuracy 0.802 -> 0.795  log loss 0.607 -> 0.600  effective n 78%
shift 1.0  accuracy 0.758 -> 0.680  log loss 0.800 -> 0.658  effective n 39%
shift 1.5  accuracy 0.666 -> 0.735  log loss 1.178 -> 0.635  effective n 15%
shift 2.0  accuracy 0.536 -> 0.777  log loss 1.737 -> 0.583  effective n 6%
```

The weighted log loss is lower than the unweighted at every non-zero shift; at zero the weights are all 1 and change nothing. To see what the weights aim at, the weighted model is compared with a linear model fitted to 200,000 rows drawn from the test distribution itself, the best linear model for those inputs. The weighted model comes within 0.048 of its accuracy and 0.035 of its log loss, closer at small shifts than at large ones. At $s = 2$ the weights turn the line around, so that it fits the falling stretch of the wave, and accuracy goes from 0.536 to 0.777.

At $s = 1$ accuracy goes the other way, from 0.758 to 0.680. This is not the weights failing. The best linear model for $N(1, 1)$ inputs, judged by log loss, is a nearly flat line (it scores 0.665 itself), because over that range the wave rises and falls about equally. Log loss rewards a line that hedges near 0.5; accuracy rewards one that commits to a side. Importance weighting optimises the loss it is given, and when the model is misspecified, the loss-optimal and the accuracy-optimal models are different models. With a correctly specified model the two would coincide at the true probabilities, and the distinction would not arise. The table below collects the comparison.

## The weights shrink the sample

Weights are not free. A weighted sample of 500 rows carries less information than 500 equally weighted rows, and Kong's *effective sample size* measures how much less:

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}.
$$

It equals $n$ when all weights are equal and falls towards 1 as a single weight dominates. At a shift of 1 it is 39% of the sample. At 1.5 it is 15%, and at 2 it is 6%: a few rows in the training set's right tail carry most of the weight, and the fit is effectively made from about 32 of them.

That is why the weighted linear model's accuracy varies more from one training set to the next. Its standard deviation over the twenty repetitions is 0.069 at $s = 2$, against 0.001 unweighted. The weights buy a smaller bias at the price of a larger variance, and the effective sample paying for it shrinks at every step of the shift. When the test inputs lie where the training data have none, there is nothing to reweight.

For the flexible model the weights make little difference in the region it can already follow, and a small one at $s = 2$: accuracy 0.805 unweighted and 0.816 weighted, with the spread across repetitions falling from 0.063 to 0.015. That is the extrapolating tail again. Weighting puts more of the fit's attention on the few rows near the test region, which plausibly steadies what the splines do just beyond them; the mechanism was not isolated here.

This chapter found that a model fitted over one range of inputs is charged for its form when used over another. Chapter 30 meets the same problem with a curve instead of a classifier: a power law fitted to small models and used to predict much larger ones.

## In practice

- **Separate the shift from the model.** When inputs drift, ask whether $P(y \mid x)$ has plausibly stayed fixed; if so, the cost of the drift is the model's misspecification, and the first remedy to try is a more flexible model.
- **Compare a flexible model on the shifted rows before reweighting.** If its accuracy holds where the current model's falls, the current model was approximating, and that, not the shift, is the problem.
- **Choose the metric before reweighting.** Importance weighting optimises the loss it is given. With a misspecified model it can lower log loss while lowering accuracy; report both.
- **Always compute the effective sample size of the weights.** If it is a few percent of the sample, the fit rests on a handful of rows, its variance is large, and the test population sits where the training data are thin.
- **Check for drift in $P(y \mid x)$ with labelled data from the new population.** No reweighting can correct a change in the rule itself.

## Limits of the evidence

- **The weights were exact.** Both densities were known. In practice the ratio is estimated, with a classifier trained to tell training inputs from test inputs or with a density-ratio method, and estimated weights add their own variance, worst exactly where the true weights are largest.
- **Only the inputs moved.** If $P(y \mid x)$ changes as well, the setting is *concept shift* and none of this applies: the Bayes rule itself moves, and no reweighting of old labels can learn the new one. In real drift the two usually come together, and telling them apart needs labelled data from the new population.
- **One dimension.** In one dimension the flexible model sees the whole range it will be tested on, up to the tail. In many dimensions a shift can move the inputs to combinations no training row came near, and every model extrapolates there. The lesson that misspecification is what the shift charges for still holds; a flexible model just runs out of support sooner.
- **A periodic rule.** The wave was chosen so that a line would fail. Where the true rule is close to linear over both ranges, a linear model loses little to the shift and weighting has nothing to correct.

## Key points

1. Under covariate shift the best possible accuracy need not change. Here the Bayes accuracy stayed between 0.832 and 0.839 while the inputs moved two standard deviations.
2. What the shift costs is the model's misspecification, exposed. A flexible model stayed within 0.018 of Bayes up to a shift of 1.5; a linear one fell from 0.816 to 0.536.
3. Importance weighting refits the model for the test inputs. It lowered log loss at every non-zero shift and moved towards the best linear model for the test distribution (within 0.048 of its accuracy), which at a shift of 1 was a worse classifier (0.680 against 0.758).
4. The weights shrink the sample: at a shift of 2 the effective sample was 6% of 500 rows.
5. Before reweighting, check whether a more flexible model makes the shift harmless, and check the effective sample size of the weights before trusting what they produce.
