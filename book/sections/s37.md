# Rows From the Same People Are Not New Data

Chapter 36 found that rows close in time are a problem for cross-validation only when they share something the model cannot see. People are the other common source of rows that share something: a patient measured at every visit, a customer with a year of transactions, a speaker with a hundred recordings, a writer with a page of digits. Chapter 3 measured exact duplicate rows straddling folds and noted that near-duplicates, such as the same patient twice, leak by the same mechanism with less force. This chapter measures that case, and the sentence that makes it easy to miss: *more rows means more data, and cross-validation over the rows measures how the model will do.* If the sentence is false, a team that collects many rows from a few people will overestimate its model, choose the data set that generalises worst, and believe it has far more evidence than it does.

The chapter proceeds in three steps. It first compares row-level and subject-level cross-validation with accuracy on new people, as the same 2,000 rows are drawn from fewer and fewer people. It then explains what each kind of fold measures. Finally it asks what extra rows from the same people are worth, and connects the answer to the design effect from survey sampling.

## The same number of rows from fewer people

The simulation gives each simulated person, or *subject*, two things that belong to them alone. One is a *signature*: a random offset, normal with standard deviation 2, added to all ten of their features, so that their rows sit together in feature space. The other is a *propensity*: a random offset, also with standard deviation 2, added to the log-odds of all their outcomes, so that some people are simply positive more often than others. Neither tells the model anything about a new person. What does carry over is a relationship between two features and the outcome that is the same for everyone: the shared part of the log-odds is $1.5x_1 + x_2$.

Each design has 2,000 rows; what changes is how many people they come from, from 2,000 people with one row each down to 20 people with 100 rows each. Two kinds of five-fold cross-validation are compared:

- **Row-level cross-validation** shuffles the rows and deals them into folds, ignoring who they came from. A person's rows are spread across the folds.
- **Subject-level cross-validation** keeps all of a person's rows in a single fold, so each held-out row belongs to someone the model never saw in training. In scikit-learn this is `GroupKFold`.

The target both are trying to estimate is accuracy on new people: the model is fitted on all 2,000 rows and scored on 2,000 new subjects with five rows each. Every figure is an average over six simulated data sets per design. The first block prints the results for five-nearest-neighbours (5-NN).

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

for r in rows.subject_cv():   # 2,000 rows each time
    if r["model"] == "5-NN":
        print(f"{r['subjects']:5d} subjects x {r['per']:3d} rows: "
              f"row CV {r['row']:.3f}, subject CV {r['subject']:.3f}, "
              f"new people {r['new']:.3f}")
```

```text
 2000 subjects x   1 rows: row CV 0.737, subject CV 0.741, new people 0.741
  400 subjects x   5 rows: row CV 0.782, subject CV 0.747, new people 0.735
  100 subjects x  20 rows: row CV 0.827, subject CV 0.719, new people 0.718
   40 subjects x  50 rows: row CV 0.818, subject CV 0.674, new people 0.704
   20 subjects x 100 rows: row CV 0.825, subject CV 0.662, new people 0.685
```

With one row from each of 2,000 people, the two kinds of fold are the same thing, and row-level cross-validation agrees with the truth: 0.737 against 0.741. As the same 2,000 rows come from fewer people, the estimate and the truth move in opposite directions. Row-level cross-validation rises, to above 0.81 from 100 people down and to 0.825 with 20, while accuracy on new people falls at every step, to 0.685.

The direction matters as much as the size. A practitioner choosing between data sets by their row-level scores would pick the ones built from 100 people or fewer, which look like the best of the five, and the one from 20 people scores the forest highest of all. Row-level folds reward exactly the data sets that generalise worst. The figure below shows the opposing trends for all three models.

![From 20 subjects, row-level cross-validation scored five-nearest-neighbours at 0.825; on new people it scored 0.685.](ml137-f0-cv.png){alt="Dashed lines for row-level cross-validation climb as the number of subjects falls from 2,000 to 20; solid lines for accuracy on new subjects fall; dotted subject-level lines follow the solid ones."}

## What each kind of fold measures

### Row-level folds reward recognising people

A row-level fold holds out one fifth of each person's rows and trains on the other four fifths. Consider a held-out row from one of 20 people. Because the signature shifts all of that person's features together, the row's nearest neighbours in the training set are almost all rows from the same person: 96.6% of the five nearest training rows, in this simulation. Those rows also carry the same propensity, so their labels lean the same way as the held-out row's label. Five-nearest-neighbours therefore answers by asking the person's other rows what usually happens to this person. That is an excellent way to predict a person the model has already met, and no way at all to predict a new one, whose signature places them in an unfamiliar region and whose propensity is unknown.

The forest finds the same shortcut through its splits: 0.847 under row-level folds, against 0.770 on new people. A tree can isolate the region of feature space a person's signature occupies and assign it that person's usual outcome. Logistic regression, which can only draw one plane through the feature space, has less room to memorise people, and its gap is smaller but not zero: 0.824 against 0.783. A plausible reason, not isolated here, is that with 20 people a few of them can by chance have signatures that line up with their propensities, and a linear model fits that accident as though it were part of the shared relationship.

### Subject-level folds measure new people

The repair is to make the folds match the question. Subject-level cross-validation scored the nearest-neighbour model at 0.662 and the forest at 0.751 with 20 subjects, slightly below the truth. The pessimism is likely due in part to training size: each fold trains on 16 people instead of the 20 the final model sees, and with so few people, losing four is a noticeable loss of information about what generalises. It is a feature of very few groups. With 400 subjects the same folds were a little optimistic, by up to 0.012. The table below collects all fifteen combinations of design and model.

## What more rows from the same people buy

The second half of the sentence, that more rows mean more data, can be measured directly by growing the training set in two ways: one row from each of N new people, or N/20 rows from each of the same 20 people. Both training sets are scored on new people.

```python
for r in rows.subject_growth():
    print(f"{r['rows']:5d} rows, {r['how']:12s}: logistic "
          f"{r['logistic']:.3f}, forest {r['forest']:.3f}")
```

```text
  100 rows, new subjects: logistic 0.785, forest 0.752
  100 rows, 20 subjects : logistic 0.772, forest 0.731
  400 rows, new subjects: logistic 0.804, forest 0.783
  400 rows, 20 subjects : logistic 0.782, forest 0.751
 1600 rows, new subjects: logistic 0.809, forest 0.796
 1600 rows, 20 subjects : logistic 0.787, forest 0.766
 6400 rows, new subjects: logistic 0.810, forest 0.798
 6400 rows, 20 subjects : logistic 0.788, forest 0.777
```

One row per person keeps improving: logistic regression goes from 0.785 at 100 rows to 0.810 at 6,400. Twenty people with more and more rows each barely move, from 0.772 to 0.788. That final figure, from 6,400 rows, is about what 100 rows from 100 people gave (0.785), and below what 400 rows from 400 people gave (0.804). The forest behaves the same way: 6,400 rows from 20 people reached 0.777, again below 400 rows from 400 people (0.783). The figure below plots both ways of growing the data.

![With 6,400 rows from 20 subjects, logistic regression reached 0.788; with 400 rows from 400 subjects it reached 0.804.](ml137-f1-growth.png){alt="Solid lines rise with the number of rows and level off near 0.81 for logistic regression and 0.80 for the forest; dashed lines stay below them at every size."}

### The design effect

Survey statisticians have a name for this: the *design effect* of cluster sampling. Suppose $n$ rows come in clusters of $m$ rows each, and the *intra-cluster correlation* $\rho$ is the correlation between two rows from the same cluster, equivalently the share of the outcome's variance that lies between clusters rather than within them. Kish's formula gives the *effective sample size*, the number of independent rows that would estimate a mean with the same precision:

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{1 + (m - 1)\rho}.
$$

The formula is about survey means, not fitted models, but it describes the pattern measured here. When $\rho = 0$, every row counts fully. When $\rho$ is large and $m$ is large, the denominator grows with $m$, and adding rows to existing clusters barely increases $n_{\text{eff}}$. Here the differences between people are large compared with what varies within a person, so extra rows from the same person add almost nothing about what is true of people in general. They add a great deal about that person, and that is precisely what row-level folds reward.

## Which question is being asked

Row-level cross-validation is not wrong in itself; it answers a different question. If the model will only ever be used on the people it was trained on, such as predicting a known customer's next purchase or a known patient's next reading, then held-out rows from the same person are exactly the relevant test, and subject-level folds would be too pessimistic. Saeb and colleagues made this point for clinical models built from smartphone data: the right split is the one that imitates the use. The mistake is to answer one question and report the answer as though it were the other.

The same logic covers any grouping the deployment will not share: hospitals when a model moves to a new hospital, schools, devices, plots of land in ecology, and the writers of handwritten digits. Roberts and colleagues reviewed it for temporal, spatial and phylogenetic groupings and reached the same rule: block the folds by whatever the new data will be new in. The next chapter turns from who the rows came from to which values in them are missing, and why.

## In practice

- **Name the unit of deployment before choosing folds.** Ask what the new data will be new in (people, accounts, devices, sites, hospitals) and group the folds by that identifier, for example with `GroupKFold`.
- **Run both kinds of cross-validation and report the gap.** The difference between row-level and grouped scores is the part of the model's apparent skill that comes from recognising sources it has already seen.
- **Count sources, not rows.** Compute the intra-cluster correlation of the target within each source and apply Kish's formula. Quote the effective sample size alongside the row count.
- **Do not choose between data sets by row-level scores.** In this chapter, row-level scores rose as the data came from fewer people while true accuracy fell.
- **Spend collection effort on new sources.** When between-source variation is large, a row from a new person is worth far more than another row from a known one.
- **With very few sources, use leave-one-source-out folds** and state the uncertainty plainly.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Strong subject effects, by construction.** The signature and the propensity both have a standard deviation of 2, large enough that a person's rows are easy to recognise. Where differences between people are small, the gap between the two kinds of cross-validation shrinks towards the one-row-per-person case, where it vanishes.
- **Effects that do not transfer at all.** Here nothing about a person helps with a new one. In practice some person-level traits are measured, and a model that uses them generalises better; the problem is the part that is not measured.
- **Twenty subjects is few.** Subject-level cross-validation with 20 groups trains on 16 of them per fold, and its estimates are noisy from data set to data set. With very few subjects, leave-one-subject-out folds and an honest statement of the uncertainty are the best available.
- **Balanced subjects.** Every subject contributed the same number of rows. With a few heavy contributors, row-level scores are dominated by them, and the distortion can be worse.

## Key points

1. Rows from the same person share what makes that person different. With 2,000 rows from 20 people, row-level cross-validation scored five-nearest-neighbours at 0.825; on new people it scored 0.685.
2. As the same rows came from fewer people, row-level scores went up, from 0.737 to above 0.81, while true accuracy fell at every step, so row-level folds favoured exactly the data sets that generalise worst.
3. With 20 subjects, subject-level folds matched the truth to within 0.023, slightly pessimistic.
4. 6,400 rows from 20 people trained logistic regression to 0.788, about what 100 rows from 100 people gave. Extra rows from the same people added far less than their count, the pattern Kish's design effect describes for survey means.
5. Count people, not rows, and split the folds by whatever the deployment will be new in.
