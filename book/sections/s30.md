# A Power Law Fitted on Small Models Cannot See the Floor

Chapter 29 measured what a model loses when its inputs move away from its training data. The last chapter of Part VI measures a more ambitious extrapolation, from small models to large ones. The sentence under test is the one that justifies building large language models at all: *scaling laws predict performance*. Kaplan and colleagues found in 2020 that a language model's loss falls as a power of its parameter count over many orders of magnitude, so that a line drawn through cheap small models says what an expensive large one will achieve before it is trained. Practitioners meet the sentence whenever a pilot ladder of small models is used to choose the size of the model that will actually be trained, or to forecast what more parameters will buy. If the law holds only over a short reach, such forecasts will be optimistic, and the error will appear only after the expensive model has been built.

This chapter trains a ladder of eight small transformers under one fixed training budget and fits the law to the smallest of them. It checks the predictions for the larger models and examines the pattern of residuals rather than a single hit. It then rules out overfitting as the main cause of the misses, relates them to the number of training characters each model sees per parameter, and shows that a loss floor, the usual repair, cannot be estimated from the small models alone.

## A ladder of eight models under one budget

A *scaling law* in model size is a relation of the form

$$
L(N) = a\,N^{-b},
$$

where $L$ is the validation loss, $N$ the number of parameters and $a$, $b$ positive constants. Taking logarithms gives $\log L = \log a - b \log N$, a straight line on log-log axes with slope $-b$. That is what makes the law easy to fit and tempting to extrapolate: a few points fix the line, and the line can be extended.

The ladder here consists of eight *character-level transformers*, language models that read and predict text one character at a time, trained on Shakespeare. They range from 4,288 to 1,789,248 parameters outside the embeddings. The *embedding* parameters are the lookup tables that turn each input character into a vector; the remaining *non-embedding* parameters, in the attention and feed-forward layers, are the count Kaplan's law uses. The models are pre-norm transformers with 4 heads and a context of 64 characters, with widths from 16 to 192 and from 1 to 4 layers.

Every model gets the same recipe: 2,000 steps of AdamW, learning rate 3e-3 with warm-up and cosine decay, weight decay 0.1, batches of 32. The training text is the first 90% of a million-character Shakespeare corpus and validation uses the last 10%. Each model sees 4.1 million characters in all, about four passes over the training text. Training the ladder took 1.9 hours on two CPU threads, so the results are stored and read back. Loss is measured in *nats per character*, the average negative natural logarithm of the probability the model assigns to the next character.

```python
from standarderror.ml import scaling as sc

r = sc.results()     # written by scripts/train_scaling.py
for run in r["runs"]:
    print(f"{run['width']:3d} wide x {run['layers']} layers: "
          f"{run['non_embedding']:7,d} parameters, training loss "
          f"{run['final_train']:.3f}, validation {run['final_val']:.3f}")
```

```text

 24 wide x 2 layers:  15,864 parameters, training loss 2.153, validation 2.192
 32 wide x 2 layers:  27,296 parameters, training loss 1.956, validation 2.033
 48 wide x 2 layers:  59,376 parameters, training loss 1.736, validation 1.888
 64 wide x 3 layers: 153,472 parameters, training loss 1.569, validation 1.744
 96 wide x 3 layers: 340,800 parameters, training loss 1.461, validation 1.641
128 wide x 4 layers: 799,616 parameters, training loss 1.384, validation 1.578
192 wide x 4 layers: 1,789,248 parameters, training loss 1.333, validation 1.542
```

Validation loss falls with every step up the ladder, from 2.333 to 1.542 nats per character. Larger models are better throughout; the question is whether the law fitted to the small ones says by how much.

## Fitting on five models and predicting three

The next block fits the law to the five smallest models and asks it about the three it has not seen.

```python
small = r["runs"][:5]
law = sc.fit([x["non_embedding"] for x in small],
             [x["final_val"] for x in small], floor=False)
print(f"L = {law['a']:.3f} N^-{law['b']:.4f}")
for run in r["runs"][5:]:
    guess = sc.predict(law, run["non_embedding"])
    print(f"{run['non_embedding']:7,d} parameters: predicted "
          f"{guess:.3f}, measured {run['final_val']:.3f}")
```

```text
L = 4.821 N^-0.0846
340,800 parameters: predicted 1.640, measured 1.641
799,616 parameters: predicted 1.526, measured 1.578
1,789,248 parameters: predicted 1.425, measured 1.542
```

The exponent is 0.085. The sixth model, 2.2 times the size of the fifth, comes in +0.04% from the prediction: the law did exactly what it is famous for. The seventh, 5.2 times the fifth, is +3.4% off, and the eighth, 12 times the fifth, is +8.2% off. Every miss is in the same direction: the large models are worse than the line promised. The figure below shows the fit.

## Judge a law by its residuals, not by one hit

A more cautious analyst would fit on fewer models and check on more, and might be reassured. Fitted to the three smallest models, the law's exponent is 0.070, and its prediction for the largest model, 66 times the third, is off by only -0.1%. Taken alone, that looks like a strong confirmation of the law.

The *residuals*, the relative differences between measured and predicted loss across the whole ladder, show how it got there. For the four models in between, the three-model law was too *pessimistic*, by as much as 5.3%: its slope was too shallow for the middle of the ladder, and the bend at the top happened to cancel the error. The figure below shows the residual profiles for laws fitted to three, five and seven models. Fitted to seven models, the law misses the eighth, only 2.2 times the seventh, by +6.2%.

![Fitted to three models, the law is too pessimistic for the middle of the ladder and lands on the largest by cancellation (-0.1%). Fitted to seven, it misses the eighth by +6.2%.](ml130-f1-profiles.png){alt="Three lines of residuals: the three-model fit dips below zero through the middle of the ladder and returns to zero at the end; the five- and seven-model fits stay near zero and then rise above it for the largest models."}

One correct prediction is not evidence that the law holds; the pattern of residuals is. If the law were right, residuals would scatter randomly about zero at every size. Instead, the three-model fit's residuals form a U, below the line through the middle of the ladder and back at the top. The five- and seven-model fits scatter by a few percent about the line through the models they were fitted to (up to 3.1% for the five) and then rise sharply above it at the top. A systematic curve in the residuals means the functional form is wrong, and no straight line on log-log axes can follow that bend.

The scatter matters too, because it sets the size of a miss that means something. The seventh model's +3.4% is comparable to the 3.1% scatter of the fitted models, so on its own it would not be convincing. Only the eighth's +8.2% clearly exceeds it.

## The misses are not mainly overfitting

The obvious suspect is overfitting. The largest model has more non-embedding parameters, 1,789,248, than the training text has characters to constrain them, about a million, and the gap between training and validation loss does widen up the ladder, from 0.026 for the smallest model to 0.210 for the largest.

The test is to fit the same law to training loss. If the large models were merely overfitting, they would do *better* than the line on the data they trained on and worse only on validation data. In fact the law fitted to the five smallest models' training loss misses in the same direction: the seventh and eighth models' training losses are +5.2% and +11.0% above prediction. These models do worse than the line on both. They are not learning as much per parameter as the small ones did, on any data.

The table below suggests why. The number of training characters seen per non-embedding parameter falls from 955 for the smallest model to 2.3 for the largest, because every model sees the same 4.1 million characters while the parameter count grows. Hoffmann and colleagues found that a *compute-optimal* language model, one that achieves the lowest loss for a fixed amount of training computation, sees on the order of twenty tokens per parameter. Their tokens are subwords and these are characters, so the comparison is only rough. The small models here are trained far past that point; the two largest, at 5.1 and 2.3, have been given the same budget and stopped well short of it. Kaplan's law in $N$ describes models that are not limited by data or training steps. The ladder's largest rungs are plausibly limited by both, though the budget was never varied to show it.

![The first five rows fix the law; the last three test it. ## The floor the small models cannot see

The standard repair, which Chapter 4 tried on learning curves, is to add a floor:

$$
L(N) = a\,N^{-b} + c,
$$

where $c$ is the loss no model size can get below with this data and this budget. On log-log axes this curve is close to a straight line while $aN^{-b}$ is much larger than $c$, and bends towards the horizontal as $aN^{-b}$ approaches it. The block fits it to the five small models and to all eight.

```python
floor = sc.fit([x["non_embedding"] for x in small],
               [x["final_val"] for x in small])        # a N^-b + c
everyone = sc.fit([x["non_embedding"] for x in r["runs"]],
                  [x["final_val"] for x in r["runs"]])
print(f"floor fitted to the 5 smallest: c = {floor['c']:.3f}")
print(f"floor fitted to all {len(r['runs'])}:     c = {everyone['c']:.3f}")
```

```text
floor fitted to the 5 smallest: c = 0.000
floor fitted to all 8:     c = 0.855
```

From the five small models the fitted floor is 0.000, the lowest value the fit allows. In their range the curve is still a straight line on log-log axes, so the data contain no curvature from which to estimate a floor, and the best fit puts it as low as it can go. With all eight models the floor is 0.86, and the curve bends towards it. The information that the curve bends lives only in the models that have already bent, which are exactly the models the law was supposed to make unnecessary.

That is Chapter 4's finding met again at a larger scale. There, a learning curve fitted early on a pilot study was optimistic in all nine fits, and adding a floor fixed only the curves that had already bent. Here the law fitted to five small models was optimistic about every model past the sixth, and the floor could not be seen from them. The arithmetic is the same in both cases: a power law extrapolated past the range that determined it assumes nothing new happens there, and within a fixed budget of data and steps, something eventually does.

The chapters of Part VI have each found that what a network's fit shows depends on conditions the fit itself does not reveal: whether labels are noisy, whether anything keeps pushing the network after the fit, whether an augmentation's assumption holds, where the inputs lie, and here, whether the budget still suffices at the next size up.

## In practice

- **Hold back more than one size when validating a scaling law.** A single correct prediction can come from cancelling errors. Fit on some sizes, check on several larger ones, and plot the residuals across the whole ladder.
- **Read the residuals, not the headline.** If they curve rather than scatter about zero, the functional form is wrong, and extrapolation will drift further the further it reaches.
- **Compare misses with the fitted models' scatter.** A miss no bigger than the scatter about the fitted points is not yet evidence of a bend; one well beyond it is.
- **Fit the law to training loss as well.** If training loss misses in the same direction, the cause is not mainly overfitting but something that limits learning, plausibly data or training steps.
- **Track data seen per parameter.** When it falls far below the level at which the small models of the ladder were trained, a law in parameter count alone may be misspecified; scale data and steps with the model, or expect a floor the small models cannot reveal.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One budget for every model.** The laws used to plan large models are fitted to models each trained with enough data and compute for their size, or along a compute-optimal frontier. This ladder holds the budget fixed on purpose, which is the setting in which a law in $N$ alone is misspecified. A ladder with data growing alongside the models would be expected to bend later, or not at all in this range; that was not run.
- **Small models, one seed each.** The models run from four thousand to two million non-embedding parameters, each trained once, and the run-to-run spread was not measured. The sixth model's agreement with the line, to a fraction of a percent, may be partly luck, and so may the seventh's miss, which is about the size of the scatter of the fitted models. The eighth's miss is more than twice that scatter, which seed noise of that size would be unlikely to produce.
- **One learning rate.** Every model used 3e-3. Larger models often want smaller rates, and part of the shortfall at the top of the ladder may be a rate that suits the small models better.
- **Character-level Shakespeare.** A million characters of one author is a narrow distribution with a low ceiling on what any model can learn from it. Kaplan's laws were measured on billions of tokens of web text, where the floor is far away.

## Key points

1. A power law fitted to five small models predicted the next size to +0.04%. Scaling laws do predict, over a short reach.
2. Over a longer reach they were optimistic: +3.4% for a model 5.2 times the largest fitted, +8.2% for one 12 times. A law fitted to three models hit the largest by cancelling two errors, and one fitted to seven missed it by +6.2%. Judge a law by its residuals, not by one hit.
3. Training loss missed the same way, so the cause was not mainly overfitting. With a fixed budget, characters per parameter fell from 955 to 2.3, consistent with the largest models being limited by data and steps rather than size; the budget itself was not varied.
4. A floor fitted to the small models was 0.000; fitted to all eight, 0.86. Extrapolate a fitted law only as far as the conditions that produced it still hold.
