# A Missing Value Is Information, Until the Process Behind It Changes

Chapters 36 and 37 were about rows that resemble each other because of where they came from. Missing values are about rows that are incomplete because of where they came from. A blood test not ordered, a survey question skipped, a sensor that dropped out: each leaves a gap in the table. The standard advice treats every gap the same way, and this chapter tests it in the form *missing values are a nuisance; fill them in as accurately as you can and model the rest.* Almost every tabular pipeline contains an imputation step built on this sentence. If it is false, a careful imputation can throw away the most informative thing in the data, or a model can come to depend on a pattern of gaps that will not survive a change in how the data are collected.

The chapter first sets out the standard classification of missing data, which says in principle when the sentence is safe. It then removes values from a complete data set in three different ways and compares five strategies under each: gaps at random, gaps that depend on the missing value, and gaps that depend on the outcome. A fourth condition changes the gap mechanism between training and testing. The chapter ends with how to choose a strategy.

## Three kinds of missingness

Rubin's classification of 1976 distinguishes three mechanisms by what the probability of a gap depends on.

- **Missing completely at random (MCAR).** Whether a value is missing is independent of everything, observed or not. The gaps are then a pure loss of data: the observed rows are a random subsample of the complete ones.
- **Missing at random (MAR).** Whether a value is missing depends only on other values that were observed. The missing values can then be filled in, in a statistical sense, from the rest of the row.
- **Missing not at random (MNAR).** Whether a value is missing depends on the value itself, or on something outside the observed features, such as the outcome the model has to predict. The gap then carries information that the observed values do not.

The sentence under test is safe under the first mechanism and, with a good imputer, under the second. Under the third it is not obvious what "as accurately as you can" should mean.

## The experiment

The breast-cancer data, 569 tumours with 30 features each, are complete, so gaps can be made to order. In each of three ways, 30% of the cells are removed, over five random masks. Five strategies are compared, each followed by logistic regression except the last:

- **Mean.** Replace each gap with the column mean.
- **Mean plus indicator.** The same, plus one *missingness indicator* per feature: a binary column that is 1 where the value was missing, so the model can give the fact of absence its own weight.
- **5-NN imputation.** Fill each gap with the average of that feature over the five most similar tumours, similarity being measured on the observed features.
- **Iterative imputation.** Model each feature with missing values as a regression on the other features, fill the gaps with the predictions, and cycle through the features repeatedly (here for 10 rounds) so that each round's fills improve the next.
- **Boosted trees, native.** Gradient-boosted trees given the raw gaps. At each split such a learner decides which branch rows with a missing value should follow, choosing whichever direction most reduces the loss.

Every imputer is fitted inside the cross-validation folds, so no information from a test fold reaches the imputation. Models are compared by *log loss*, the average of $-\log$ of the probability the model gave to the true class, which penalises confident mistakes heavily, and by accuracy. Imputations are compared by their root mean squared error on the removed cells, in standard deviations of each feature.

The first block reports the complete data and the first two mechanisms.

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

m = rows.missing_study()          # 30% of cells, 5 masks
print(f"complete data: log loss {m['complete']['nll']:.3f}")
for kind in ("random", "large values"):
    print(f"missing: {kind}")
    for meth, r in m[kind].items():
        if not meth.startswith("_"):
            print(f"   {meth:22s} log loss {r['nll']:.3f}, "
                  f"accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}")
```

```text

missing: random
   mean                   log loss 0.113, accuracy 0.960
   mean + indicator       log loss 0.124, accuracy 0.952
   5-NN imputation        log loss 0.119, accuracy 0.957
   iterative imputation   log loss 0.091, accuracy 0.969
   boosted trees, native  log loss 0.174, accuracy 0.948
missing: large values
   mean                   log loss 0.261, accuracy 0.880
   mean + indicator       log loss 0.121, accuracy 0.957
   5-NN imputation        log loss 0.170, accuracy 0.931
   iterative imputation   log loss 0.143, accuracy 0.945
   boosted trees, native  log loss 0.151, accuracy 0.953
```

## Gaps at random: the best imputation gives the best model

Missing completely at random, the sentence mostly holds. Iterative imputation recovers the removed values best, with a root mean squared error of 0.44 standard deviations against 1.01 for the column mean, and it gives the best model: log loss 0.091, against 0.113 for the mean and 0.079 for the complete data. The thirty features are strongly correlated measurements of the same tumours, so most of a missing value can be read off the others, and a regression on them recovers it well.

The indicator columns add nothing here, because a random gap has nothing to indicate. They cost a little: 0.124 against 0.113. The ordering between imputation accuracy and model quality is not perfect, though. Five-nearest-neighbour imputation recovers the values much better than the mean (0.63 standard deviations against 1.01) and still gives a slightly worse model (0.119 against 0.113). The figure below shows accuracy for every strategy under all four conditions this chapter examines.

## When the gap depends on the value

In the second setting the chance that a cell goes missing rises with its value: in every feature, the larger measurements are the ones that tend to be absent. This is missing not at random. The second half of the output changes sharply. Mean imputation now replaces precisely the large values with an average one, systematically, so a tumour with large measurements is made to look ordinary, and the model falls to accuracy 0.880 and log loss 0.261.

Iterative imputation does better, 0.143, but it can only fill a gap with what the observed features predict. Those observed features were selected by the same mechanism, so the regression that fills the gaps is fitted on values skewed towards the small end and is likely to pull its predictions towards them. The simplest fix wins: mean imputation plus an indicator, log loss 0.121. As an imputation it is the worst of the three, 1.74 standard deviations of error against 1.31, since the mean is far from the large values it replaces. But the indicator says "this value was probably large", and logistic regression gives that its own weight, recovering the information the imputation lost. Boosted trees, which learn a direction for missing values at each split, reach 0.151, between iterative imputation and the plain mean.

This is the first half of the sentence failing: filling in accurately and predicting well are different objectives. Josse and colleagues studied the question theoretically. A learner is *consistent* if, as the training set grows, its predictions approach the best possible ones. They showed that when the gaps are not informative, even imputing a constant is consistent for a learner flexible enough to recover the pattern from where the constant appears, and recommended letting trees treat missingness as its own branch, which also captures informative gaps. Logistic regression is not that flexible, so here the indicator has to be handed to it explicitly; a careful imputation that hides the pattern loses it. ## When the gap depends on the outcome

The third mechanism is the one hospitals produce. A cell is missing nine times as often for a benign tumour as for a malignant one, the way a follow-up test is ordered mainly when something looks wrong. Now the gap depends on the outcome itself. The next block also includes a fourth condition, described below, in which the gap mechanism changes after training.

```python
for kind in ("by diagnosis", "policy change"):
    print(f"missing: {kind}")
    for meth, r in m[kind].items():
        if not meth.startswith("_"):
            print(f"   {meth:22s} log loss {r['nll']:.3f}, "
                  f"accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}")
```

```text
missing: by diagnosis
   mean                   log loss 0.150, accuracy 0.952
   mean + indicator       log loss 0.012, accuracy 0.999
   5-NN imputation        log loss 0.064, accuracy 0.982
   iterative imputation   log loss 0.091, accuracy 0.972
   boosted trees, native  log loss 0.041, accuracy 0.988
missing: policy change
   mean                   log loss 0.124, accuracy 0.954
   mean + indicator       log loss 0.458, accuracy 0.817
   5-NN imputation        log loss 0.130, accuracy 0.952
   iterative imputation   log loss 0.087, accuracy 0.972
   boosted trees, native  log loss 0.362, accuracy 0.892
```

### The pattern of gaps predicts the diagnosis

With indicators, logistic regression scores an accuracy of 0.999, with log loss 0.012. That is better than the same model on the complete data, 0.980, because the pattern of missing tests says almost everything about the diagnosis: a tumour with many gaps is very probably benign. Boosted trees find the same signal on their own (0.988). Five-nearest-neighbour imputation also beats the complete data (0.982), presumably because the neighbours it borrows from are chosen using the cells that happened to be observed, so tumours with similar gap patterns, and hence similar diagnoses, fill each other's gaps. Agniel, Kohane and Weber found the real version of this in hospital records: for most of the laboratory tests they studied, the timing of the test predicted three-year survival better than its result.

### When the policy changes, the signal disappears

The model has learned the clinicians' behaviour, which is a legitimate signal for as long as the clinicians behave the same way. The policy-change condition shows what happens when they do not. The same trained models are scored on tumours whose cells are missing at random, as though the testing policy had changed: every test now ordered, or lost, regardless of suspicion. The indicator model falls to 0.817, and the boosted trees to 0.892. Their strongest feature has stopped meaning what it meant in training. Iterative imputation, which never looked at the pattern, scores 0.972, the same accuracy it had when tested under the mechanism it was trained on, with log loss 0.087 against 0.091. (With random gaps in both training and testing it scored 0.969.)

## Choosing a strategy

The three settings produce three different winners, and no strategy is safe by default.

- If the gaps are close to random, impute as well as possible and leave the pattern out.
- If the gaps depend on the missing values, give the model the pattern, through indicators or a learner that handles missing values natively.
- If the gaps depend on a decision made by people or systems upstream, the pattern will probably be the strongest feature in the model, and its value lasts only as long as the decision rule does.

Which case applies cannot be read from the data alone. Missing-not-at-random is, by definition, not *identifiable* from what was observed: two different mechanisms can produce exactly the same observed data, so no test on those data can tell them apart. The answer has to come from knowing how the data were collected. What can be measured is how much the model depends on the pattern: fit it with and without indicators, and if the indicators add a great deal, find out why the values are missing before trusting the gain. The next chapter takes the most extreme form of outcome-dependent missingness, in which an entire row's label is missing because of a decision.

## In practice

- **Ask why each value is missing** before choosing an imputer. Find out, for each column with gaps, who or what decides whether it is recorded.
- **Fit the model with and without missingness indicators** and compare cross-validated scores. A small difference is consistent with gaps close to random; a large gain means the model depends on the collection process.
- **Do not judge an imputer by its reconstruction error alone.** Here the worst imputation with an indicator beat the best imputation without one when large values went missing.
- **Fit imputers inside the folds**, as with every preprocessing step.
- **Stress-test an indicator-dependent model** by scoring it with the gaps redistributed at random, which imitates a change in collection policy. If performance collapses, document the dependence and monitor the policy.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Artificial mechanisms.** The three masks are clean versions of what real data mix together. Real missingness is usually partly random, partly value-dependent and partly decided by someone, in proportions nobody wrote down.
- **Thirty correlated features.** Breast-cancer measurements are redundant, which is why iterative imputation does so well at random. With weakly related features, every imputation is closer to the mean, and the differences shrink.
- **One rate.** Thirty percent of cells, everywhere. Real gaps concentrate in a few columns and a few rows, and dropping such columns or rows is sometimes the better choice.
- **Small data.** With 569 tumours, the differences between strategies at random are about two hundredths of log loss; under the informative mechanisms they are several times larger, which is the point.

## Key points

1. Missing at random, the best imputation gave the best model: iterative imputation, error 0.44 standard deviations, log loss 0.091; the mean, 1.01, log loss 0.113. Indicators added nothing.
2. Imputation accuracy and predictive quality are different objectives: 5-NN imputation recovered values better than the mean and still gave a slightly worse model.
3. When large values went missing, the indicator mattered more than the imputation: 0.121 with mean imputation and indicators, 0.143 with iterative imputation alone.
4. When the gaps followed the diagnosis, the indicator model scored 0.999, above the complete data, and 0.817 once the gaps became random.
5. Ask why each value is missing before choosing a strategy, and treat a large gain from missingness indicators as a dependence on the process that collected the data.

## Further reading

- Rubin, "Inference and missing data", *Biometrika* (1976): the original classification of missing-data mechanisms.
- Josse, Prost, Scornet and Varoquaux, "On the consistency of supervised learning with missing values" (2019): when simple imputation suffices for prediction, and why trees can treat missingness as a branch.
- Agniel, Kohane and Weber, "Biases in electronic health record data due to processes within the healthcare system", *BMJ* (2018): evidence that the timing of laboratory tests can predict outcomes better than their results.
