# DBSCAN's Radius Is a Density, and It Gets Only One

k-means cuts the data into k convex pieces, which is why Chapter 32 found it cutting two parallel groups crosswise. Density-based clustering starts from a different definition: a cluster is a region where points are packed closely, separated from other such regions by sparse space. DBSCAN, the best-known density method, carries a reputation that follows from that definition, and it is the sentence under test in this chapter: *DBSCAN finds clusters of any shape without being told how many*. DBSCAN does not take k. It does take a radius, and if the radius decides the answer as much as k decides a k-means answer, then the method has moved the choice rather than removed it, and a clustering reported at one radius is a statement about that radius.

The chapter defines DBSCAN and how its results are scored, then measures on two half-moons how wide the window of good radii is and whether the standard heuristic finds it. Next it places clusters of different densities side by side, where a single radius fails, and compares HDBSCAN, which needs no radius. Finally it applies both to the handwritten digits.

## Clusters as dense regions

DBSCAN (density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise) was introduced by Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu in 1996 and has been widely used since. It makes the definition of a cluster operational with two settings, a radius `eps` and a count `min_samples`:

- A **core point** has at least `min_samples` points, itself included, within distance `eps`.
- Core points within `eps` of each other belong to the same cluster, and so, by chaining, does every core point reachable through a sequence of such steps.
- A non-core point within `eps` of a core point joins that core point's cluster as a **border point**.
- Anything not reachable from a core point is **noise** and belongs to no cluster.

Together, `eps` and `min_samples` set a density threshold: a point is in a cluster's interior if its neighbourhood of radius `eps` holds at least `min_samples` points. The number of clusters is whatever number of connected dense regions results.

Agreement with the true groups is measured throughout by the *adjusted Rand index* (ARI), which compares two partitions by the pairs of points they treat alike and corrects for chance, giving 1 for a perfect match and about 0 for an unrelated partition. Noise needs a convention. Here every noise point is counted as its own singleton cluster, so that calling a whole cluster noise costs what it should. (If noise were counted as one more cluster, a cluster's worth of discarded points could earn a high score by forming a "cluster" of its own.) The "best" radius for any data set below is chosen with the true labels, which a user never has. It is the most DBSCAN can do, not what it will do.

## Any shape, at the right radius

Two interlocking half-moons, 1,000 points from scikit-learn's `make_moons`, are the textbook case for density clustering, and the textbook is right. The block below sweeps the radius and reports the best score, the window of radii scoring at least 0.9, the score at a heuristic radius, and k-means.

```python
from standarderror.ml import unsup

m = unsup.dbscan_moons()            # 1,000 points, two half-moons
lo, hi = m["window"]
print(f"best {m['best']:.2f}; radii scoring 0.9+: {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}")
print(f"knee radius {m['knee']:.3f} scores {m['knee_score']:.2f}; "
      f"k-means scores {m['kmeans_ari']:.2f}")
```

```text
best 1.00; radii scoring 0.9+: 0.071 to 0.206
knee radius 0.061 scores 0.57; k-means scores 0.25
```

k-means, asked for two clusters, cut the moons in half and scored an ARI of 0.25: its pieces are convex, and the moons are not. DBSCAN scored 0.95 or more at every radius between 0.071 and 0.206, and separated the moons perfectly from 0.116 to 0.206. Outside that window it fails in two opposite ways. Smaller radii set the density threshold too high for the moons' own sparser stretches, so each moon shatters into fragments and noise. Larger radii let the chain of core points bridge the narrow gap where the two moons come close, and the moons join into one cluster.

### Finding the window with the k-distance plot

The good window is about a factor of 2.9 wide, the perfect one 1.8, and something has to find it without labels. Ester and colleagues suggested the *k-distance plot*: compute every point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour, sort these distances, and read the radius off the *knee* of the curve, where the distances start to climb steeply. The reasoning is that points inside clusters have small k-distances and noise points large ones, so the knee marks the boundary between them. Here the knee was found geometrically, as the point on the sorted curve farthest below the straight chord joining its ends, using each point's fourth-nearest other point.

The knee was at 0.061, just below the window, and that radius scored 0.57. That miss belonged to this draw of the moons. Chapter 66, which reruns single-draw results from across the book on fresh data, repeated this on five other draws of the moons, and the knee fell inside the good window every time, scoring 0.80 to 0.96. The heuristic is a reasonable start on data like the moons, and, as the digits below show, it can still land in the wrong place.

### The second setting moves the window

The other setting, `min_samples`, moves the window more than it widens it. With `min_samples` at 3 or 5 the radii scoring at least 0.9 ran from 0.071 to 0.206; at 10, they began at 0.081; at 20, they ran from 0.106 to 0.247; at 40, from 0.151 to 0.308. Requiring more neighbours raises the density a core point needs, so the same moons need a larger radius to reach it. The two settings act roughly as one density threshold written two ways, and fixing either leaves the other to be tuned. scikit-learn's defaults, a radius of 0.5 with 5 points, put both moons into one cluster and scored 0.00. The default radius is a number, not a property of anyone's data; it depends on the units in which distances happen to be measured.

Schubert and colleagues, revisiting DBSCAN in 2017, give rules of thumb for checking a result. If the largest cluster holds more than 20 to 50% of the clustered points, reduce the radius or use a hierarchical method such as HDBSCAN. Noise should usually be between 1 and 30% of the points. And `min_samples` should be about twice the number of dimensions.

## One radius cannot serve two densities

A single radius with a fixed `min_samples` is a single density threshold, and real clusters differ in density. The second simulation makes that difference controllable. Two tight clusters of 300 points, each with standard deviation 0.2, sit 1.4 apart. A third cluster of 300 points, at distance 6 from them, has a spread that grows from 0.2, the same as the others, to 1.6, eight times theirs. In two dimensions eight times the spread means about 64 times lower density, since the same number of points occupies an area that grows with the square of the spread.

This creates a dilemma that no radius resolves. A radius small enough to keep the tight pair apart is too small for the wide cluster's sparse points to qualify as core points, so the wide cluster dissolves into noise. A radius large enough to hold the wide cluster together lets the dense region of the tight pair bridge the gap between them, and the pair merges. The block below prints, for the equal and the eightfold spread, how many of the 150 radii tried scored at least 0.9, the best score, and HDBSCAN's score.

```python
for d in unsup.dbscan_densities(spreads=(0.2, 1.6)):
    print(f"spread {d['ratio']:.0f}x: {d['good_radii']:3d} good radii, "
          f"best {d['best']:.3f}, HDBSCAN {d['hdbscan']:.3f}")
```

```text
spread 1x:  36 good radii, best 0.998, HDBSCAN 0.992
spread 8x:   0 good radii, best 0.729, HDBSCAN 0.884
```

With equal spreads, 36 of the 150 radii scored at least 0.9. As the third cluster widened through the five spreads tried, the count fell to 32, 7, 1 and 0. Five further draws of the data gave 0 or 1 good radii at sixfold spread and none at eightfold. At eightfold spread the best radius in the first draw scored 0.73. It kept the tight pair apart by declaring 77% of the wide cluster noise, which is 26% of all the points; the other draws' best radii scored 0.73 to 0.85. The number of clusters was never the problem; the single density was. Schubert and colleagues' noise rule would have accepted that answer, since a quarter of the points is inside their range of 1 to 30%. ### HDBSCAN builds every radius at once

HDBSCAN, from Campello, Moulavi and Sander in 2013, removes the single radius. Conceptually, it builds DBSCAN's clusters at every radius at once, which gives a hierarchy: as the radius shrinks, clusters split and shed points as noise. It then keeps the clusters that persist over the widest range of densities, subject to a *minimum cluster size*, which replaces the radius as the setting the user chooses (25 here). Because different clusters can be selected at different levels of the hierarchy, a dense cluster and a sparse one can both survive.

In the first draw HDBSCAN scored 0.88 to 1.00 across the five spreads, and in the other five draws 0.82 to 0.89 at eightfold spread. It was better than any single radius at large contrasts, and not perfect either. The table below collects the counts and scores.

## The digits

In the digits' 64 pixel dimensions, the best of 60 radii between 5 and 40, chosen with the labels, scored 0.71 at a radius of 21.2. It did so by producing 20 clusters and calling 17% of the images noise: the ten classes broken into denser and sparser pieces. The knee of the k-distance curve was at 25.2. At that radius DBSCAN put 96% of the images into a single cluster and scored 0.00: the radius that looked natural on the curve was just past the point where the classes merge. The two radii differ by 19%, a margin that a reading of a sorted-distance curve is unlikely to resolve reliably.

Schubert and colleagues' largest-cluster rule would have caught the knee's answer, since one cluster held far more than half the data. But their suggested `min_samples` for 64 dimensions is 128, not 5, so this test departs from their guidance.

HDBSCAN did not rescue the digits. It scored 0.38 and called 54% of them noise. (Chapter 56 quoted 0.17 for the same clustering because it counted noise as one more cluster; counting noise points as singletons gives the higher figure.) Chapter 33 found that the digits' nearest neighbours behave like those of uniform data in only about eight dimensions, so the concentration of distances that afflicts truly high-dimensional data is not the obvious culprit; why density estimates fail on the raw pixels was not measured. Chapter 56 also found that the same HDBSCAN applied to a t-SNE picture of the digits recovered them well, because the picture had already turned neighbourhoods into dense, separated blobs.

DBSCAN's freedom from k, then, is real, but it is paid for with a density threshold that must be right and must be the same everywhere. The next chapter moves from finding dense groups to finding the points that belong to none, and asks what an anomaly detector's output actually is.

## In practice

- **Treat `eps` as a declared density.** Choosing a radius, together with `min_samples`, is choosing how dense a region must be to count as a cluster. Report both with every result, and never rely on scikit-learn's default radius.
- **Sweep the radius and watch what changes.** Run at the chosen radius, half of it and double it, and record the number of clusters, the size of the largest and the noise share. If they change a lot, the clustering is a statement about the radius.
- **Do not trust the k-distance knee by itself.** It missed the good window once on the moons and on the digits landed just past the point where every class merged. Use it as a starting point for the sweep.
- **Check the largest cluster and the noise share.** Apply Schubert and colleagues' rules: a largest cluster above 20 to 50% of the clustered points or noise outside 1 to 30% calls for another look. Remember that a passing noise share can still hide a discarded cluster.
- **When clusters may differ in density, use a method that does not need one radius.** HDBSCAN handled a large density contrast better than any single radius, though not perfectly; its minimum cluster size is then the setting to vary.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Two dimensions, Gaussian clusters.** The density-contrast problem is cleanest in two dimensions; how it changes in more dimensions was not measured beyond the digits.
- **One `min_samples`.** Five is scikit-learn's default; Ester and colleagues suggested four for two dimensions. Raising it smooths the density estimate and moves the good window; it does not give one radius two densities.
- **One knee rule.** The knee was found by a simple geometric rule. People reading the curve by eye will pick other points, and the moons' window was narrow enough that a small difference mattered.
- **HDBSCAN's own setting.** Its minimum cluster size plays the part the radius played, with a gentler effect here. It was not swept.

## Key points

1. At the right radius DBSCAN separated two half-moons where k-means scored 0.25. Radii scoring 0.95 or more spanned a factor of 2.9, and in this draw the k-distance knee fell just outside them (in five other draws, reported in Chapter 66, inside).
2. The radius and `min_samples` act as one density threshold: raising one shifts the good range of the other.
3. With one cluster eight times the spread of its neighbours, no radius scored 0.9 in any of six draws; the best in the first called 77% of the wide cluster noise. HDBSCAN scored 0.82 to 0.89.
4. On the digits the knee's radius put 96% of the images in one cluster and scored 0.00; the best radius, chosen with the labels, scored 0.71.
5. Treat `eps` as the density you declare a cluster to have. Sweep it and look at what changes, check the largest cluster and the noise share, and when clusters may differ in density use a method that does not need one radius.
