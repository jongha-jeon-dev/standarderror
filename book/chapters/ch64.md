# How Much History to Retrain On Depends on How Fast the World Moves

Chapter 61 found that a change in how outcomes depend on inputs shows up only in labelled outcomes; no monitor on the inputs can see it. The standard defence does not try to detect such a change at all. It retrains the model on a schedule, on recent data, so that the model follows the world wherever it goes. The practice rests on a sentence with two parts that can each fail: *retrain regularly on the most recent data and the model stays current.* "Most recent" can be the wrong amount of data, and "regularly" assumes the labels are there to retrain on. Either failure leaves a team paying for retraining that does not keep the model current.

This chapter tests both parts on simulated data streams. It first compares retraining windows of different lengths in four kinds of world, from stable to abruptly changing, and shows that the best window depends on how fast the world moves. It then asks whether the window can be chosen from past performance, and finally measures what a delay in labels costs.

## A stream of data and five retraining policies

### The four worlds

The test is a stream. Each period brings 60 new rows with ten standard Gaussian inputs and a logistic outcome. The relation between inputs and outcome, the world's true coefficient vector, behaves differently in four kinds of world:

- **stable**: it stays fixed;
- **slow drift**: it takes a small random step each period;
- **fast drift**: it takes a larger random step each period;
- **jump**: it stays fixed and then, halfway through, at period 40, jumps once to a new direction.

The relation's strength, the length of the coefficient vector, is held constant throughout, so only its direction changes. A change in direction means that the same inputs now point to different outcomes: concept drift in the sense of Chapter 61.

### The policies

After ten periods of history, a logistic model is retrained every period under five policies. *Never retrain* keeps the model fitted on the first ten periods forever. *All history*, also called an *expanding window*, refits on every labelled row seen so far. The other three use a *sliding window*, refitting only on the last 1, 4 or 16 periods. Each policy is scored on the next period's rows, which the model has not seen, and everything is averaged over six streams per world.

The code generates one fast-drift stream and prints its shape.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import deploy

rng = np.random.default_rng([0, 2])            # one fast-drift stream
Xs, ys = deploy._stream("fast drift", rng, periods=80, rows=60, dim=10)
print(len(Xs), "periods of", Xs[0].shape[0], "rows,",
      Xs[0].shape[1], "inputs; outcome rate in period 0:",
      round(float(ys[0].mean()), 2))
```

```text
80 periods of 60 rows, 10 inputs; outcome rate in period 0: 0.43
```

## The best window depends on the world

### In a stable world, recent data is just less data

In the stable world, the policy that sounds most current was the worst. Retraining on the last period alone, 60 rows, gave accuracy 0.729, against 0.779 for all history. Nothing was changing, so discarding old data only discarded sample size. A ten-input model fitted on 60 rows has noisy coefficients, and each period's model was a fresh draw of that noise. The model that was never retrained scored 0.777, as good as any: in a stable world, a model fitted once on enough data has nothing to catch up with.

### Under drift, old data becomes misleading

Under slow drift the order changed, and the best policy was a long window: the last 16 periods scored 0.770, all history 0.762, the last period 0.728. Under fast drift, all history fell to 0.648, dragged towards relations that no longer held, and the last 4 periods scored 0.740. After a jump, the last 4 periods were best again (0.758).

The next block runs the comparison for the stable and fast-drift worlds and prints each policy's mean accuracy. It uses two streams per world rather than six, so its figures differ slightly from those quoted above.

```python
r = deploy.retraining(delays=(0,), reps=2)["results"]
for world in ("stable", "fast drift"):
    v = r[f"{world}/0"]["mean"]
    print(world)
    print("  " + "  ".join(f"{p} {a:.3f}" for p, a in v.items()))
```

```text
stable
  static 0.778  expanding 0.782  window 1 0.727  window 4 0.765  window 16 0.774
fast drift
  static 0.605  expanding 0.661  window 1 0.723  window 4 0.744  window 16 0.715
```

The pattern is the same: in the stable world accuracy rises with the window, and under fast drift it peaks at four periods and falls on either side.

### A bias-variance trade along the time axis

So the best window was all history, 16, 4 and 4 in the four worlds, and no window was best in every kind of world. This is the bias-variance trade of Chapter 9, with the window length as the knob, laid along the time axis. A long window has low *variance*: it averages over many rows, so its coefficients are stable. But it has *bias* when the world moves, because it is fitted partly to relations that no longer hold. A short window tracks change, so its bias is small, but it is fitted on few rows and is noisy. The best length balances the two, and the balance point depends on how fast the relation moves relative to how many rows arrive per period.

Widmer and Kubat built adaptive windows for this in 1996, and Klinkenberg and Joachims chose window sizes by estimating error on recent data. Both work by measuring how fast the world is moving, which a fixed schedule does not. The table below collects every world and policy.

## Choosing the window by backtest

### Right when the pace is steady

The practical fix is to choose the window the way Klinkenberg and Joachims did, by its record. A *backtest* replays the past: at each earlier date, retrain each policy on the data available then, score it on the following period, and keep the policy with the best record. Done here on the first 30 scored periods of each world (periods 10 to 39, averaged over the six streams) and judged on the remaining 40, the backtest chose all history in the stable world, which was right, and the four-period window under fast drift, also right (0.741).

### Wrong when the pace changes

It chose wrong in the other two worlds. Under slow drift the first 30 periods favoured all history, because the relation had not yet moved far, and over the next 40 that choice scored 0.754 against 0.768 for the sixteen-period window. In the world that jumped, the first 30 periods were all before the jump and favoured all history, which then scored 0.625 over the remaining periods against 0.750 for the four-period window.

A backtest measures how fast the world moved, not how fast it will move. It works when the pace of change is itself stable, and it cannot anticipate a change that has not happened in its history.

### The cost of never retraining

The never-retrained model is the other reference point. In the stable world it lost nothing. Under fast drift it scored 0.572, and after the jump its accuracy fell to 0.538, not far above a coin, because the old direction said little about the new one. A model that is never retrained bets that the world is stable, and the bet is either free or very expensive.

## Late labels delay every recovery

The schedule also assumes that labels are there to retrain on. Often they are not. A loan defaults months after the decision, a churned customer is known only after the renewal date, and a fraud label waits for an investigation. The *label delay* is the time between a prediction and the arrival of its outcome. With labels arriving eight periods late, each retraining could use only rows at least eight periods old.

In the stable world the delay cost nothing (0.776 against 0.779 for all history), since old rows were as good as new. Under fast drift every retrained policy got worse, and the best one's advantage over never retraining fell from 0.168 to 0.096: the delay took away 43% of what retraining bought. A delayed model is always trained on a relation that is eight periods out of date, and in a fast-moving world eight periods is a long time.

The jump makes the cost concrete. Averaged over the stream, the four-period window scored 0.730 with late labels against 0.758 with prompt ones. After the jump, it took 3 periods to recover with immediate labels and 19 with late ones, 16 more, against a delay of eight. All history did not recover within the 40 periods after the jump either way, still anchored to the relation before it. Every period of delay is a period the model spends wrong before it can even begin to learn the new relation, and nothing in the inputs can shorten it, as Chapter 61 found. Much of the evidence in this book, this chapter included, comes from simulated worlds built to separate one effect from another. The next chapter takes four verdicts that earlier chapters measured only on simulations and retries them on real data.

## In practice

- **Do not assume shorter is safer.** In a stable world a short window only adds noise; with 60 rows a period the last period alone cost 0.049 of accuracy against all history.
- **Measure how fast your relation changes** from performance on labelled outcomes over time, and choose the window from that, not from a habit or a schedule.
- **Backtest the window choice on your own history**, retraining at each past date only on data whose labels would have been available then. Re-run the backtest periodically, since it reflects the past pace of change, not the future one.
- **Know your label delay and plan around it.** Under change it eats much of what retraining buys and lengthens every recovery. Shorten it where you can, for instance with faster investigation of outcomes.
- **Keep a never-retrained baseline** in your evaluation. If it holds up, retraining is buying little; if it collapses, the world is moving.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Logistic model, logistic world.** The model was correctly specified in every period, so its only problems were noise and staleness. A misspecified model adds a third: more data of the wrong kind cannot fix it.
- **Fixed windows.** Weighting rows by age, or retraining only when monitored performance drops, sit between the policies measured here; their best settings depend on the drift rate in the same way.
- **Constant volume.** Sixty rows a period made short windows noisy. With far more rows per period, short windows lose less in a stable world.
- **Six streams per cell.** Differences of under a point between neighbouring windows are within the noise; the ordering of the best policies across worlds was not.

## Key points

1. In a stable world, retraining on the last period alone cost 0.049 of accuracy against all history, and never retraining cost nothing.
2. The best window was all history, the last 16 periods, the last 4 and the last 4 across four worlds; no fixed choice was best everywhere. Window length is a bias-variance knob.
3. A backtest chose the right window when the pace of change was steady and the wrong one when it was not.
4. Labels eight periods late took 43% of retraining's gain under fast drift and added 16 periods to the recovery after a jump.
5. Choose a retraining window by measuring how fast your relation changes, from performance on labelled outcomes over time. Plan around your label delay; shorten it where you can, because it adds to every period a changed model spends wrong.
