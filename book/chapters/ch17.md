# Clusters

Clustering is what a practitioner reaches for when there is no target to predict but there is a question about groups: which customers form segments, which documents belong together, whether a set of measurements falls into types. The output is a label for every row, and those labels are then named, profiled and acted on as if they had been found in the data. Chapter 16 showed that a picture of high-dimensional data can draw structure that is not there. This chapter asks the same of the algorithms that assign the groups, and of the rules used to decide how many groups there are.

Every clustering method answers two questions at once: where the boundaries between groups lie, and how many groups there are. The four sections take the most widely used methods and rules in turn and test the sentence each is usually summarised by:

- *k-means finds the clusters in the data* (Section 17.1);
- *the BIC tells you how many clusters a Gaussian mixture should have* (Section 17.2);
- *DBSCAN finds clusters of any shape without being told how many* (Section 17.3);
- *the right number of clusters is the one whose clustering is most stable under resampling* (Section 17.4).

The method throughout is the one this book uses for every claim: run the method on data whose answer is known. Here that means simulated shapes in two dimensions, chosen so that each method's assumptions either hold or visibly fail, and including shapes with no clusters at all; and the handwritten digits, whose ten classes give a real data set with a known number of groups. Agreement with the true groups is measured by the adjusted Rand index, defined in Section 17.1 and used in every section after it.

The sections build on each other. Section 17.1 starts with k-means and the silhouette, the default pair, and introduces the tools the rest of the chapter uses: the adjusted Rand index, the idea of comparing a clustering score with its value on structureless reference data, and the uniform square and the digits as test cases. It finds that k-means draws convex pieces of similar spread whatever the data contain. Section 17.2 replaces the heuristic with a probability model, a Gaussian mixture whose number of components is chosen by a likelihood criterion, and finds that the criterion counts Gaussians rather than clusters. Section 17.3 drops the number of clusters altogether and defines a cluster by density, and finds that the choice of k has been replaced by the choice of a single density threshold. Section 17.4 returns to choosing k, now by asking which answer reproduces under resampling, sets the gap statistic beside it, and closes by drawing together what the methods without labels in this part of the book measured.

The common finding is that each method answers a narrower question than the one it is used for: how to cut space into tidy convex pieces, how many Gaussians of a given shape the data can pay for, which regions exceed one density, whether the clustering objective has a unique optimum. None of these is the same as whether the data contain groups. The practical consequence, developed in the closing sections, is to test every clustering score and every rule for choosing k on data with no clusters before believing what it says about data that might have some.

## k-Means Finds k Pieces Whether or Not There Are Clusters

<!-- source: ml-32-kmeans; hero: ml132-hero.png -->


Section 16.1 reduced the inputs without looking at a target. Clustering goes further and invents a target: it assigns each row to a group, and the groups are then named, profiled and acted on as if they had been found in the data. The most used algorithm is k-means, and the sentence under test is how it is usually described: *k-means finds the clusters in the data*. If the sentence is false, a segmentation can be a partition of empty space, or a slicing that cuts real groups in half, and the usual quality score will not say which.

This section first defines what k-means optimises and how the silhouette is used to choose the number of clusters. It then runs both on four simulated shapes, one of which has no clusters at all, and compares the silhouette on noise with its value on handwritten digits that do have classes. It examines two kinds of real group that k-means cuts wrongly, and finally measures how much the answer depends on where the algorithm starts.

### What k-means optimises, and how k is chosen


Given a number $k$, *k-means* places $k$ centres $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k$ and assigns each point to its nearest centre, choosing the centres to make the *within-cluster sum of squares* as small as it can:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \min_{j} \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2 .
$$

The standard algorithm alternates assigning each point to its nearest centre and moving each centre to the mean of its points. Neither step can raise the sum, so it stops at a *local minimum*, which need not be the best available. Because every point goes to its nearest centre, each cluster is a convex region of space, and because the criterion is a sum of squared distances, it favours clusters of similar spread. Whether the result is "the clusters" depends on whether the data contain groups of that kind.

The number $k$ must be chosen as well, and the usual tool is the *silhouette*. For point $i$, let $a(i)$ be its mean distance to the other points in its own cluster and $b(i)$ its mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster. The point's silhouette is

$$
s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i), b(i)\}},
$$

which is near 1 when the point sits tightly inside a well-separated cluster, near 0 when it lies on a boundary, and negative when it is closer to another cluster than its own. The silhouette of a clustering is the average over points, and the usual rule picks the $k$ with the largest value.

To judge a clustering against known groups, this chapter uses the *adjusted Rand index* (ARI). The Rand index is the fraction of pairs of points on which two partitions agree, both placing the pair together or both apart; the adjusted version subtracts the agreement expected by chance and rescales, so that 1 means the partitions are identical and 0 means agreement no better than chance.

#### Four shapes, from ideal to empty

Four simulated two-dimensional shapes, each of 1,000 points and each drawn ten times, test both tools. The first is what k-means looks for: four round blobs of unit variance with centres 8 apart. The second has no structure at all: points uniform on the unit square. The third has one large diffuse group of 900 points with standard deviation 1.5 and two small tight groups of 50 with standard deviation 0.3. The fourth has two long parallel groups of 500, stretched 12 times along one axis and 3 apart along the other. For each shape the code records which $k$ the silhouette picks across the ten draws, the best silhouette, and the ARI of k-means at the true $k$.

```python
from standarderror.ml import around

r = around.kmeans_shapes()      # ten draws of each shape
for name, v in r.items():
    ari = "  -  " if v["ari"] is None else f"{v['ari']:.3f}"
    print(f"{name:10s} silhouette picks k = {sorted(set(v['picks']))}, "
          f"best silhouette {v['silhouette']:.3f}, ARI at true k {ari}")
```

```text
separated  silhouette picks k = [4], best silhouette 0.759, ARI at true k 1.000
uniform    silhouette picks k = [4], best silhouette 0.403, ARI at true k   -  
unequal    silhouette picks k = [8, 9, 10], best silhouette 0.379, ARI at true k 0.086
elongated  silhouette picks k = [2], best silhouette 0.487, ARI at true k 0.001
```

On the separated blobs everything works. The silhouette chooses $k = 4$ in all ten draws, and k-means recovers the blobs with an ARI of 1.00. The figure below shows the partitions k-means produces on one draw of each shape.

### The silhouette rewards a tidy partition of noise


On uniform noise there is nothing to find, and both tools find something anyway. k-means cuts the square into $k$ pieces, as it cuts anything; the criterion is defined for any data and always has a minimum. The silhouette then chooses $k = 4$ in all ten draws, with a mean best score of 0.40. Four quadrants of a square are compact, so $a(i)$ is small, and their boundaries are short, so few points sit near another cluster. By the silhouette's arithmetic they are decent clusters, although they only divide a region of uniform density.

A score of 0.40 means little until it is compared with data that do have classes. The digits data are 1,797 images of handwritten digits in ten classes, clustered here on their raw pixel values. The code scans $k$ for the best silhouette, reports the ARI against the true digits at $k = 10$, and then runs k-means ten times with a single start each.

```python
d = around.kmeans_digits()
best = max(d["scan"], key=lambda t: t["silhouette"])
print(f"digits: silhouette peaks at k = {best['k']} "
      f"({best['silhouette']:.3f}); ARI at k = 10: {d['ari10']:.3f}")
print("one start per seed, ARI: " + " ".join(
    f"{x['ari']:.2f}" for x in d["single"]))
```

```text
digits: silhouette peaks at k = 9 (0.189); ARI at k = 10: 0.666
one start per seed, ARI: 0.62 0.67 0.66 0.67 0.66 0.65 0.57 0.67 0.68 0.57
```

On the digits, ten classes written by different people, the silhouette peaks at $k = 9$ with a score of 0.19, less than half its value on the noise. The suggested number of clusters is about right, but the score on its own would be read as weak structure. Compared across data sets, the silhouette would call the empty square more clustered than the handwriting. The figure below shows the silhouette against $k$ for the separated blobs, the noise and the digits.

![Uniform noise scores 0.40 at its best k; the digits never pass 0.19.](ml132-f1-silhouette.png){alt="Curves of silhouette against k: the separated blobs peak high at 4; uniform noise sits around 0.4; the digits curve is lowest, under 0.2 at every k."}

The lesson is that a silhouette value has no meaning without a reference for what it would be if there were no clusters. That is the idea behind the *gap statistic* of Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie: compute the clustering criterion on the real data and on reference data drawn uniformly over the same range, and claim clusters only when the real data beat the reference by more than the reference's own variability. In this section the reference is the second row of the table above, and it scores 0.40.

### Real groups of the wrong shape are cut wrongly


The last two shapes do contain groups, but not of the shape k-means draws. With one large diffuse group and two small tight ones, k-means at the true $k = 3$ scores an ARI of 0.09. The mechanism is in the criterion. The large group, with its wide spread, contributes most of the within-cluster sum of squares, so the sum falls most when centres are spent slicing that group into pieces. Giving each tight small group its own centre would reduce the sum far less. The figure of the four shapes shows the result: the large group cut into slices, the two small groups sharing a colour. The silhouette makes matters worse, choosing between 8 and 10 clusters across the ten draws.

With two long parallel groups, the silhouette picks the correct $k = 2$ in every draw, and k-means scores an ARI of 0.00. Each group is stretched twelve times as far along its length as across it, and the two lie only 3 apart. A cluster that follows one band has a large sum of squares, because its points spread far along the band; splitting the plane across the bands into a left and a right half gives two rounder pieces with a smaller sum. So the partition k-means settles on cuts both groups in half across their length instead of separating them. The answer has the right number of clusters and the wrong clusters.

In both cases the algorithm did what it promises: convex regions of similar spread around $k$ centres. When the true groups are not shaped like that, it returns its own shapes, and nothing in its output signals the mismatch. The table below collects the results.

![The silhouette was right about k in two cases out of four, and k-means was right about the groups in one.](ml132-f2-table.png){alt="A five-row table: separated blobs recovered; uniform noise given four clusters with a fairly high silhouette; the unequal and elongated shapes recovered at chance level."}

### The answer depends on where k-means starts


Because k-means stops at a local minimum, the answer depends on where the centres start. On the digits, ten runs with a single random start each gave ARIs from 0.57 to 0.68. Running ten starts and keeping the one with the smallest within-cluster sum of squares, the setting used elsewhere in this section, gave 0.67. The groups a single run reports are partly an accident of its seed. This is the clustering counterpart of Section 1.2, where networks that differed only in their random seed reached nearly the same accuracy and disagreed on many individual predictions.

Keeping the start with the smallest sum of squares is the right rule for k-means' own objective, and it is worth seeing how loosely that objective tracks the digits' real classes. Ranked by sum of squares, the best of the ten single starts agreed with the true digits at an ARI of 0.66; the start that agreed best, at 0.68, ranked 8 of 10 by sum of squares. A lower sum of squares is a better k-means solution, not a better account of the handwriting.

k-means, like the nearest-neighbour methods, rests on distances between rows, and on the digits those distances are taken across every pixel; whether distances in many dimensions still mean anything is the subject of Section 18.1. Within this chapter, the next step is to relax k-means' fixed shapes and its heuristic choice of k at once: Section 17.2 turns to a probability model whose components can be ellipses of different sizes, with k chosen by likelihood.

## The BIC Counts Gaussians, Not Clusters

<!-- source: ml-57-bic; hero: ml157-hero.png -->


Section 17.1 above found that k-means returns k pieces whatever the data, and that the silhouette chose four clusters for uniform noise when asked to choose k. A Gaussian mixture looks like the principled alternative. It is a probability model, so the number of groups can be chosen by a likelihood-based criterion rather than a heuristic, and the standard criterion is the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Model-based clustering, as Fraley and Raftery set it out, made the BIC the usual way to pick the number of components, and practice reads that number as the number of clusters: *the BIC tells you how many clusters a Gaussian mixture should have*. Anyone who reports "the data contain k segments" from a mixture relies on it. If it is false, the reported number of groups is a property of the clusters' shape and of the sample size.

This section defines the mixture model, the BIC and the alternative criterion ICL; fits mixtures to single clusters of known, non-Gaussian shape as the sample grows; repeats the test on three separated clusters and on the digits; and finally asks how much the fitting algorithm's starting point decides.

### Gaussian mixtures and the criteria for choosing k


A *Gaussian mixture* with $k$ components models the density of the data as

$$
f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j),
$$

with mixing weights $\pi_j$ that sum to one, means $\mu_j$ and covariance matrices $\Sigma_j$. The covariances may be *full*, allowing each component to be an ellipse at any angle, or *diagonal*, allowing only axis-aligned ellipses. The parameters are fitted by maximum likelihood with the *expectation–maximisation* (EM) algorithm, which alternates between computing each point's *responsibilities*, the posterior probabilities that it came from each component, and re-estimating the parameters from those responsibilities. Assigning each point to its most responsible component turns the mixture into a clustering.

Adding components never lowers the maximised likelihood, so the likelihood alone always prefers more. The *Bayesian information criterion*, from Schwarz in 1978, adds a penalty for the number of free parameters $p$:

$$
\mathrm{BIC} = -2\log \hat L + p \log n,
$$

where $\hat L$ is the maximised likelihood and $n$ the number of points. The $k$ with the smallest BIC is chosen. The penalty grows with the logarithm of $n$, while the log-likelihood is a sum over points and so grows in proportion to $n$; this asymmetry will matter below. A difference in BIC between two models approximates twice the logarithm of the *Bayes factor* between them. Kass and Raftery's scale calls a BIC difference above 10 very strong evidence.

The *integrated completed likelihood* (ICL), proposed by Biernacki, Celeux and Govaert in 2000, adds to the BIC a charge for how uncertain the assignment of points to components is: roughly, twice the entropy of the responsibilities. Components that overlap heavily leave many points with split responsibilities and are expensive; components sitting on separate clusters assign each point confidently and cost almost nothing extra. ICL is designed to count clusters rather than components.

### A non-Gaussian cluster earns more components as the data grow


The test needs clusters whose number is known and whose shape is not Gaussian, because real clusters seldom are. The simplest is a single two-dimensional cluster: a uniform square, or a heavy-tailed blob drawn from a Student t distribution with three degrees of freedom, with a Gaussian blob as the control. The number of clusters is one in every case. The code fits mixtures with one to ten components to a uniform square at two sample sizes and prints the chosen $k$ and how much worse a single component scores.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import unsup

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for n in (200, 1000):
    X = rng.random((n, 2))                 # one uniform square
    rows = unsup._bic_icl(X, range(1, 11))
    best = min(rows, key=lambda r: r["bic"])
    print(f"n={n:5d}  BIC picks k={best['k']}  "
          f"(BIC at k=1 minus best: "
          f"{rows[0]['bic'] - best['bic']:.1f})")
```

```text
n=  200  BIC picks k=4  (BIC at k=1 minus best: 9.8)
n= 1000  BIC picks k=7  (BIC at k=1 minus best: 242.6)
```

Across the full experiment, for the Gaussian blob the BIC chose one component at every size, from 200 points to 20,000. For the uniform square it chose 2 at 200 points, 7 at 1,000, 14 at 5,000 and 18 at 20,000. For the heavy-tailed cluster it chose 2, 3, 4 and 9.

None of this is a bug in the BIC. A square is not a mixture of any finite number of Gaussians, so there is no true $k$ for the BIC to find. Its flat top and sharp edges are better described by a patchwork of overlapping components. The gain in log-likelihood from each extra patch is a sum over points, so it grows in proportion to the number of points, while the BIC's penalty for the patch's parameters grows only with the logarithm of that number. With enough data every departure from Gaussian shape earns more components, without limit. When the data really are a Gaussian mixture, as for the control, the BIC can settle on the right number; when they are not, it counts Gaussians, and the number it reports for a non-Gaussian cluster depends on the cluster's shape and on how much data there is. The figure below shows the chosen $k$ climbing with the sample.

![The number of components chosen for one cluster as the sample grows. For one uniform square the BIC chose 2 components at 200 points and 18 at 20,000.](ml157-f0-growth.png){alt="Three solid lines. The Gaussian stays at 1; the uniform square and the heavy-tailed cluster climb with the number of points. The dotted ICL lines stay at 1 or 2."}

#### The choices were decisive

One might hope the extra components win by small margins. From 1,000 points on they did not. For the square, the chosen $k$ beat the next-best $k$ by 57 BIC units at 1,000 points, 21 at 5,000 and 54 at 20,000, and beat a single component by 230, 2,154 and 9,824. For the heavy-tailed blob the margins over the next-best $k$ were 15, 12, 45 and 20. All of these are above Kass and Raftery's threshold for very strong evidence.

At 200 points the square's choice was not decisive: $k = 2$ beat $k = 1$ by 6.6, and a different draw of 200 points, the one in the code above, chose another $k$. So an analyst who checks that the BIC's choice is decisive, and has a few thousand points, will find that it is, and will be no closer to the number of clusters.

#### ICL stays near the number of clusters

ICL chose one component for the square at every size, and one or two for the heavy-tailed blob (the dotted lines in the figure above): components tiling a single cluster overlap heavily, and ICL's charge outweighs the likelihood they gain.

### Separated clusters and real data


With three separated uniform squares of 500 points each, the BIC chose 9 components, and its clustering agreed with the squares at an adjusted Rand index (ARI) of 0.42; as in Section 17.1 above, the ARI is 1 for a partition identical to the truth and near 0 for one unrelated to it. Each square had been carved into pieces. ICL chose 3.

A common repair, from Baudry and colleagues in 2010, keeps the BIC's components and merges them hierarchically into clusters. It separates the two questions the BIC was being asked to answer at once: how to describe the density, which needs many components, and how many groups the density has, which may need few.

The digits show the same dependence in real data. Reduced to ten principal components and fitted with full covariance matrices, the BIC, tried at every $k$ from 2 to 30, chose 11 components, one more than the ten classes, with an ARI of 0.75 (at $k = 10$, 0.71). Restricted to diagonal covariances, components that cannot tilt, it chose 28, with an ARI of 0.44; the same diagonal model at $k = 10$ scored 0.65. Neither 11 nor 28 is the number of digit classes in any deeper sense than the other. Each is the number of components of the allowed shape that the data can pay for. The table below collects the three cases.

![The BIC and ICL on three separated squares and on the digits with two covariance shapes. With diagonal covariances and k fixed at 10, the digits model scored an ARI of 0.65.](ml157-f1-table.png){alt="A three-row table. The BIC and ICL disagree on the squares and agree on the digits, where the count depends on the covariance shape."}

### The starting point of EM does much of the work


Every $k$ on these curves came from EM, which climbs to a local maximum of the likelihood from wherever it starts. scikit-learn's `GaussianMixture` runs one start unless told otherwise, and by default initialises the responsibilities from a k-means clustering. The experiment used fifteen overlapping clusters, with a start at the true centres as the reference. A run *reached* the reference if its final log-likelihood was within 0.005 per point of the reference's.

The default start from k-means reached the reference in 90% of sixty single runs, with a median ARI of 0.85 against 0.88 for the reference. Starting from randomly chosen data points as means reached it in 5%, with a median ARI of 0.67 and a worst run at 0.49, where the worst k-means start still scored 0.74. Starting from random responsibilities reached it in none, with a median ARI of 0.21. The code below repeats the comparison for k-means and random-responsibility starts.

```python
em = unsup.em_starts(runs=10)
for init in ("kmeans", "random"):
    print(f"{init:7s} reached {em[init]['reached']:.0%}, "
          f"median ARI {em[init]['ari_median']:.2f}")
```

```text
kmeans  reached 80%, median ARI 0.86
random  reached 0%, median ARI 0.21
```

The code runs ten starts of each kind, the first ten of the sixty, so its shares are coarser than the full experiment's.

So a mixture's "maximum likelihood" is the maximum near where k-means put it. That is usually a good place, but it means a BIC curve is a curve of local optima: a $k$ that EM happens to fit badly looks worse than it is, and the chosen $k$ can move with it. Running several starts per $k$ and checking that the best likelihood repeats is cheap insurance. The table below summarises the starts.

![Agreement with the reference fit for each kind of EM start. scikit-learn's GaussianMixture fits one start unless n_init is raised.](ml157-f2-em.png){alt="A five-row table. The k-means starts reach the reference in about 90% of runs; random starts in 5% or none."}

The BIC answered a narrower question than the one asked of it: how many Gaussian components of a given shape the data can afford. The next section turns to a method that defines a cluster by density rather than by a parametric shape, and asks what its single radius setting decides.

## DBSCAN's Radius Is a Density, and It Gets Only One

<!-- source: ml-58-dbscan; hero: ml158-hero.png -->


Both methods so far describe a cluster by a shape and need to be given, or to choose, a number of them. k-means cuts the data into k convex pieces, which is why Section 17.1 found it cutting two parallel groups crosswise; a Gaussian mixture describes clusters as ellipses, which is why Section 17.2 found the BIC tiling a single square with many of them. Density-based clustering starts from a different definition: a cluster is a region where points are packed closely, separated from other such regions by sparse space. DBSCAN, the best-known density method, carries a reputation that follows from that definition, and it is the sentence under test in this section: *DBSCAN finds clusters of any shape without being told how many*. DBSCAN does not take k. It does take a radius, and if the radius decides the answer as much as k decides a k-means answer, then the method has moved the choice rather than removed it, and a clustering reported at one radius is a statement about that radius.

This section defines DBSCAN and how its results are scored, then measures on two half-moons how wide the window of good radii is and whether the standard heuristic finds it. Next it places clusters of different densities side by side, where a single radius fails, and compares HDBSCAN, which needs no radius. Finally it applies both to the handwritten digits.

### Clusters as dense regions


DBSCAN (density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise) was introduced by Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu in 1996 and has been widely used since. It makes the definition of a cluster operational with two settings, a radius `eps` and a count `min_samples`:

- A **core point** has at least `min_samples` points, itself included, within distance `eps`.
- Core points within `eps` of each other belong to the same cluster, and so, by chaining, does every core point reachable through a sequence of such steps.
- A non-core point within `eps` of a core point joins that core point's cluster as a **border point**.
- Anything not reachable from a core point is **noise** and belongs to no cluster.

Together, `eps` and `min_samples` set a density threshold: a point is in a cluster's interior if its neighbourhood of radius `eps` holds at least `min_samples` points. The number of clusters is whatever number of connected dense regions results.

Agreement with the true groups is measured, as in the previous two sections, by the adjusted Rand index (ARI). Noise needs a convention. Here every noise point is counted as its own singleton cluster, so that calling a whole cluster noise costs what it should. (If noise were counted as one more cluster, a cluster's worth of discarded points could earn a high score by forming a "cluster" of its own.) The "best" radius for any data set below is chosen with the true labels, which a user never has. It is the most DBSCAN can do, not what it will do.

### Any shape, at the right radius


Two interlocking half-moons, 1,000 points from scikit-learn's `make_moons`, are the textbook case for density clustering, and the textbook is right. The block below sweeps the radius and reports the best score, the window of radii scoring at least 0.9, the score at a heuristic radius, and k-means.

```python
from standarderror.ml import unsup

m = unsup.dbscan_moons()            # 1,000 points, two half-moons
lo, hi = m["window"]
print(f"best {m['best']:.2f}; radii scoring 0.9+: {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}")
print(f"knee radius {m['knee']:.3f} scores {m['knee_score']:.2f}; "
      f"k-means scores {m['kmeans_ari']:.2f}")
```

```text
best 1.00; radii scoring 0.9+: 0.071 to 0.206
knee radius 0.061 scores 0.57; k-means scores 0.25
```

k-means, asked for two clusters, cut the moons in half and scored an ARI of 0.25: its pieces are convex, and the moons are not. DBSCAN scored 0.95 or more at every radius between 0.071 and 0.206, and separated the moons perfectly from 0.116 to 0.206. Outside that window it fails in two opposite ways. Smaller radii set the density threshold too high for the moons' own sparser stretches, so each moon shatters into fragments and noise. Larger radii let the chain of core points bridge the narrow gap where the two moons come close, and the moons join into one cluster.

#### Finding the window with the k-distance plot

The good window is about a factor of 2.9 wide, the perfect one 1.8, and something has to find it without labels. Ester and colleagues suggested the *k-distance plot*: compute every point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour, sort these distances, and read the radius off the *knee* of the curve, where the distances start to climb steeply. The reasoning is that points inside clusters have small k-distances and noise points large ones, so the knee marks the boundary between them. Here the knee was found geometrically, as the point on the sorted curve farthest below the straight chord joining its ends, using each point's fourth-nearest other point.

The knee was at 0.061, just below the window, and that radius scored 0.57. That miss belonged to this draw of the moons. Section 22.2, which reruns single-draw results from across the book on fresh data, repeated this on five other draws of the moons, and the knee fell inside the good window every time, scoring 0.80 to 0.96. The heuristic is a reasonable start on data like the moons, and, as the digits below show, it can still land in the wrong place.

#### The second setting moves the window

The other setting, `min_samples`, moves the window more than it widens it. With `min_samples` at 3 or 5 the radii scoring at least 0.9 ran from 0.071 to 0.206; at 10, they began at 0.081; at 20, they ran from 0.106 to 0.247; at 40, from 0.151 to 0.308. Requiring more neighbours raises the density a core point needs, so the same moons need a larger radius to reach it. The two settings act roughly as one density threshold written two ways, and fixing either leaves the other to be tuned. scikit-learn's defaults, a radius of 0.5 with 5 points, put both moons into one cluster and scored 0.00. The default radius is a number, not a property of anyone's data; it depends on the units in which distances happen to be measured.

Schubert and colleagues, revisiting DBSCAN in 2017, give rules of thumb for checking a result. If the largest cluster holds more than 20 to 50% of the clustered points, reduce the radius or use a hierarchical method such as HDBSCAN. Noise should usually be between 1 and 30% of the points. And `min_samples` should be about twice the number of dimensions.

### One radius cannot serve two densities


A single radius with a fixed `min_samples` is a single density threshold, and real clusters differ in density. The second simulation makes that difference controllable. Two tight clusters of 300 points, each with standard deviation 0.2, sit 1.4 apart. A third cluster of 300 points, at distance 6 from them, has a spread that grows from 0.2, the same as the others, to 1.6, eight times theirs. In two dimensions eight times the spread means about 64 times lower density, since the same number of points occupies an area that grows with the square of the spread.

This creates a dilemma that no radius resolves. A radius small enough to keep the tight pair apart is too small for the wide cluster's sparse points to qualify as core points, so the wide cluster dissolves into noise. A radius large enough to hold the wide cluster together lets the dense region of the tight pair bridge the gap between them, and the pair merges. The block below prints, for the equal and the eightfold spread, how many of the 150 radii tried scored at least 0.9, the best score, and HDBSCAN's score.

```python
for d in unsup.dbscan_densities(spreads=(0.2, 1.6)):
    print(f"spread {d['ratio']:.0f}x: {d['good_radii']:3d} good radii, "
          f"best {d['best']:.3f}, HDBSCAN {d['hdbscan']:.3f}")
```

```text
spread 1x:  36 good radii, best 0.998, HDBSCAN 0.992
spread 8x:   0 good radii, best 0.729, HDBSCAN 0.884
```

With equal spreads, 36 of the 150 radii scored at least 0.9. As the third cluster widened through the five spreads tried, the count fell to 32, 7, 1 and 0. Five further draws of the data gave 0 or 1 good radii at sixfold spread and none at eightfold. At eightfold spread the best radius in the first draw scored 0.73. It kept the tight pair apart by declaring 77% of the wide cluster noise, which is 26% of all the points; the other draws' best radii scored 0.73 to 0.85. The number of clusters was never the problem; the single density was. Schubert and colleagues' noise rule would have accepted that answer, since a quarter of the points is inside their range of 1 to 30%. The figure below plots the score against the radius as the third cluster widens.

With equal spreads a wide band of radii scores near 1; as the third cluster widens the band shrinks, and at eightfold no radius reaches 0.9."}

#### HDBSCAN builds every radius at once

HDBSCAN, from Campello, Moulavi and Sander in 2013, removes the single radius. Conceptually, it builds DBSCAN's clusters at every radius at once, which gives a hierarchy: as the radius shrinks, clusters split and shed points as noise. It then keeps the clusters that persist over the widest range of densities, subject to a *minimum cluster size*, which replaces the radius as the setting the user chooses (25 here). Because different clusters can be selected at different levels of the hierarchy, a dense cluster and a sparse one can both survive.

In the first draw HDBSCAN scored 0.88 to 1.00 across the five spreads, and in the other five draws 0.82 to 0.89 at eightfold spread. It was better than any single radius at large contrasts, and not perfect either. The table below collects the counts and scores.

![Good radii, best DBSCAN score and HDBSCAN score at each spread of the third cluster. Scores are adjusted Rand indices with every noise point its own singleton.](ml158-f1-table.png){alt="A five-row table. The count of good radii falls to 0; HDBSCAN stays above 0.85."}

### The digits


In the digits' 64 pixel dimensions, the best of 60 radii between 5 and 40, chosen with the labels, scored 0.71 at a radius of 21.2. It did so by producing 20 clusters and calling 17% of the images noise: the ten classes broken into denser and sparser pieces. The knee of the k-distance curve was at 25.2. At that radius DBSCAN put 96% of the images into a single cluster and scored 0.00: the radius that looked natural on the curve was just past the point where the classes merge. The two radii differ by 19%, a margin that a reading of a sorted-distance curve is unlikely to resolve reliably.

Schubert and colleagues' largest-cluster rule would have caught the knee's answer, since one cluster held far more than half the data. But their suggested `min_samples` for 64 dimensions is 128, not 5, so this test departs from their guidance.

HDBSCAN did not rescue the digits. It scored 0.38 and called 54% of them noise. (Section 16.2 quoted 0.17 for the same clustering because it counted noise as one more cluster; counting noise points as singletons gives the higher figure.) Section 18.1 found that the digits' nearest neighbours behave like those of uniform data in only about eight dimensions, so the concentration of distances that afflicts truly high-dimensional data is not the obvious culprit; why density estimates fail on the raw pixels was not measured. Section 16.2 also found that the same HDBSCAN applied to a t-SNE picture of the digits recovered them well, because the picture had already turned neighbourhoods into dense, separated blobs.

DBSCAN's freedom from k, then, is real, but it is paid for with a density threshold that must be right and must be the same everywhere. The last section of this chapter returns to the question that DBSCAN set aside and that k-means and the mixture answered badly, how many clusters there are, and tests the rule that picks the answer which reproduces best.

## The Most Stable k Is Not the Number of Clusters

<!-- source: ml-60-stability; hero: ml160-hero.png -->


Choosing the number of clusters is the question every clustering analysis has to answer, and this chapter has shown two popular answers failing. Section 17.1 found the silhouette choosing four clusters in uniform noise, and Section 17.2 found the BIC choosing as many Gaussian components as a cluster's shape could pay for; DBSCAN, in Section 17.3, avoided the question only by asking for a density instead. A third idea sounds more like science than either: a real structure should reproduce. Cluster two random subsamples of the data, compare the two clusterings on the points they share, and repeat; the right k is the one whose answer comes back most consistently. Resampling stability is now a widely used check, and the sentence under test is the rule itself: *the right number of clusters is the one whose clustering is most stable under resampling*. An analyst who reports "k = 4 was the most stable solution" as evidence that four groups exist is relying on it. If it is false, stability certifies groups in data that have none.

This section first defines the stability score and the comparison method, the gap statistic. It then applies both to two shapes with no clusters at all, explains with a theoretical result why stability can be high there, and turns to shapes where clusters do exist, including a two-level hierarchy. It then tries both on the handwritten digits. A final subsection draws together what the methods without labels, in this chapter and on either side of it, measured.

### Two ways to choose k


The stability score used here works as follows. Draw two random subsamples, each containing 80% of the points; run k-means with $k$ clusters on each; restrict both clusterings to the points the two subsamples share; and measure their agreement with the adjusted Rand index (ARI) of Section 17.1, here comparing two clusterings with each other rather than one with the truth. Repeat for 20 pairs of subsamples (10 for the digits) and average. The ARI is invariant to how clusters are numbered, so two runs that find the same groups under different labels agree perfectly. The k with the highest mean agreement is chosen.

Ben-Hur, Elisseeff and Guyon proposed a version of this in 2002. Their own rule looks at the whole distribution of agreements across pairs and picks the largest k whose agreements are still concentrated near 1; the version tested here takes the k with the highest mean agreement.

The comparison is the *gap statistic* of Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, whose idea Section 17.1 introduced: judge a clustering criterion against its value on reference data with no clusters. Formally, let $W_k$ be the total within-cluster dispersion of a k-means clustering with $k$ clusters, the sum over clusters of squared distances of points to their cluster centre. $W_k$ always falls as $k$ grows, so its value alone cannot choose $k$. The gap statistic compares it with what the same procedure gives on reference data drawn uniformly over the same region, which by construction has no clusters:

$$
\mathrm{Gap}(k) = \mathbb{E}^{*}\!\left[\log W_k^{*}\right] - \log W_k,
$$

where the expectation is estimated by averaging over several reference data sets (10 here, 5 for the digits). The rule chooses the smallest $k$ such that $\mathrm{Gap}(k) \ge \mathrm{Gap}(k+1) - s_{k+1}$, where $s_{k+1}$ is the standard deviation of $\log W_{k+1}^{*}$ across the $B$ reference sets, multiplied by $\sqrt{1 + 1/B}$ to allow for the error in the simulated average: stop when one more cluster no longer improves the gap by more than its own noise. Because $\mathrm{Gap}(1)$ is defined, the gap statistic, unlike stability, can answer $k = 1$.

The block below applies both to one elongated Gaussian, a single cloud of 1,000 points with standard deviations 3 and 1, which has no clusters.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import unsup

X = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(1000, 2)) * [3, 1]
for k in (2, 3, 5):                 # one elongated Gaussian, no clusters
    print(f"k={k}  stability {unsup.stability(X, k):.3f}")
g = unsup.gap(X)
print("gap statistic chooses", g["rule"])
```

```text
k=2  stability 0.920
k=3  stability 0.899
k=5  stability 0.636
gap statistic chooses 1
```

### Stability is high where there are no clusters


On the elongated Gaussian, a single cloud three times longer than it is wide, $k = 2$ had a stability of 0.92: two random subsamples split the cloud at almost the same place nearly every time. Stability was 0.90 at $k = 3$ and 0.87 at 4, then dropped, so the highest-mean rule chose 2. Ben-Hur and colleagues' own rule would probably have accepted 3 or 4, which is no better. On a uniform square of 1,000 points the most stable $k$ was 4, at 0.94, the same four that the silhouette chose in Section 17.1. Neither shape has any clusters.

#### Why: stability measures the uniqueness of the optimum

Ben-David, von Luxburg and Pál explained why in 2006. Stability measures whether the clustering objective has a unique best answer on the distribution the data come from. If it does, then as the sample grows every subsample's clustering converges to that same answer, and two subsamples agree almost perfectly, whether or not the answer corresponds to real groups. An elongated cloud has a unique best two-way cut for the k-means objective, across its long axis at the centre, so every subsample cuts there. A square has a unique best four-way cut, into quadrants. Both are therefore highly reproducible.

Conversely, where the objective has several equally good answers, as it would for a perfectly round cloud, which can be cut in two along any diameter, different subsamples settle on different answers and stability is low, whether or not clusters exist. Reproducibility is a property of the method on the data, telling us whether its optimum is unique; it is not evidence that the groups are real. And since stability needs at least two clusters to compare, it can never answer that there is only one: at $k = 1$ every point is in the same cluster in both subsamples, so agreement is trivially perfect and carries no information. The figure below shows the stability curves for the four simulated shapes.

![Mean stability against k for four simulated shapes. One elongated Gaussian with no clusters was stable at 0.92 for k = 2.](ml160-f0-stability.png){alt="Four curves. The elongated Gaussian peaks at k = 2, the uniform square at 4, the three blobs at 3 with 1.0, and the two pairs at 2 and 4, both near 1."}

### Where clusters exist


On three well-separated unit Gaussian blobs, centred at (0,0), (6,0) and (3,5), stability did its job: $k = 3$ reproduced perfectly (1.00), and every other $k$ less so. Here the unique optimum coincides with the real groups.

The fourth shape is a hierarchy: four unit blobs on a line at $x$ = 0, 4, 20 and 24, forming two pairs that are far apart, with the two blobs within each pair close. Here $k = 2$ reproduced perfectly (1.00) and $k = 4$ almost as well (0.99). Both answers are right, at different scales, and the rule picked the coarser one because merging the close blobs left nothing to disagree about. A hierarchy is not a mistake, but a single "most stable k" hides it.

The gap statistic chose one cluster for the elongated Gaussian and for the square, and three for the blobs. On the two pairs its stopping rule chose 2, and its largest gap was at 4, so it too registered both levels. Of the two methods, only the gap could say "no clusters", because only it compares the data with data that have none. The table below collects both methods' choices.

![The number of clusters chosen by stability and by the gap statistic for each shape and the digits. Stability at k = 1 is trivially perfect and so uninformative: it can never choose 'no clusters'.](ml160-f1-table.png){alt="A five-row table. ### The digits


On the 1,797 digits, k-means stability rose from 0.29 at $k = 2$ to a peak of 0.869 at $k = 10$, the number of classes, with $k = 9$ and 11 within 0.013 of it. It is a fragile success. With two other resampling seeds the peak moved to $k = 9$ (0.883 and 0.930). Three neighbouring answers are indistinguishable on this evidence, and Section 17.1 found k-means at $k = 10$ agreeing with the digit classes only partly, so even the right number of clusters does not imply the right clusters.

The gap statistic, computed from 1 to 15, rose at every step by more than the next step's standard error, so its rule found no stopping point and fell back to 15, the largest $k$ tried. At the last step the margin was about 0.001, with five reference sets. It would presumably have gone on. In 64 dimensions, against uniform reference data in a box, every extra centre plausibly explains structure that uniform data lack, whether that structure is the ten digit classes or the many ways each digit is written. The gap statistic was built to detect clustering against a null of none, and on data this far from uniform it detects only that.

### What the methods without labels measured


Part VI of this book takes the labels away, and five of its sections test an unsupervised method on data with a known answer: Section 16.2 before this chapter, Sections 17.2 to 17.4 of this chapter, and Section 18.2 after it. A t-SNE picture kept each point's neighbours and drew cluster sizes and gaps that were not in the data (Section 16.2). The BIC counted the Gaussians a cluster's shape required, more of them the more data it saw (Section 17.2). DBSCAN found any shape at one density and none when the densities differed sixfold or more, and its standard radius heuristic missed on the digits and in one of six draws of the half-moons (Section 17.3). An anomaly detector ranked well and flagged whatever share it was told to; local scores failed for grouped anomalies, and the isolation forest for anomalies hidden in a few of many features (Section 18.2). And the most reproducible clustering was reproducible because its answer was unique, not because the clusters existed (this section). The k-means results of Section 17.1, convex pieces of similar spread on any data, belong to the same list.

Each method answered a narrower question than the one it is used for: who is near whom, how many Gaussians, how dense, how unusual in these features, how unique the optimum. The common repair was the one this book has relied on since Section 1.1, data with a known answer: simulate structure whose truth you know, including the case of no structure at all, and see whether the method can tell. Chapter 18 turns from groups of points to single points: whether distances between rows still mean anything in many dimensions, and what an anomaly detector's output actually is.

## In practice

The four sections lead to the same habits, whichever method is used. They are grouped here by what they guard against.

### Test every score and every rule on data with no clusters

- **Compare every clustering score with structureless data.** Draw each column uniformly between its minimum and maximum, independently of the others, cluster that reference the same way, and report the real score beside the reference score. A silhouette is only evidence of clusters if it clearly beats the reference.
- **Test any rule for choosing k on data with no clusters first.** A single Gaussian cloud and a uniform square of the same scale as your data are a minimum. If the rule picks k greater than one there, its choice on your data is not evidence of clusters.
- **Compare with a null that has no structure.** Use a method that can answer $k = 1$, such as the gap statistic, or rerun the whole procedure on data with the structure destroyed, for example by shuffling each column independently.
- **Do not compare silhouettes across data sets as a measure of how clustered each is.** In Section 17.1 the handwritten digits scored less than half what uniform noise did.

### Read each answer as what the method measures

- **Do not treat the lowest within-cluster sum of squares as the best account of the data.** It answers k-means' question, which may not be yours.
- **Read the BIC's k as a number of components, not clusters.** Report it as "the number of Gaussian components of this covariance shape the data support", and say which covariance shape was allowed.
- **Do not treat a decisive BIC margin as evidence of clusters.** With thousands of points, margins far above 10 arise from non-Gaussian shape alone.
- **Treat `eps` as a declared density.** Choosing a DBSCAN radius, together with `min_samples`, is choosing how dense a region must be to count as a cluster. Report both with every result, and never rely on scikit-learn's default radius.
- **Report stability as a property of the method's answer.** "k-means at k = 4 is reproducible" is what stability shows; "the data contain four groups" is not.

### Match the method to the shapes and densities the data may have

- **Ask whether the clusters you expect are round and of similar spread.** If they could be of very different sizes or elongated, k-means will slice them by its own criterion; consider a method with other assumptions and check its assumptions too.
- **For a number of clusters from a mixture, use a criterion that charges for overlap, or merge.** Compute ICL alongside the BIC, or merge the BIC's components hierarchically into clusters, and report both counts if they differ.
- **When clusters may differ in density, use a method that does not need one radius.** HDBSCAN handled a large density contrast better than any single radius, though not perfectly; its minimum cluster size is then the setting to vary.

### Vary what the answer depends on

- **Use several starts and look at what changes.** Rerun k-means with different seeds and count how many rows change cluster. Report the clustering as unstable if many do.
- **Fit several EM starts per k.** Raise `n_init` above its default of one, use k-means initialisation, and check that the best likelihood repeats before reading the BIC curve.
- **Check whether the chosen k grows with the sample.** Refit on a subsample; if the chosen k falls, the extra components were describing shape, not groups.
- **Sweep the radius and watch what changes.** Run DBSCAN at the chosen radius, half of it and double it, and record the number of clusters, the size of the largest and the noise share. If they change a lot, the clustering is a statement about the radius.
- **Do not trust the k-distance knee by itself.** It missed the good window once on the moons and on the digits landed just past the point where every class merged. Use it as a starting point for the sweep.
- **Check the choice of k across resampling seeds.** On the digits the most stable k moved with the seed; report the range of k that are indistinguishable, not a single winner.

### Check the result before reporting it

- **Check the largest cluster and the noise share.** Apply Schubert and colleagues' rules: a largest cluster above 20 to 50% of the clustered points or noise outside 1 to 30% calls for another look. Remember that a passing noise share can still hide a discarded cluster.
- **Look for more than one stable k.** When two values of k are both nearly perfectly stable, the data may have a hierarchy; report both levels instead of the coarser one alone.

## Limits of the evidence

All four sections drew their simulated shapes in two dimensions, so that the shapes could be seen and each mechanism isolated, and went beyond two dimensions only on the digits. In many dimensions the same failures happen and cannot be seen, which is the argument for checking clusters against a null reference rather than by eye. Some effects plausibly change with dimension in ways that were not measured. A non-Gaussian cluster can depart from Gaussian shape in more ways, so the number of components the BIC can justify plausibly grows faster. How the density contrast that defeats a single DBSCAN radius changes in more dimensions was not measured. And whether higher-dimensional data tend toward unique or ambiguous optima, which decides what stability shows, depends on the data; the digits are one example. The remaining limits are specific to each section.

### k-means and the silhouette (Section 17.1)

- **Other algorithms draw other shapes.** Gaussian mixtures allow ellipses of different sizes, and density-based methods such as DBSCAN are designed to follow groups of any shape and to leave sparse regions unclustered. They were not run in Section 17.1, and each has its own assumptions and failures, which Sections 17.2 and 17.3 measure.
- **Raw pixels for the digits.** Clustering the digits after scaling or on learned features would change both the silhouette and the ARI. The comparison with the uniform square is about what the score means, not about the best possible clustering of handwriting.
- **Silhouette only.** The other common rule, the elbow of the within-cluster sum of squares plotted against $k$, has the same blind spot: the sum falls as $k$ grows on any data, noise included.

### Gaussian mixtures and the BIC (Section 17.2)

- **ICL is not a cure.** It penalises overlap, so it under-counts clusters that genuinely overlap, which is where mixture models are most useful. On the digits it chose the same $k$ as the BIC with both covariance shapes.
- **k up to 20, three starts each.** More starts can only raise each $k$'s likelihood, which could move the chosen $k$ by a component or two; that was not measured.
- **Other mixtures.** Mixtures of t or skewed distributions describe heavy-tailed or asymmetric clusters with fewer components, but they move the problem to whatever shape they assume.

### DBSCAN and HDBSCAN (Section 17.3)

- **Gaussian clusters.** The density-contrast problem was measured on Gaussian clusters, where it is cleanest.
- **One `min_samples`.** Five is scikit-learn's default; Ester and colleagues suggested four for two dimensions. Raising it smooths the density estimate and moves the good window; it does not give one radius two densities.
- **One knee rule.** The knee was found by a simple geometric rule. People reading the curve by eye will pick other points, and the moons' window was narrow enough that a small difference mattered.
- **HDBSCAN's own setting.** Its minimum cluster size plays the part the radius played, with a gentler effect here. It was not swept.

### Stability and the gap statistic (Section 17.4)

- **k-means only.** Stability was measured for k-means, whose objective has unique optima on these shapes. Other algorithms have other objectives and other kinds of stability; the argument of Ben-David and colleagues applies to any algorithm whose answer converges to a unique optimum.
- **One version of each method.** Stability has variants that compare to the full-data clustering, use bootstrap samples or normalise by a null; the gap statistic has variants in its reference distribution. Some of them can answer $k = 1$; this one cannot.
- **k from 2 to 8.** On the uniform square stability rose again from $k = 7$ to $k = 8$ while staying below its value at 4. A wider range was not tried, and could in principle find a more stable k for any shape.

## Key points

1. k-means returns $k$ convex pieces of similar spread on any data. It recovered separated round blobs (ARI 1.00) and nothing else: real groups of the wrong shape were cut wrongly, at ARI 0.09 for one large group with two small ones and 0.00 for two long parallel ones.
2. The silhouette rates the tidiness of the pieces, not the presence of groups. Uniform noise scored 0.40 at its chosen $k$; the digits never passed 0.19.
3. Fitting starts matter. Single k-means starts on the digits ranged from ARI 0.57 to 0.68, and the lowest sum of squares did not pick the start that best matched the classes. With one start per fit, a Gaussian mixture initialised from random responsibilities never reached the likelihood that k-means initialisation reached 90% of the time.
4. For one cluster that was not Gaussian, the BIC chose more components the more data it had: 2 to 18 for a square as the points grew from 200 to 20,000, while for a Gaussian cluster it chose one throughout. From 1,000 points on these choices were decisive by Kass and Raftery's scale, so a decisive BIC margin is not evidence of separate clusters.
5. Three separated squares got 9 components from the BIC and 3 from ICL; the digits got 11 or 28 depending on the covariance shape allowed. The BIC's k is a number of components of the allowed shape.
6. At the right radius DBSCAN separated two half-moons where k-means scored 0.25. Radii scoring 0.95 or more spanned a factor of 2.9, and in this draw the k-distance knee fell just outside them (in five other draws, reported in Section 22.2, inside). The radius and `min_samples` act as one density threshold.
7. One radius serves one density. With one cluster eight times the spread of its neighbours, no radius scored 0.9 in any of six draws, and the best in the first called 77% of the wide cluster noise; HDBSCAN scored 0.82 to 0.89. On the digits the knee's radius put 96% of the images in one cluster and scored 0.00.
8. Stability chose $k = 2$ for one elongated Gaussian (0.92) and $k = 4$ for a uniform square (0.94); neither has clusters, and stability cannot answer one. It measures whether the clustering objective has a unique optimum, which a clusterless shape can have.
9. Stability chose correctly for three separated blobs and chose the coarser of two right answers for two pairs of blobs. The gap statistic said one cluster where there were none and three where there were three, and on the digits found no stopping point up to 15; stability chose 10 or 9 there, depending on the resampling seed.
10. Each method answers a narrower question than the one it is used for. Test any clustering score or rule for choosing k on data with no clusters first, vary the starts and settings the answer depends on, and report component counts, densities and reproducibility as properties of the method's answer rather than as facts about the data.
