# Adam Is a Little Faster and No Better

Most networks today are trained with Adam, and its reputation is the sentence under test: *Adam converges faster and generalises as well as SGD*. A practitioner relies on it every time Adam is chosen by default, and every time a comparison of optimisers is read as a verdict on the optimisers themselves. Both halves have been challenged. If the first half is false, the time saved by choosing Adam is illusory; if the second is false, the choice costs accuracy on new data. And if comparisons of optimisers depend mainly on how carefully each one was tuned, many published rankings measure the tuning rather than the method.

This chapter compares Adam with SGD with momentum on the UCI digits and on a simulated task where the best possible error is known. It first describes how each optimiser updates the weights. It then tunes both by the same procedure and compares their test accuracy over twenty splits, which addresses generalisation. Next it measures speed at every learning rate on each optimiser's grid, and shows that which optimiser is faster depends on the rate. Finally it explains why the tuning procedure chose the rates it did, and what that implies for comparing optimisers.

## Two optimisers

*SGD with momentum* keeps a running *velocity* $v$, an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients, and moves the weights along it:

$$
v \leftarrow \mu v + g, \qquad w \leftarrow w - \eta v,
$$

where $g$ is the current minibatch gradient, $\eta$ the learning rate and $\mu$ the momentum, here 0.9 as is usual in practice. Momentum smooths the gradient across steps and lengthens the effective step along directions in which successive gradients agree. The size of each step is proportional to the size of the gradient.

*Adam* (Kingma and Ba) keeps two running averages for every weight: $m$, of the gradient, and $s$, of the squared gradient. After correcting both for their initialisation at zero, it updates each weight by

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s} + \epsilon},
$$

with $\epsilon$ a small constant for numerical safety. Dividing by the root of the average squared gradient normalises each weight's step by an estimate of that weight's gradient scale. A weight with large gradients and a weight with small ones therefore move at similar rates, and the size of a step is set mainly by $\eta$ rather than by the magnitude of the gradient. Chapter 22 found that for plain SGD the learning rate and the batch size act as one setting; Adam's normalisation is one reason that relation does not carry over unchanged.

The challenges to Adam's reputation come from two directions. Wilson and colleagues reported adaptive methods such as Adam generalising worse than SGD. Choi and colleagues found that rankings of optimisers change when every optimiser's settings are tuned with the same care. This chapter follows the second point and tunes both by one procedure.

## Tuned the same way, the two generalise equally well

The tuning uses a separate split of the digits, kept apart from the evaluation splits. On it, each optimiser's learning rate is chosen from a grid of five rates spanning a factor of 100, by accuracy on a held-out fifth of the training data, a *validation set*. Both then train from the same initial weights on twenty fresh splits. The network has two hidden layers of 256 units with ReLU activations and is trained for 30 epochs in minibatches of 32.

The same procedure is repeated on the simulated task from Chapter 4: two overlapping Gaussian classes in 10 dimensions, with 2,000 training rows per split and a network with two hidden layers of 64 units. Because the data are simulated, the *Bayes rule*, the best possible classifier for the true distribution, is known; on the 5,000 test rows used here it errs on 0.276 of them (0.268 on the 20,000 rows of Chapter 4). That leaves room for a better optimiser to show itself.

The block prints the tuned rates, each optimiser's mean test accuracy, and the mean difference with its standard error.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

for task in ("digits", "gauss"):
    r = nets.adam_vs_sgd(task=task)      # 20 splits, same init each
    a, b = r["adam"]["acc"], r["sgd"]["acc"]
    print(f"{task}: rates Adam {r['tuned']['adam']:g}, SGD "
          f"{r['tuned']['sgd']:g}; accuracy Adam {sum(a) / 20:.4f}, "
          f"SGD {sum(b) / 20:.4f}")
    print(f"    difference {r['diff_mean']:+.4f} ± {r['diff_se']:.4f}, "
          f"Adam ahead on {r['adam_wins']} of 20")
```

```text
digits: rates Adam 0.0003, SGD 0.01; accuracy Adam 0.9732, SGD 0.9722
    difference +0.0009 ± 0.0007, Adam ahead on 13 of 20
gauss: rates Adam 0.0003, SGD 0.003; accuracy Adam 0.7083, SGD 0.7078
    difference +0.0005 ± 0.0007, Adam ahead on 9 of 20
```

Each split gives one difference in accuracy between Adam and SGD, measured on the same test rows from the same starting weights, so the comparison is *paired*: what varies between the two runs is the optimiser and its tuned rate. The standard error is that of the mean of the twenty paired differences, their standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{20}$.

On the digits Adam's mean test accuracy is 0.9732, ahead by **+0.0009** with a standard error of 0.0007. It is ahead on 13 splits of 20 and level on 1. On the simulated task it is ahead by +0.0005 ± 0.0007, on 9 splits of 20. An interval of two standard errors around either difference includes zero, so neither is distinguishable from no difference. On the digits the whole difference is less than one test image in a thousand. The figure below shows the twenty differences for each task.

![Mean difference +0.0009 ± 0.0014 on the digits and +0.0005 ± 0.0014 on the hard task (two standard errors).](ml123-f1-diffs.png){alt="Two rows of points scattered either side of zero, with mean markers whose error bars cross zero."}

The second half of the sentence survives. Tuned by the same procedure, Adam generalises as well as SGD with momentum here, no better and no worse.

## Which optimiser is faster depends on the learning rate

The first half of the sentence is about speed, and speed depends on the learning rate at least as much as on the optimiser. The speed study trains both optimisers at every rate on their grids, on five splits of the digits, and records the training loss after every epoch. The block prints each run's loss after five epochs and the epoch at which it first falls below 0.01.

```python
sp = nets.optimiser_speed()              # digits, five splits
for name in ("adam", "sgd"):
    for lr, curve in zip(sp[name]["lrs"], sp[name]["curves"]):
        hit = next((i + 1 for i, v in enumerate(curve) if v < 0.01), None)
        when = f"at epoch {hit}" if hit else "not in 30 epochs"
        print(f"{name:4s} rate {lr:<6g} loss after 5 epochs "
              f"{curve[4]:.4f}, reaches 0.01 {when}")
```

```text
adam rate 0.0001 loss after 5 epochs 1.2943, reaches 0.01 not in 30 epochs
adam rate 0.0003 loss after 5 epochs 0.2420, reaches 0.01 at epoch 26
adam rate 0.001  loss after 5 epochs 0.0327, reaches 0.01 at epoch 9
adam rate 0.003  loss after 5 epochs 0.0114, reaches 0.01 at epoch 6
adam rate 0.01   loss after 5 epochs 0.1122, reaches 0.01 at epoch 19
sgd  rate 0.001  loss after 5 epochs 2.0037, reaches 0.01 not in 30 epochs
sgd  rate 0.003  loss after 5 epochs 1.0220, reaches 0.01 not in 30 epochs
sgd  rate 0.01   loss after 5 epochs 0.1469, reaches 0.01 at epoch 25
sgd  rate 0.03   loss after 5 epochs 0.0228, reaches 0.01 at epoch 8
sgd  rate 0.1    loss after 5 epochs 0.0266, reaches 0.01 at epoch 15
```

Two comparisons can be read off the table, and they point in opposite directions.

At the rates validation chose, 0.0003 for Adam and 0.01 for SGD, **SGD is faster**. After five epochs its training loss is 0.147 against Adam's 0.242, and it reaches 0.1 at epoch 7 against Adam's 9. Both reach 0.01 at about the same time, at 25 and 26 epochs.

At each optimiser's fastest rate, 0.003 for Adam and 0.03 for SGD, **Adam is faster**. It reaches 0.01 in 6 epochs and SGD in 8, and after ten epochs Adam's loss is 0.0006 against SGD's 0.0055. That is the advantage Adam is known for, and it is real. It is also two epochs out of eight, and it exists only at the rate chosen for speed. The figure below shows the training curves.

## Why validation chose the slower rates

The fast rates are not worse on new data. The table below gives test accuracy at every rate, averaged over the five splits of the speed study. Adam's fastest rate scores 0.977, level at that precision with the best of its grid; SGD's fastest rate is the best of its grid, at 0.975. The rates validation chose score 0.976 and 0.974.

The tuning split could not see differences of that size. Its held-out fifth is 180 digits, so a single digit is worth 0.0056 of accuracy, larger than the differences in question. On it, Adam's rates of 0.0003 and 0.001 tied, and so did SGD's three largest rates. Ties went to the smaller rate, which is the cautious choice and also the slower one. The comparison of speed "at the tuned rates" is therefore a comparison of tie-breaks, and it favoured SGD by accident. When a validation score is coarser than the differences it ranks, the tie-breaking rule makes the choice, not the data.

### Sensitivity to the learning rate

What does differ between the optimisers is how much the rate matters. Across Adam's grid of five rates, spanning a factor of 100, test accuracy stayed between 0.961 and 0.977; across SGD's, between 0.928 and 0.975. On these grids Adam was less sensitive to its learning rate. That is plausibly because it divides each step by the gradient's scale, so a given rate produces steps of a similar size across weights and layers. The mechanism was not isolated here, and a range measured across a grid depends on where the grid sits relative to each optimiser's good rates. Lower sensitivity is a practical virtue, but a different claim from converging faster. Choi and colleagues made the general point: rankings of optimisers depend on how, and how much, each one is tuned.

## A narrower sentence

On these two problems, measured over twenty splits with both optimisers tuned, the sentence becomes: *at a learning rate chosen for speed, Adam reaches a low training loss a little sooner than SGD with momentum; tuned for validation accuracy, the two generalise equally well; Adam's accuracy depends less on the learning rate*. Each part is narrower than the original, and each is measured.

The generalisation gap Wilson and colleagues reported does not appear here. Their networks were far larger and their tasks harder, so this is not a refutation of their result; it is a reminder that "Adam generalises worse" is also a claim about particular settings. The honest summary is that the choice of optimiser matters less than tuning whichever one is chosen, and that a comparison which tunes one optimiser and not the other measures the tuning. Both networks in this chapter had nothing between their layers but activations; Chapter 24 adds the layer most modern networks place there, batch normalisation.

## In practice

- **Tune every optimiser you compare by the same procedure.** Same grid width, same validation data, same budget. A comparison that tunes one and uses defaults for the other measures the tuning.
- **Say what the rate was tuned for.** A rate chosen for validation accuracy and a rate chosen for speed can differ, and the speed comparison reverses between them.
- **Compare paired runs over several splits.** Start both optimisers from the same weights on each split, report the mean difference with its standard error, and treat a difference within two standard errors of zero as not distinguishable from no difference.
- **Check the validation set's resolution.** If one validation example is worth more accuracy than the differences you are ranking, expect ties, and decide deliberately how ties are broken.
- **Value Adam for robustness, not for a better model.** Its smaller sensitivity to the learning rate is useful when tuning is expensive; it is not evidence that it finds better solutions.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Small networks, small data.** Two hidden layers on 900 digits or 2,000 simulated rows. On large language models Adam (or AdamW) is the near-universal choice, and it is common in vision, partly because SGD is hard to tune at that scale and partly because adaptive scaling helps with very different gradient scales across layers, such as embeddings.
- **A coarse grid.** Five rates per optimiser, spaced by about a factor of three. Both "fastest" and "tuned" are known only to that resolution, and validation accuracy on a fifth of one split (180 digits) has ties: Adam's 3e-4 and 1e-3 scored the same, and the smaller was taken.
- **One schedule.** Constant learning rates, no warm-up, no decay. Schedules change both speed and final accuracy, for both optimisers.
- **No weight decay.** Adam's interaction with weight decay is its own subject (AdamW exists because of it), and was left out by giving neither optimiser any.

## Key points

1. Tuned on validation data and run over twenty splits, Adam and SGD with momentum reached the same test accuracy: differences of +0.0009 ± 0.0007 on the digits and +0.0005 ± 0.0007 on a hard simulated task.
2. Speed belongs to the learning rate. At the rates validation chose, SGD's loss fell faster; at each optimiser's fastest rate, Adam reached a loss of 0.01 in 6 epochs and SGD in 8.
3. The fastest rates scored as well as any on test digits. Validation on 180 digits could not tell them apart from slower ones, and its tie-break chose the slower.
4. Adam's accuracy varied less across its grid (0.961 to 0.977) than SGD's (0.928 to 0.975), a practical virtue distinct from speed.
5. Compare optimisers only with each tuned by the same procedure, and report what the rates were tuned for.
