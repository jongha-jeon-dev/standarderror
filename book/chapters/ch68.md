# More Data Cures the Noise, Not the Design

Many of the problems in this book were demonstrated on small data: 200 rows, 300 tumours, 1,797 digits. A practitioner with millions of rows is entitled to wonder which of them still apply, and the common reassurance is that most of them fade: *with enough data, these problems go away*. The sentence matters because it decides where attention goes on a large project. If it is true, a big data set lets one stop worrying about leaks, error bars and validation design; if it is false for some problems, those problems survive into exactly the projects where nobody checks for them. Some problems do fade. The question is which, and the answer follows a simple rule.

This chapter takes seven problems from earlier chapters, rebuilds each in a compact version that can be run at any size, and measures each at 200, 800 and 3,200 rows. It sorts them by whether a sixteenfold increase in data made them smaller, explains the mechanism behind each group, and ends with a test that tells the groups apart before any data are collected.

## How the problems were measured

Each problem was rebuilt in a compact version of its chapter's setting and run at 200, 800 and 3,200 rows, on ten simulated data sets per size. Each is measured as the size of the error it causes, in its own unit: points of accuracy, points of AUC, points of a default rate, or a count of components. The question for each is the same: did sixteen times the data make it smaller?

The code below runs two of the seven at the smallest and largest sizes, on five data sets each. The first is a leak from selecting features on all rows; the second is the bias of a model trained only on approved loan applicants, both described in detail below.

```python
from standarderror.ml import course

for n in (200, 3200):
    leak = course._selection_leak(n, reps=5)        # a variance problem
    bias = course._selection_bias(n, reps=5)        # a design problem
    print(f"{n:5d} rows: selection leak {100 * leak:5.1f} points, "
          f"refused-applicant bias {100 * bias:6.1f} points")
```

```text
  200 rows: selection leak  25.2 points, refused-applicant bias  -42.4 points
 3200 rows: selection leak   7.1 points, refused-applicant bias  -45.5 points
```

The leak falls from 25.2 points to 7.1 as the rows grow sixteenfold. The bias does not fall at all: it is -42.4 points at 200 rows and -45.5 at 3,200. The comments in the code anticipate the explanation: one is a variance problem, the other a design problem. The rest of the chapter uses all ten data sets per size, so its figures differ slightly from this five-data-set run.

## Four problems of noise, which shrink

### A test score's standard error

The error bar of Chapter 1 is the textbook case. A test accuracy $\hat p$ on $n$ independent rows has standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$. At an accuracy of 0.8 it was 2.83 points at 200 rows, 1.41 at 800 and 0.71 at 3,200, halving with each fourfold increase in rows, as the $1/\sqrt{n}$ predicts.

### The optimism of a tuned score

Chapter 35 showed that the best cross-validated score in a search is optimistic, a *winner's curse*: each setting's score is an estimate with noise, and the setting that wins is disproportionately the one whose noise was favourable. Tuning a nearest-neighbour classifier over 32 settings, the optimism was 3.7 points at 200 rows, and -0.1 and +0.1 at 800 and 3,200. With more rows each setting's cross-validated score is less noisy, the best of them has less luck to exploit, and the search's reported score converges on what it delivers.

### Selecting features on all rows

Chapter 3's leak selects 20 of 1,000 noise features using every row, then cross-validates a model on them. It turned coin flips into 75% accuracy at 200 rows, 63.5% at 800 and 57.1% at 3,200. With more rows the noise features' chance correlations with the label are smaller, so the selection finds less to exploit. But at 3,200 rows it was still 7.1 points of pure fiction.

### A target encoding's leak into training

*Target encoding* replaces each level of a categorical feature with the mean of the label over the training rows at that level. Chapter 40 showed that, fitted on the training rows, it leaks into training: each row's own label is part of its encoded value. A 200-level noise category encoded this way ranked its own training labels at 43 points of AUC above chance with one row per level on average, 28 with four and 14 with sixteen. The more rows share a level, the less any one row's label moves the level's mean.

### What the four have in common

All four are the same kind of problem: an estimate fitted to a sample's noise, which shrinks as the sample grows. More data is the cure, at a rate that differs by problem: one over the square root of $n$ for the standard error, faster here for the tuning optimism, roughly in proportion to rows per level for the encoding. With a fixed number of features, settings or categories, the cure eventually arrives. The figure below shows all seven problems as the rows grow.

## Two problems of design, which stay

### Outcomes observed only for the approved

Chapter 39 studied *selective labels*: a lender observes whether a loan defaults only for applicants who were approved. In the compact version, a loan officer approves on information the model lacks as well as on the model's features. A model trained on the approved applicants underestimated the refused applicants' default rate by 47.7 points at 200 rows, 46.0 at 800 and 45.4 at 3,200.

More approved applicants teach the model more precisely about approved applicants. They say nothing about the refused, because the refused are never in the data, and the officer's extra information makes the two groups differ in ways the features do not show. Meng's analysis of big-data surveys makes the same point with a memorable result: a large non-random sample can carry no more information than a small random one, because its bias does not shrink with its size.

### Rows from the same people

Chapter 37 showed that rows from the same people are not new data. In *row-level cross-validation*, rows are assigned to folds at random, so each person's rows appear in both training and validation folds and the model is scored partly on people it has already seen. With every row from the same 20 people, it overstated accuracy on new people by 13.2 points at 200 rows, 14.8 at 800 and 15.6 at 3,200. More rows from the same people made the model better at recognising those people, which row-level folds reward and new people do not, so the gap grew slightly. The fix is more people, not more rows, and folds that keep each person's rows together.

## One problem that grows

The *Bayesian information criterion* (BIC) chooses a number of components for a Gaussian mixture by trading the fit's likelihood against a penalty on the number of parameters. For a single uniform square, which contains no clusters at all, it chose 4 components at 200 rows, 6 at 800 and 8 at 3,200. Chapter 57 found the same rising count up to 20,000 points, though not with the same numbers, on a different draw and with fewer starts of the fit here.

The reason is that the BIC is a *consistent* criterion: as the data grow, it selects the model that best approximates the data among those it is offered. A square is not a mixture of Gaussians, so no number of components is right, and as the data grow the evidence that one more component would approximate it better grows too. A consistent criterion becomes more confident about a misspecified model's answer. The answer is about the model's shape, not about clusters in the data. The table below collects all seven problems.

## The rule: variance shrinks, bias does not

Sorted, the seven fall into the old distinction between variance and bias. The four that shrank were variance: errors that come from fitting a sample's accidents, which average away. The two that stayed were bias in the data's design, who is in the sample and how the rows relate to each other, which no amount of the same data can reveal. The one that grew was bias in the model's assumptions, which more data make more confidently.

For a practitioner with a large data set, the book's problems therefore split in two. Error bars, tuning optimism and leaks from fitting on noise can mostly be retired, though not entirely: a leak that is 7 points at 3,200 rows is still a leak. Selection, grouping, drift, misspecified assumptions and the gap between a metric and its use keep their full force, and often hide better, because a large sample produces narrow error bars around the wrong number.

A practical test separates the two kinds before any data are collected: ask whether the problem would disappear if the sample grew to the whole population it was drawn from. A standard error, a tuned score's luck and a leak from fitting on noise would. A sample of approved applicants, rows from twenty people and a misspecified model would not: every approved applicant there is still contains no refused ones, and every row from those twenty people still describes only them.

Chapters 66 to 68 have each examined one kind of fragility. Chapter 69 puts them together, running a single project through every check in the book to see which ones change what the project would actually do.

## In practice

- **Classify each problem as noise or design** before deciding whether a large data set has dealt with it, using the whole-population test above.
- **Retire noise problems only relative to complexity.** A selection leak, an encoding leak or tuning optimism shrinks with rows only if the number of features, categories and settings does not grow with them.
- **Do not let a large sample excuse a biased one.** For selective labels, ask who never enters the data and how their outcomes could be measured, for example by approving a random sample.
- **Count independent units, not rows.** Where rows come from people, sessions or sites, validate with folds that keep each unit together, and grow the number of units.
- **Expect criteria such as the BIC to grow more confident, not more correct,** when the model family is wrong.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Compact versions.** Each problem was rebuilt in a small simulation. The rates at which the real versions shrink depend on the number of features, categories and settings, which grow with data in real projects.
- **Fixed complexity.** With 1,000 noise features held fixed, the selection leak shrank. If the number of features grows with the data, as it often does, the leak need not shrink at all.
- **Ten data sets per size.** Enough to separate shrinking from staying; not enough to pin the third digit of any value.
- **Seven problems of many.** Problems such as drift (Chapter 61) and feedback loops (Chapter 50) depend on time, not sample size, and were not on the list.

## Key points

1. Four problems shrank with sixteen times the data: the standard error (2.8 to 0.7 points), tuning optimism (3.7 to 0.1), the selection leak (24.7 to 7.1) and the encoding leak (43 to 14).
2. Two did not: selective labels stayed 45 points wrong and row-level cross-validation with 20 people stayed 16 points optimistic.
3. The BIC's count of Gaussians for one uniform square grew from 4 to 8.
4. Noise averages away; the design of the data and the assumptions of the model do not.
5. With a large data set, spend your attention on who is in the data, how rows depend on each other and what the model assumes, not on error bars.
