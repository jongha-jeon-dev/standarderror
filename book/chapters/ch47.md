# The Model With the Best AUC Was Never the Best When Misses Cost Ten Times More

Before a threshold is set, a model has usually been chosen, and the usual basis is a single number that ignores thresholds altogether: the area under the ROC curve. The inference drawn from it is the sentence this chapter tests: *the model with the higher AUC makes better decisions*. Practitioners meet it in every model-selection table, leaderboard and comparison of candidate models. If it fails when the costs of errors are unequal, the model selected for deployment can be the wrong one for the decision it will actually make, even though it ranks cases best on average.

The chapter first defines the AUC and compares five models by it and by the cost of their best decision at several cost ratios. It then looks at the part of the ranking where an expensive-miss decision is made, the cases a model rates safest, and shows that two models can each beat the other at opposite ends of the same ranking. It closes with two candidate replacements for the AUC, a partial AUC and the cost at the operating point.

## AUC and cost, compared on the same models

The *ROC curve* of a scoring model plots its true-positive rate against its false-positive rate as the threshold moves from the top of the score range to the bottom. The *area under the ROC curve* (AUC) has a direct probabilistic reading: it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive case is scored above a randomly chosen negative one. An AUC of 0.5 is a random ranking and 1 a perfect one. Its appeal is that it does not depend on the threshold, the *operating point* at which the model will be used; it summarises the whole ranking at once.

Chapter 46 showed how the threshold should depend on the *cost ratio* $r$, the cost of a missed positive divided by the cost of a false alarm. To compare models by their decisions rather than by their ranking alone, the experiment here gives each model its own best threshold at each cost ratio, so that calibration plays no part and only the quality of the ranking at the relevant point is compared.

Five models are trained on Chapter 4's simulated task, a two-class problem with Gaussian features in 10 dimensions and heavily overlapping classes: logistic regression, Gaussian naive Bayes, a fifteen-nearest-neighbour classifier (15-NN), a random forest and boosted trees. Each is trained on ten different sets of 2,000 rows and evaluated on 50,000 test rows. The code prints each model's mean AUC and mean best cost per case at $r = 1$, 10 and 20, and then, for each criterion, the fraction of the ten training sets in which each model came first.

```python
from standarderror.ml import decide

a = decide.auc_and_decisions()   # 10 training sets of 2,000 rows
for k, v in a["mean"].items():
    print(f"{k:14s} AUC {v['auc']:.3f} | best cost at r=1 "
          f"{v['cost1']:.3f}, r=10 {v['cost10']:.3f}, r=20 {v['cost20']:.3f}")
for q in ("auc", "cost1", "cost10", "cost20"):
    w = {k: v for k, v in a["wins"][q].items() if v}
    print(f"best by {q}: {w}")
```

```text
naive Bayes    AUC 0.708 | best cost at r=1 0.345, r=10 0.490, r=20 0.494

forest         AUC 0.754 | best cost at r=1 0.311, r=10 0.484, r=20 0.494

```

When both errors cost the same, the AUC chose correctly: the forest had the highest AUC in all ten training sets and the lowest cost in all ten. When a miss cost ten times a false alarm, the forest was the cheapest model in none of them. The boosted trees were cheapest in 7, and logistic regression, whose mean AUC of 0.677 was the lowest of the five by a wide margin, in 3. At twenty times, logistic regression and the boosted trees split the ten between them. The figure below shows the wins under each criterion.

## The decision is made at the safe end of the ranking

Chapter 46 found that when misses are expensive the right decision is to flag nearly everything. The model's job then shrinks to finding the few cases safe enough to clear, and every positive among the cleared cases is a costly miss. What matters is therefore the very bottom of the ranking: how pure, in negatives, are the cases a model rates safest. The next block prints, for each model, the share of positives among the 2% and the 5% of test cases it scores lowest.

```python
for k, v in a["mean"].items():
    print(f"{k:14s} positives among its safest 2%: "
          f"{v['bottom0.02']:.1%}, safest 5%: {v['bottom0.05']:.1%}")
```

```text
logistic       positives among its safest 2%: 2.8%, safest 5%: 7.4%
naive Bayes    positives among its safest 2%: 6.5%, safest 5%: 9.6%
15-NN          positives among its safest 2%: 4.9%, safest 5%: 8.2%
forest         positives among its safest 2%: 5.6%, safest 5%: 7.3%
boosted trees  positives among its safest 2%: 2.8%, safest 5%: 5.5%
```

Among the 2% of cases each model rated safest, the forest's held 5.6% positives and logistic regression's 2.8%, half as many. The boosted trees matched logistic regression there. Clearing the forest's safest cases therefore incurs about twice as many misses as clearing the same number of logistic regression's.

Why the forest is weaker at the extreme is not established by this experiment. A plausible reason is that its leaves average at least five training rows each, and a forest's score is an average over many such leaves, which blunts its scores at the extremes; a linear model's score, by contrast, keeps growing as a case moves further from the boundary. The measurement shows the effect, not that cause.

The AUC is an average over the whole ranking, every pair of a positive and a negative, and the few percent of cases at the safe end are a small part of it. A model can therefore lead on average and trail in the corner the decision uses. Hand made a stronger point in 2009. Comparing AUCs turns out to be equivalent to comparing expected costs averaged over a distribution of cost ratios, and that distribution depends on the classifier itself. Two models' AUCs are thus averages over different, implicit beliefs about the costs, and using the AUC to choose between them assumes something about the decision that nobody wrote down. The table below collects the comparison.

## Two models, two ends of one ranking

Naive Bayes shows the same thing from the other side. Its AUC, 0.708, was clearly above logistic regression's 0.677, and among the 1% of cases it rated riskiest it found 79.0% positives, against logistic regression's 53.4%. At the safe end, however, it was the worst of the five: 6.5% positives among its safest 2%, and the highest cost of all at $r = 10$ (0.490). Each of the two models is better than the other, at opposite ends of the same ranking, and the AUC adds the two ends together.

The switch from one winner to another is gradual. As the cost ratio rises from 1 to 20, the forest's wins go from 10 in ten to 9, 2 and then none, while the boosted trees' go from none to 7 at $r = 5$, and logistic regression enters at $r = 5$ and takes half the wins at $r = 20$. Each cost ratio corresponds to a different point on the ROC curve, and the models' curves cross somewhere along the way.

Sampling noise also plays a part. Across the ten training sets the AUCs differed by more than their standard deviations, so the AUC ranking was stable. At $r = 10$ the costs of the leading models differed by only about one standard deviation, which is why the wins there are split rather than unanimous. The forest's complete absence from the winners at high cost ratios is the robust part of the result.

## Better summaries, and the one the decision uses

The obvious repair is a *partial AUC*, proposed by McClish in 1989: the area under only the part of the ROC curve that matters for the decision. Restricted to the high-sensitivity end, false-negative rates of 10% or less, it chose the forest in 4 training sets and the boosted trees in 6, and never logistic regression. That is closer to the cost-based ranking than the full AUC, but not the same. A plausible reason is that the decisions at $r = 10$ and 20 sit further out still, clearing only a few percent of cases, at the far edge of the region the partial AUC averages over, so most of that area lies away from the operating point. A partial AUC is only as good as the guess about where the operating point is.

The direct summary is the one the decision uses: the cost at the operating point, or, when capacity rather than cost fixes how many cases can be acted on, the *precision in the top k*, the share of positives among the k cases ranked riskiest. At the top end of the ranking the AUC's order roughly held. Among the 5% of cases each model rated riskiest, the forest or the boosted trees, the top two by AUC, had the highest precision in every training set, the forest alone in 6. A capacity-limited decision that acts on the riskiest cases and an expensive-miss decision that clears the safest cases use opposite ends of the ranking. Which end matters is a fact about the use, not about the model.

The differences in cost at $r = 10$ are small, up to about a hundredth per case. As Chapter 46 found, even the true probabilities save only about a tenth of the cost of flagging everyone on this task (0.440 at their best threshold here, against about 0.497). Small differences in a small saving are still the differences that decide which model to deploy.

Chapters 46 and 47 act on a model's ranking: flag these cases, clear those. Chapter 48 turns to decisions that act on the world, and asks whether the features a model relies on are the ones to change.

## In practice

- **Decide the operating point before comparing models.** From the costs (Chapter 46) or from the capacity, work out where on the ROC curve the model will be used.
- **Compare models at that point.** Report each candidate's cost at its operating point, or its precision in the top k when capacity is fixed. Use the AUC only as a first filter.
- **Plot the ROC curves together.** If they cross, the AUC's ranking says nothing about which model is better at a given operating point.
- **Inspect both ends of the ranking.** Compute the positive rate among the cases each model rates safest and riskiest. A model can lead at one end and trail at the other.
- **Do not rely on a partial AUC without knowing where the decision sits.** It helps only if its range covers the operating point.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One task with overlapping classes.** On tasks where models separate the classes well, their ROC curves may cross less often; that was not measured here.
- **Each model at its own best threshold.** Real deployments choose the threshold on validation data, as in Chapter 46, which adds noise to every cost and can change the winner in close cases.
- **Ten training sets.** The counts of wins are out of ten; the forest's zero at $r = 10$ and its ten by AUC are the robust part.
- **Ranking-only decisions.** Decisions that depend on calibrated probabilities, not just the ranking, add calibration to the comparison, as Chapter 46 measured.

## Key points

1. The forest had the highest AUC in all ten training sets and the lowest cost when both errors cost the same.
2. When a miss cost ten times a false alarm it was the cheapest in none; logistic regression, last by AUC at 0.677, was cheapest in 3, and at twenty times in 5.
3. At those costs the decision depends on the cases a model rates safest, where the forest put 5.6% positives and logistic regression 2.8%.
4. The AUC averages over the whole ranking, and over implicit cost ratios that differ between models; two models can each be better at opposite ends of one ranking.
5. Choose a model by its cost at the operating point you will use, or by precision at the capacity you have, and use the AUC only as a first filter.

## Further reading

- Hand, "Measuring classifier performance: a coherent alternative to the area under the ROC curve", *Machine Learning* (2009): shows that the AUC implicitly averages costs over a classifier-dependent distribution, and proposes an alternative.
- Provost and Fawcett, "Robust classification for imprecise environments", *Machine Learning* (2001): the ROC convex hull and choosing classifiers when costs are uncertain.
- McClish, "Analyzing a portion of the ROC curve", *Medical Decision Making* (1989): the partial AUC, restricted to the region of the curve relevant to a decision.
