# Sixty-Nine Sentences, Measured, and One Held as Written

Each of the previous sixty-nine chapters took one sentence from the standard machine-learning curriculum, the kind of sentence a textbook, a course or a senior colleague states as fact, and measured where it stopped being true. The sentences ran from how a score is read (*the model with the higher test score is better*) through models, training, the rows, decisions and language models, to a model in production and the book itself (*a verdict measured once will hold on the next draw*). This last chapter tests no new sentence. It sorts what happened to the sixty-nine, and counts the checks that kept the book's numbers honest. For a practitioner the sorting is useful in its own right: it shows which kinds of rule broke most often here, and so which of the rules repeated in their own team deserve a measurement first.

The chapter first counts the assertions that pinned the book's numbers. It then states the rule used to classify each verdict and gives the result, by part. It groups the broken sentences into four families, each a habit worth unlearning, and records what the book's own checks found wrong with the book. It closes the book.

## The book's numbers were pinned by assertions

Every number in a chapter came from code that ran when the chapter was built, and the numbers the prose quotes are pinned by *assertions*: statements in the build code that check a computed value against the value the text states. If the code stops producing that value, the build fails and the chapter cannot be published. Those assertions can be counted. The code below counts the `assert` lines in every chapter's build.

```python
from standarderror.ml import course

c = course.assert_census()       # counts `assert` lines in every build
print(c["episodes"], "episodes,", c["asserts"], "assert statements")
print("median per episode:", c["median"], " most:", c["most"])
```

```text
70 episodes, 936 assert statements
median per episode: 13.0  most: (29, 21)
```

The builds behind the 70 chapters carry 936 assert statements, a median of 13 per chapter; the most, 21, are in Chapter 29's build.

The 936 statements are the book's working evidence. They guard against a number going stale when the code or a library changes. Chapter 66 showed what they do not guard against: a number that was lucky, pinned as firmly as one that was typical. The figure below shows the count for each chapter.

## How each verdict was classified

Sorting a verdict needs a rule, and this is the one used:

- a sentence **failed** if it was false in the setting its chapter measured;
- it **held under conditions** if it was true in some measured settings and false in others, or true for one half of a two-part sentence;
- it **held** if it was true in every setting measured.

The classification is the author's reading of each chapter's own conclusion, and some calls are close. The list of calls is in the chapter's code, for anyone who would sort differently.

## Thirty-eight failed, thirty held under conditions, one held

By that rule, 38 sentences failed as stated, 30 held under conditions, and 1 held as written. The one that held was Chapter 3's: *fit every preprocessing step inside the folds*. Even that came with a measured scale. Violations ranged from nothing, on real data with strong features, to coin flips scored at 0.88 on noise, but no measured setting made the rule wrong.

The failures were spread across the book. Every part had at least one, and three parts had four or more of their five: what a score means (Part I), turning predictions into decisions (Part X, all five) and grading a language model (Part XI). Those are the parts whose sentences are rules for reading a number or acting on it: the higher score is better, the threshold is 0.5, the benchmark measures the model. Such a rule compresses a judgement into a default, and a default is what a measurement in a new setting tends to break.

The part with the fewest failures was trees and ensembles (Part III), with one. Its sentences, about forests, boosting and out-of-bag error, were mostly true within limits the curriculum seldom states. The figure below shows every verdict, arranged by part.

## Four families of broken sentences

Read together, the failures and the conditional verdicts fall into a few families. Each family is a single habit worth unlearning.

### A score answers a narrower question than the one asked of it

Cross-validation estimates the average error of a training procedure, not the error of the particular model fitted on the data in hand (Chapter 2). A test score has an error bar wider than most gains (Chapter 1). The best score of a search reflects the search's luck as well as the model's quality (Chapters 35 and 67). A benchmark score includes its prompt and, for multiple choice, the distractors it offered (Chapters 53 and 54). An AUC measures ranking; it does not choose a decision (Chapter 47). In each case the number is correct, but it answers a narrower question than the one a reader puts to it.

### A method reports on the model or its assumptions, not on the data

Importance measures what a model uses, not what matters in the world or what could be changed (Chapters 16 and 48). Partial dependence and SHAP answer questions about rows that do not exist, or about a background someone chose (Chapters 17 and 18). The BIC counts Gaussians, and t-SNE keeps neighbours (Chapters 56 and 57). Each method is faithful to something, but the thing it is faithful to is the model or the method's own assumptions.

### The rows are not what they seem

Rows from the same people, applicants who were refused, a category's own labels and a time series' remembered errors (Chapters 36, 37, 39 and 40) all make the data appear to say more than they hold. Chapter 68 showed that more rows of the same kind did not fix the first two: they were problems of the data's design, not of its size.

### A prediction is not a decision

The threshold, the cost of a mistake, who is helped by an intervention, and what a deployed rule does to the data it later learns from (Chapters 46 to 50, and 63) are not contained in the predictions. In Chapter 69, a single project run through every check, the threshold mattered more than the choice of model.

## What the book got wrong about itself

The book held itself to the same standard, and did not fully meet it. Chapter 66 reran the twelve verdicts that had rested on a single random draw, with five new seeds each. Seven held every time, and two held four times in five, where a threshold written into the prose was tighter than the evidence. Three did not hold as written: a heuristic radius that missed in the published draw and hit in five others (Chapter 58), a share described as "most" that was 33% to 53% on other splits (Chapter 18), and an anomaly score's AUC that crossed the round threshold its assertion wrote down (Chapter 59). Those chapters now say so.

Chapter 65 took four of the book's simulated findings to real data and found that the directions replicated while the sizes often did not. Chapter 67 showed how a sequence of defensible choices can produce a number nobody chose. Both apply to this book too: its sentences were chosen by one author, and its settings were chosen to make each effect visible.

## The end of the book

This is the last chapter of *Machine Learning, Taught Through What Breaks*. Its argument has been the same throughout: a sentence about machine learning is a claim about some setting, and it can be measured there. Most of the curriculum is true somewhere, but only one of the sixty-nine sentences held in every setting measured. What the book offers in place of its sixty-nine sentences is the habit of asking where each one was measured. The fourteen-item checklist in Chapter 69 is the shortest way to bring that habit to a project. The other chapters are there for the day one of their sentences matters to a decision you are about to make.

## In practice

- **Ask where a sentence was measured** before acting on it, and whether your setting resembles that one.
- **Measure the rules your team repeats.** Rules for reading a number or acting on it, such as which score is better or where the threshold sits, are the sentences that broke most often here, so test those first.
- **Rewrite conditional truths as conditions.** When a rule holds only in some settings, state which, rather than keeping the unconditional version.
- **Pin your numbers, and rerun them.** Automated checks keep reported numbers from going stale; rerunning on new seeds keeps them from being lucky. Neither replaces the other.

## Limits of the evidence

- **The sentences were chosen to break.** Each was picked because it is stated as fact and could be tested on a laptop. The count of failures says how often such sentences stop short, not how often the curriculum is wrong.
- **One author's classification.** The line between "failed" and "held under conditions" is a judgement. A different reader would move a handful of chapters, and the code lists every call so that they can.
- **Small data and simulations.** Most chapters used data sets of hundreds to a few thousand rows, or simulations built to show an effect. Chapters 65 and 68 measured how far such findings travel; the answer was: in direction, usually; in size, not always.
- **Assertions pin what was measured.** They cannot catch a mistake in the code that produced a number, only a change in it.

## Key points

1. Of sixty-nine sentences, 38 failed as stated, 30 held under conditions and 1 held as written: fit every preprocessing step inside the folds. Most of the curriculum is true somewhere; the work is finding where.
2. The failures clustered in three parts: how a score is read, how a prediction becomes a decision, and how a language model is graded.
3. The broken sentences fall into four families: a score answering a narrower question than the one asked, a method reporting on the model rather than the data, rows that are not what they seem, and a prediction mistaken for a decision.
4. 936 assertions kept the numbers from going stale, and five extra seeds showed that three of twelve single-draw verdicts were luckier than the words chosen for them.
5. Before acting on a sentence about machine learning, ask what setting it was measured in, and measure it in yours.
