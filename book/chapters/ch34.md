# Recalibration Fixes the Average, Given Enough Rows

Chapter 33 found that distances can mean less than the column count suggests, and sometimes more. Predicted probabilities are the other number a model hands over to be trusted. Earlier chapters met models whose probabilities were too confident: boosting whose test error held steady while its log loss deteriorated (Chapter 14), and dropout's weight scaling, which kept dropout's answers but lost its doubts (Chapter 25). The standard repair is recalibration, and the sentence under test is how it is usually presented: *Platt scaling or isotonic regression fixes a model's probabilities*. A practitioner relies on it whenever probabilities feed a decision, a threshold set from costs, or a risk shown to a person. If it is false, a recalibration step can make good probabilities worse, or leave a whole group of rows with wrong probabilities while the overall check looks perfect.

This chapter first defines calibration and the two methods, then measures how many held-out rows each needs before it helps an overconfident model. It then recalibrates a model that is already nearly calibrated, looks at what recalibration does to ranking and to the usual calibration error, and finally shows a limit that no amount of data removes: a model calibrated on average and badly miscalibrated within a group.

## Calibration and two ways to repair it

A classifier is *calibrated* if, among the rows to which it assigns probability $q$, a fraction $q$ are positive: when it says 0.6, about 60% of those rows are positive. Two scores measure departures from it. *Log loss* is the average of $-\log$ of the probability the model gave to the outcome that occurred; it rewards probabilities that are both well ranked and well calibrated, and needs no binning. The *expected calibration error* (ECE) sorts rows into bins by predicted probability, here ten equal-width bins, and averages the gap between each bin's mean prediction and its observed positive rate, weighting bins by their number of rows.

Both repair methods fit a second, small model on rows the first model did not train on, the *calibration set*, mapping the first model's score to a new probability.

- *Platt scaling* fits a logistic regression to the score's *logit*, $\log\{q/(1-q)\}$. The map is $\sigma(a \cdot \mathrm{logit}(q) + b)$, with $\sigma$ the logistic function: two parameters, and an increasing curve whenever $a$ is positive.
- *Isotonic regression* fits any non-decreasing step function from score to probability, chosen to fit the calibration labels as closely as possible in squared error. It has as many free steps as the data allow.

Both need held-out rows, and the question is how many.

### How many rows each method needs

The task is the simulated two-class problem of Chapter 4: two Gaussian classes in 10 dimensions. Two models are trained on 2,000 rows: a naive Bayes model and boosted trees. Each is recalibrated on fresh calibration sets of 50 to 5,000 rows, twenty sets at each size, and every version is scored on 20,000 test rows. The code prints each model's uncalibrated log loss and calibration error, then the mean log loss after each method at each size.

```python
from standarderror.ml import around

c = around.calibration_size()        # 20 calibration sets per size
for name, row in c.items():
    print(f"{name}: uncalibrated log loss {row['raw']['nll']:.3f}, "
          f"calibration error {row['raw']['ece']:.3f}")
    for p, i in zip(row["platt"], row["isotonic"]):
        print(f"   {p['n']:5d} rows   Platt {p['nll']:.3f}   "
              f"isotonic {i['nll']:.3f}")
```

The boosted trees start badly calibrated, with an expected calibration error of 0.157. Platt scaling on just 50 rows takes their log loss from 0.748 to 0.612. Isotonic regression on the same 50 rows makes it worse, 0.761, and starts to help only at 100 rows. At every size Platt scaling does better than isotonic regression. ## Recalibrating a calibrated model makes it worse

The naive Bayes model starts nearly calibrated, with an error of 0.023. Recalibrating it can help only a little, and estimating the correction has a cost. On 50 rows Platt scaling raises its log loss from 0.626 to 0.647; it needs 1000 rows before it improves on doing nothing, and then by 0.0010. Isotonic regression is worse than doing nothing at every size up to 5,000 rows, where it still costs 0.0005.

The reason is the usual one for any fitted model: variance. A correction estimated from 50 labelled rows is noisy, and when the probabilities were already right, any error in the correction moves them away from right; there is no bias to remove that could pay for it. Isotonic regression, with its many free steps, has more variance than Platt scaling's two parameters, which is why Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana recommended Platt scaling for small calibration sets and isotonic regression only when there are thousands of rows. The measurement agrees, and adds a point that is easy to miss: recalibration is not free even when it is unnecessary, so the calibration should be checked before it is corrected.

### What recalibration does to the ranking

Recalibration also touches the ranking of rows, measured by the *AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen positive row receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative one. Platt scaling with a positive slope, as here, is a strictly increasing map, so it preserves the order of the scores exactly, and the AUC after it is identical to the uncalibrated AUC at every size. Isotonic regression maps whole ranges of scores to the same value, creating ties among rows that the original model had ordered, and the information in that order is lost. It lowered the AUC at every one of the 6 sizes for both models: for the boosted trees on 50 rows, to 0.7424 from 0.7558. The table below collects the log losses and AUCs.

## The calibration error tells the same story with larger swings

Log loss is the honest score for probabilities, but the number usually reported is the calibration error. It tells the same story with larger swings. For the boosted trees, calibration error falls from 0.157 uncalibrated to 0.082 after Platt scaling on 50 rows and 0.013 on 5,000; isotonic regression reaches 0.107 and 0.014. For the naive Bayes model the direction reverses at the small end: from 0.023 uncalibrated to 0.079 after Platt scaling on 50 rows, 3.4 times worse, and 0.107 after isotonic regression.

There is a structural reason why the error cannot reach zero on a finite calibration set. The correction is estimated from the calibration rows' labels, which are noisy 0s and 1s. An observed rate estimated from $m$ labels has a standard error proportional to $1/\sqrt{m}$, and each region of scores the map has to fit gets only a share of the $n$ calibration rows, so the fitted map is off by at least roughly $1/\sqrt{n}$ there. Two parameters spread that noise thinly across the whole range; a step function with a step wherever the data allow puts few rows behind each step and so concentrates it. The calibration set therefore has to be large compared with the precision the probabilities are expected to have, and a few hundred rows buy precision of a few hundredths at best.

## Calibrated on average, miscalibrated within a group

The deeper limit has nothing to do with sample size. A calibration curve compares predicted and observed rates across all rows, so calibration in that sense is a property of the average. The next simulation has two groups, holding 70% and 30% of the rows, with different base rates and a shared dependence on one feature. A logistic model is trained on the feature but not told the group. The code prints its calibration error over all rows and, for each group, the true positive rate, the mean prediction and the calibration error within the group.

```python
g = around.subgroup_calibration()
print(f"calibration error, all rows: {g['blind']['all']:.3f}")
for grp in g["groups"]:
    k = grp["group"]
    print(f"group {k} ({grp['share']:.0%} of rows): true rate "
          f"{grp['rate']:.3f}, mean prediction {grp['blind_mean']:.3f}, "
          f"error {g['blind'][k]:.3f}")
```

```text
calibration error, all rows: 0.005
group 0 (70% of rows): true rate 0.399, mean prediction 0.481, error 0.083
group 1 (30% of rows): true rate 0.669, mean prediction 0.478, error 0.191
```

Over all rows the model is calibrated to 0.005. Within the smaller group, 30% of the rows, its error is 0.191: it predicts 0.48 on average for a group whose rate is 0.67. In the larger group it predicts 0.48 for a rate of 0.40. The two errors have opposite signs, and in the overall average they cancel. The mechanism is that a model which cannot see the group can at best predict, for each feature value, the pooled rate among rows with that value, which lies between the two groups' rates. That pooled rate is right on average and wrong for both groups.

No recalibration of the model's score can fix this. The score is the same function of the feature in both groups, so two rows with the same feature value, one from each group, get the same score, and any monotone correction moves both groups' predictions in the same direction. What fixes it is information: the same logistic model given the group is calibrated to 0.005 and 0.007 within the two groups. Hébert-Johnson and colleagues call calibration that holds within every identifiable subgroup *multicalibration*. It is a property of the model and its inputs, not of a correction applied afterwards. The figure below compares the two models overall and within each group.

## In practice

- **Check calibration before correcting it.** Compute log loss and a calibration curve on held-out data first. A model that is already close to calibrated is likely to be made worse by recalibration on a small set.
- **Use Platt scaling on small calibration sets.** Here it helped an overconfident model from 50 rows; isotonic regression hurt at 50 and needed 100. Reserve isotonic regression for calibration sets of thousands of rows.
- **Judge a recalibration by log loss on rows it was not fitted on.** Split the held-out data, fit on one part and score on the other; keep the correction only if log loss improves.
- **Expect isotonic regression to cost some ranking.** If the AUC or the ordering of rows matters, prefer a monotone parametric map, or check the AUC after isotonic regression.
- **Check calibration within the groups that matter.** Plot the calibration curve separately by region, product, age band or any other variable that bears on the decision. If a group is off while the overall curve is fine, recalibrating the score will not help; give the model the information that distinguishes the group.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One task, two models.** The row counts at which recalibration starts to help depend on how badly calibrated the model is and how noisy the task is. What transfers is the order: Platt scaling before isotonic regression on small sets, and checking before correcting.
- **Calibration sets drawn fresh.** In practice the calibration rows are often a slice of the training data, or the validation set already used for tuning, which adds its own optimism.
- **Expected calibration error is binned.** It used ten equal-width bins; with other binning the numbers move a little. Log loss, which needs no bins, tells the same story here.
- **Two groups, known.** Real subgroups are many, overlapping and often unrecorded. Calibration within the groups one can name is a lower bound on the problem.

## Key points

1. Recalibration is a fitted model, with variance. On 50 rows Platt scaling cut an overconfident model's log loss from 0.748 to 0.612; isotonic regression raised it to 0.761.
2. Recalibrating a model that is already calibrated makes it worse until the calibration set is large: 1000 rows for Platt scaling here, more than 5,000 for isotonic regression.
3. Platt scaling leaves the ranking untouched; isotonic regression lowered the AUC at every size.
4. Calibration is an average. A model calibrated to 0.005 overall was off by 0.19 in a group holding 30% of the rows.
5. Check calibration within the groups that matter, and fix a group's miscalibration with information, not with a correction of the score.

## Further reading

- Platt, "Probabilistic outputs for support vector machines" (1999): the original logistic map from a classifier's score to a probability.
- Zadrozny and Elkan, "Transforming classifier scores into accurate multiclass probability estimates", *KDD* (2002): isotonic regression as a calibration method.
- Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, "Predicting good probabilities with supervised learning", *ICML* (2005): a comparison of calibration across model families, with the advice on calibration-set size.
- Hébert-Johnson et al., "Multicalibration", *ICML* (2018): calibration required to hold within every identifiable subgroup.
