# A Leaked Benchmark Shows in the Set, Not in the Item

Language models are trained on large scrapes of the web, and benchmarks live on the web. A model may therefore have seen some test questions, with their answers, during training; this is *benchmark contamination*. Nobody evaluating the model can rerun its training without them, but they can examine the model, and most contamination checks rest on one sentence: *if a model has memorised a benchmark, its likelihood on the benchmark's items will show it.* A practitioner meets the sentence when a vendor reports a contamination scan that found nothing, or when deciding whether a strong benchmark result can be trusted. If it is false, a clean scan says little, and an inflated score passes for ability.

This chapter manufactures contamination with a known dose and measures what it does. It first shows that leaking a benchmark into training raises the benchmark score without raising the ability the benchmark is meant to measure. It then shows that the model's likelihood cannot say which individual items leaked, and explains why. Finally it shows that the same likelihoods, compared as whole sets against fresh problems, detect the leak in every run from two insertions on.

## Leaking a benchmark on purpose, with a known dose

The model is the main arithmetic transformer of Chapter 51, one of the small models trained in this book's repository. The benchmark is 300 problems from its test split, 150 multiplications and 150 mixed-operation (order of operations) problems, on which the starting model gets 0.74 and 0.46 right, so there is room to improve. A *fresh set* of 600 problems of the same kinds is drawn alongside and never leaked.

The model is then trained for 600 more steps, roughly one more pass over its training data, with each benchmark line inserted into the training stream $k$ times at random positions, for $k$ from 0 to 32. The number of insertions $k$ is the *dose*. The runs with $k = 0$ matter most. They receive the same extra training with nothing leaked, so they separate the effect of the leak from the effect of training further, and five seeds of them show how much a run varies by chance.

Training reads random 64-character windows from the stream rather than whole lines in order, so an insertion is an opportunity for the model to see a line, not a guaranteed reading. At one insertion, a fair share of the leaked lines is probably never seen whole.

The code loads the two sets and prints three problems from each. The comment records the call that ran each fine-tuning job.

```python
from standarderror.ml import llmeval as le

bench, fresh = le.leak_sets()          # 300 to leak, 600 never seen
print(len(bench), "leaked, e.g.", [p.line.strip() for p in bench[:3]])
print(len(fresh), "fresh,  e.g.", [p.line.strip() for p in fresh[:3]])
# le.finetune(model, [p.line for p in bench], repeats=k, seed=s) is
# what scripts/llm_eval_runs.py ran for every k and seed below.
```

```text
300 leaked, e.g. ['368*6=2208', '82*61=5002', '617*3=1851']
600 fresh,  e.g. ['362*8=2896', '64*56=3584', '137*8=1096']
```

Each problem is a single line: the question, an equals sign and the answer. The training stream has 168,856 lines, so at two insertions the leaked lines are 0.35% of it, and even at 32 they are 5.4%. The small doses are the realistic case: a benchmark is a speck in a training corpus, seen a few times through copies, not drilled.

## The benchmark score rose; the ability did not

The next block reads the committed per-item results of every fine-tuning run and prints, for each dose and seed, accuracy on the leaked benchmark, accuracy on the fresh set, and an item-level detection score that the next section explains.

```python
c = le.contamination()                 # the committed per-item results
for key, v in c["runs"].items():
    if key != "base":
        print(f"k={v['dose']:2d} seed {v['seed']}  leaked "
              f"{v['bench']:.3f}  fresh {v['fresh']:.3f}  "
              f"item AUC {v['auc_loss']:.3f}")
```

k= 0 seed 3  leaked 0.547  fresh 0.523  item AUC 0.499
k= 0 seed 4  leaked 0.503  fresh 0.522  item AUC 0.494
k= 1 seed 0  leaked 0.540  fresh 0.532  item AUC 0.515
k= 2 seed 0  leaked 0.630  fresh 0.532  item AUC 0.545
k= 4 seed 0  leaked 0.630  fresh 0.542  item AUC 0.535
k= 8 seed 0  leaked 0.647  fresh 0.525  item AUC 0.559

k= 8 seed 2  leaked 0.640  fresh 0.535  item AUC 0.561

Inserted once, the leaked problems made no measurable difference: the score was 0.540, inside the controls' range of 0.503 to 0.577. From two insertions on, every run scored above every control, and at 32 insertions the benchmark score was 0.800, +0.249 over the controls' mean. The fresh problems, meanwhile, stayed between 0.513 and 0.542 in every run, leaked or not. The benchmark reported an improvement that existed only on the benchmark.

### What an outside evaluator would see

One comparison is less flattering to the leak. Every fine-tuned run, leaked or not, lost ground against the starting model, which scored 0.600 on the benchmark and 0.562 on the fresh set. Measured against the starting model rather than against the controls, the two-insertion run gained only +0.030 on the benchmark while losing 0.030 on fresh problems. The controls make the two effects separable; an outside evaluator comparing a model with its predecessor has no controls and sees only their net.

### A training run is a sample too

The spread of the controls deserves attention in its own right. Five runs that differed only in their random seed scored between 0.503 and 0.577 on the same 300 problems, a range of 0.073, and the three runs at $k = 8$ spanned 0.630 to 0.647. At one insertion, a comparison of one contaminated run with one clean run would have been a coin toss. This is Chapter 51's lesson in the training direction: there a benchmark was a sample of questions, here a training run is a sample of possible runs.

## Item by item, the likelihood cannot say which leaked

### Membership inference by loss

The usual detector asks whether the model finds a particular item suspiciously likely. In privacy research this is *membership inference*: deciding, from a model's behaviour, whether a given example was in its training data. Its simplest version, analysed by Yeom and colleagues in 2018, thresholds the *loss*, here the negative log-likelihood the model assigns to the correct answer, on the principle that a model's loss on its training examples tends to be lower than on others. Items below the threshold are flagged as members.

Its quality as a detector is summarised by the *AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen leaked item is ranked as more suspicious than a randomly chosen fresh one; 0.5 is a coin and 1 is perfect separation. A second summary is the share of leaked items caught when the threshold is set so that a fixed share of fresh items, the *false-flag rate*, is flagged.

Ranking the 900 problems by the contaminated model's log-likelihood of the right answer separated leaked from fresh with an AUC of 0.621 at $k = 32$, and 0.545 at $k = 2$. In the output above the controls, where nothing leaked, sit near 0.5, as they should. Set to flag 5% of the fresh problems, the detector flagged 3.3% of the leaked ones at $k = 32$: no better than flagging at random.

### Why the trace is lost among the items

The leak did leave a trace. The difficulty is that problems differ from one another in how likely their answers are by far more than the leak changed them. At $k = 32$ the per-problem log-likelihoods had a standard deviation of 1.22, while the leak raised the leaked set's average by 0.51 relative to the fresh set. An easy multiplication's answer is likely whether or not it leaked, and a hard mixed-operation answer is unlikely whether or not it leaked. A threshold on the raw likelihood therefore mostly sorts problems by difficulty, and the leak shifts the leaked problems, on average, only a fraction of the way across that spread.

### Comparing with a reference model

Carlini and colleagues' remedy, in 2022, was to remove the item-to-item variation by comparing each item's likelihood under the model with its likelihood under *reference models* that did not see it. An item that is easy for every model stops looking suspicious; what remains is how much more likely the tested model finds it than a comparable model would. Here the uncontaminated starting model served as the reference, and the problems whose likelihood rose most were flagged. The AUC was 0.745 at $k = 32$, and at the same 5% false-flag rate the detector caught 24% of the leaked problems.

That is better, but still far from naming the leaked items, because extra training moves each problem's likelihood by its own amount whether or not the problem leaked. Even in the controls the change from the starting model had a standard deviation of 0.65 per problem, against 0.67 at $k = 32$. And a reference model of the same lineage that is known not to have seen the benchmark is exactly what an outside evaluator does not have. Methods built for large models, such as the *Min-K% probability* of Shi and colleagues, which scores an item by the average log-probability of its least likely tokens, refine the per-item score but face the same item-to-item variation.

## As a set, the leak is visible

The question an evaluator usually needs answered is not "which items leaked?" but "is this benchmark's score inflated?", and that question has much more data behind it. It can be answered by comparing the benchmark as a whole with a fresh set from the same source: the same generator and tasks, with problems that cannot have leaked because they were written afterwards or never published.

The comparison here is on the mean log-likelihood per problem, leaked set minus fresh set, scaled by its standard error to give a *Welch t-statistic*,

$$
t = \frac{\bar x_{\text{leaked}} - \bar x_{\text{fresh}}}{\sqrt{s_{\text{leaked}}^2/n_{\text{leaked}} + s_{\text{fresh}}^2/n_{\text{fresh}}}},
$$

where $\bar x$, $s$ and $n$ are each set's mean, standard deviation and size. Welch's form does not assume the two sets have equal variances. The mechanism that defeated the item-level detector works in this test's favour. The per-item spread is large, but the standard error of a mean over hundreds of items is that spread divided by the square root of the number of items, so a shift that is small relative to one item's variation can be large relative to the uncertainty in a set's average.

The five controls gave t-statistics between -0.25 and 0.71. Every contaminated run from $k = 2$ on gave more than 2, outside the controls' range though not far outside it at $k = 2$ and 4 (2.22 and 2.31), and at $k = 32$ it was 7.4. Accuracy told the same story with more noise: the gap between the leaked and fresh sets was -0.018 to +0.060 in the controls and +0.268 on average at $k = 32$. Neither test says which problems leaked. Both say that the score on this benchmark is not the score on the task. The table below lists every run.

This is what Zhang and colleagues did for grade-school arithmetic in 2024. They wrote new problems in the style of a popular benchmark, GSM8k, and compared models' accuracy on the two sets, finding clear drops for some model families and little for others. The method needs no access to training data and no reference model, only the discipline to keep some problems unpublished, which Chapter 45 asked of test sets in general when it showed that a test set answers honestly only the first time it is used.

A leaked benchmark inflates the score, and so, as the next chapter shows, can something much more ordinary: the text placed in front of the question.

## In practice

- **Keep a fresh set.** For any benchmark you rely on, write or generate problems in its style that have never been published, and hold them back. They are the only contamination test that needs nothing from the model's developer.
- **Compare whole sets, not items.** Test whether the benchmark's mean score or mean log-likelihood differs from the fresh set's, with a standard error, rather than scanning items for suspicious ones.
- **Do not read a clean per-item scan as evidence that nothing leaked.** At two insertions the model-alone detector here scored an AUC of 0.545, close to chance, although the benchmark score was already above every control's.
- **Check that the fresh set matches.** Confirm on a model that cannot have seen either set that the fresh problems are as hard as the benchmark's; otherwise a difference in difficulty will masquerade as contamination, or hide it.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Verbatim contamination.** Every leaked line was the exact problem and answer. Real contamination includes paraphrases, translations and solutions without the question, which can raise scores while leaving even less trace in the likelihood of the exact item.
- **A small model and a short exposure.** A large model memorises single examples more readily, so its per-item signal may be stronger, and a benchmark seen across many training epochs leaves more trace than one inserted into a single extra pass.
- **Arithmetic answers.** The answers are a few characters long, which gives a per-item likelihood little to work with. Longer answers carry more evidence per item, which helps per-item detectors.
- **A fresh set that matches.** The set-level test is only as good as the match between the fresh problems and the benchmark. Before any training, the leaked set here was already slightly easier than the fresh set, which the controls absorb; an evaluator without controls needs the fresh set to be drawn the way the benchmark was.

## Key points

1. With each benchmark problem inserted into training 32 times, the benchmark score rose from a control mean of 0.551 to 0.800, while accuracy on fresh problems of the same kinds stayed within 0.513 to 0.542.
2. Runs differing only in seed scored 0.503 to 0.577 on the same benchmark; a single run is a sample.
3. At 32 insertions the contaminated model's likelihood identified leaked problems with an AUC of 0.621, catching 3% of them at 5% false flags; with the clean model as a reference, 0.745 and 24%. Item-to-item variation in difficulty swamps the leak.
4. Compared as sets with fresh problems from the same source, every contaminated run from two insertions on stood out from all five controls.
5. Test for contamination by keeping a fresh set the model cannot have seen and comparing whole sets, and do not read a clean per-item scan as evidence that nothing leaked.

## Further reading

- Yeom, Giacomelli, Fredrikson and Jha, "Privacy risk in machine learning: analyzing the connection to overfitting", *CSF* (2018): the loss-threshold membership inference attack and its link to overfitting.
- Carlini et al., "Membership inference attacks from first principles", *IEEE S&P* (2022): calibrating each example's likelihood against reference models, and evaluating detectors at low false-positive rates.
- Shi et al., "Detecting pretraining data from large language models", *ICLR* (2024): the Min-K% probability score for detecting whether a text was in a language model's training data.
- Zhang et al., "A careful examination of large language model performance on grade school arithmetic" (2024): new problems written in the style of GSM8k, used to measure how much models' scores depend on having seen the benchmark.
