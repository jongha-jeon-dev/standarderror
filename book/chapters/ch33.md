# The Curse of Dimensionality Counts the Dimensions the Data Use

Chapter 32 clustered by distance and found that the clusters depended on what the distance could see. Nearest-neighbour methods depend on distance even more directly: to classify a point, they find the training points closest to it and take a vote among them. The textbook warning attached to these methods is the *curse of dimensionality*, the collection of ways in which methods that work in a few dimensions degrade as the number of dimensions grows. For distances it is usually stated as the sentence under test: *in high dimensions, nearest neighbours stop meaning anything*. A practitioner meets it as a reason to avoid distance-based methods on wide tables, or to reduce dimension first. If it is false as usually applied, good methods are ruled out by a column count; if true, distance-based results on wide data are close to random.

This chapter first states the warning precisely and measures it on uniform points, where it holds. It then finds that the 64-pixel handwritten digits behave like far fewer dimensions, and explains why. It next adds columns of pure noise, which reproduce the curse for a nearest-neighbour classifier much more than for logistic regression. Finally it removes most of the noise with a feature-selection step, and distance becomes useful again.

## Distances concentrate when points fill their space

A *k-nearest-neighbour classifier* (k-NN) predicts a point's class by the majority class among the $k$ training points nearest to it, here in *Euclidean distance*, the square root of the sum of squared differences over all columns. Throughout this chapter $k = 5$.

Beyer and colleagues gave the warning a precise form. For a wide class of distributions, as the number of dimensions $d$ grows, the distance from a query point to its nearest neighbour and the distance to its farthest become nearly equal, so that "nearest" no longer singles anything out. The mechanism is easy to see for points spread uniformly over a cube. The squared Euclidean distance between two such points is a sum of $d$ independent terms, one per coordinate. Its mean grows in proportion to $d$, but its standard deviation grows only in proportion to $\sqrt{d}$, so the spread of distances relative to their typical size shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$. In many dimensions almost every pair of points is at almost the same distance. This shrinking of relative spread is called the *concentration of distances*.

Two ratios measure it. The first is the distance to the nearest point divided by the median distance to all points: near 0 when the nearest point is much nearer than the rest, near 1 when it is not. The second is the gap between the farthest and the nearest distance as a multiple of the nearest, $(D_{\max} - D_{\min}) / D_{\min}$, the quantity in Beyer and colleagues' result. The code computes both, averaged over query points, for 1,000 points uniform on the unit cube in dimensions from 1 to 1,024.

```python
from standarderror.ml import around

for r in around.contrast():          # 1,000 uniform points per d
    print(f"d = {r['d']:5d}: nearest / median distance {r['ratio']:.3f}, "
          f"(farthest - nearest) / nearest {r['spread']:.2f}")
```

```text
d =     1: nearest / median distance 0.001, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 2011.69
d =     2: nearest / median distance 0.028, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 70.79
d =     4: nearest / median distance 0.144, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 11.06
d =     8: nearest / median distance 0.339, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 3.55
d =    16: nearest / median distance 0.526, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 1.66
d =    64: nearest / median distance 0.774, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 0.56
d =   256: nearest / median distance 0.886, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 0.25
d =  1024: nearest / median distance 0.944, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 0.12
```

In two dimensions the nearest of 1,000 points is at 0.03 of the median distance, and the farthest is 71 times farther than the nearest. In 1,024 dimensions the nearest is at 0.94 of the median, and the farthest is only 12% farther than the nearest. Every point is about equally far from every other. A vote among the "nearest" five is then a vote among five points chosen almost at random.

So the sentence is true of uniform points. Whether it is true of data is another question. The figure below plots the first ratio for uniform points, with the digits of the next section marked against the curve.

## Sixty-four pixels behave like eight dimensions

The digits data are 1,797 images of handwritten digits, each an 8 × 8 grid of 64 pixel intensities, here divided by 16 so that every pixel lies between 0 and 1. By column count this is a high-dimensional data set. The code computes the same nearest-to-median ratio for the digits, together with the five-fold cross-validated accuracy of 5-NN and of logistic regression. It then appends columns of uniform noise on the same 0-to-1 scale to each image, unrelated to the digit, and repeats the measurements; the first column of the output is the total number of columns.

```python
for r in around.neighbours_noise():  # digits plus uniform noise columns
    print(f"{r['d']:5d} columns: nearest / median {r['ratio']:.3f}, "
          f"5-NN {r['knn']:.3f}, logistic {r['logistic']:.3f}")
```

```text
   64 columns: nearest / median 0.339, 5-NN 0.986, logistic 0.969
   80 columns: nearest / median 0.504, 5-NN 0.981, logistic 0.960
  128 columns: nearest / median 0.695, 5-NN 0.960, logistic 0.950
  320 columns: nearest / median 0.859, 5-NN 0.867, logistic 0.924
 1088 columns: nearest / median 0.938, 5-NN 0.659, logistic 0.871
```

On the digits alone, the first row, the nearest image is at 0.34 of the median distance. Uniform points reach that ratio at about 8 dimensions, not 64. And 5-NN, the method the curse is supposed to defeat, scores 0.986, higher than logistic regression's 0.969.

### Why the pixels do not fill their dimensions

The 64 pixels are not 64 independent directions of variation. Pen strokes are continuous, so neighbouring pixels rise and fall together. Most of the grid is blank for most digits, so many pixels barely vary. And the images of one digit differ from each other in a few ways, such as slant, stroke thickness and size, rather than in 64 independent ways. The points therefore occupy a thin, curved region inside the 64-dimensional cube, and distances measured within that region behave like distances in a much smaller space. The concentration argument above needed the coordinates to vary independently; when they move together, the effective number of terms in the sum is small.

Levina and Bickel call the dimension of such a region the *intrinsic dimension* of the data and estimate it from exactly this kind of nearest-neighbour statistic.

The second ratio agrees. For the digits, the gap between a point's farthest and nearest neighbours, as a multiple of the nearest distance, is 3.0; for uniform points it is 3.6 in 8 dimensions and 0.56 in 64. By either measure the digits look like a space of a handful of dimensions, not of sixty-four. What matters for the curse is not how many columns the data have but how many directions they actually spread in.

## Columns that carry nothing bring the curse back

The noise columns show where the curse does bite. Each fills its dimension, which the pixels do not, and is unrelated to the digit. Sixteen of them barely matter. With 256, the ratio is 0.86 and 5-NN falls to 0.867. With 1,024, the ratio is 0.94, about what 1,024 uniform dimensions give, and 5-NN scores 0.659.

The mechanism is that Euclidean distance weighs every column equally. The squared distance between two images is the sum of the squared differences in the pixels and the squared differences in the noise. The pixel part is unchanged; the noise part grows with the number of noise columns and varies at random from pair to pair. Once the noise columns outnumber the pixels sixteen to one, the distance between two images is mostly the distance between their noise, and the nearest neighbours are nearest in noise. The figure below shows the accuracy of the two classifiers as noise columns are added.

![With 1,024 noise columns nearest neighbours score 0.659 and logistic regression 0.871.](ml133-f1-accuracy.png){alt="Two falling curves: nearest neighbours start higher and fall steeply past a few hundred columns; logistic regression starts a little lower and falls much less."}

Logistic regression suffers far less, falling from 0.969 to 0.871. It learns a weight for every column, and can give the noise columns weights near zero, which a plain distance cannot do. The drop for 5-NN is 3.3 times the drop for logistic regression. The practical curse is therefore a curse on methods that treat every column as equally informative, applied to data with many uninformative columns. ## Removing the noise from the distance restores the neighbours

If the trouble is columns that carry nothing, the repair is to keep them out of the distance. The bluntest version is *univariate feature selection*: score each column separately and keep the best. Here each column is scored by its analysis-of-variance *F statistic*, the ratio of the spread of the column's class means around the overall mean (the between-class mean square) to its variance within classes, which is large when the column's values differ between digit classes. The 64 columns with the largest F are kept, and the selection is made inside each training fold so that the test images play no part in it; Chapter 3 measures how much a selection step fitted on all the rows can leak.

```python
r = around.neighbours_repair()   # 1,024 noise columns, keep 64 by F
print(f"5-NN on the {r['keep']} selected columns: {r['knn']:.3f}; "
      f"real pixels among them: {r['real_share']:.0%}")
```

```text
5-NN on the 64 selected columns: 0.982; real pixels among them: 88%
```

With the 1,024 noise columns still in the data, 5-NN on the 64 kept columns scores 0.982, against 0.659 without selection and 0.986 on the clean digits. The selection is not perfect: 88% of the columns it keeps are real pixels, and the rest are noise columns that happened to differ between classes by chance in the training folds. It is enough. Distance is useful again as soon as most of it is computed on columns that carry information.

That is the honest reading of the curse. It is not a property of a number of columns, which a selection step can change without changing the problem. It is a property of how the information in the data is spread across the columns. Many columns, few of them informative, is the hard case. Many columns, all of them moving together with the thing being predicted, is the easy one, and the digits are much closer to it.

The chapters so far in this part examined steps applied to the inputs. Chapter 34 turns to the output of a model, its predicted probabilities, and to the routine step of recalibrating them.

## In practice

- **Measure before blaming dimension.** For a sample of rows, compute each row's nearest distance divided by its median distance to the rest, and compare with uniform data in a range of dimensions. The matching uniform dimension is a rough guide to how many directions your data really use.
- **Do not rule out distance-based methods by column count.** On 64 correlated pixels 5-NN beat logistic regression.
- **Look for columns that carry nothing.** Irrelevant columns enter an unweighted distance on equal terms with informative ones. Remove them, down-weight them, or scale features so that the distance reflects what matters.
- **Do any selection inside the cross-validation folds.** Choosing columns on all the rows lets the test rows influence the choice.
- **When many columns may be irrelevant, use a method that weights or selects them.** Logistic regression, which learns per-column weights, tolerated 1,024 noise columns far better than an unweighted distance, and 5-NN after selection inside the folds did better still.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Uniform noise is the worst kind.** Real irrelevant features are often correlated with each other or nearly constant, and fill fewer dimensions than independent uniform columns; they hurt less.
- **Euclidean distance, unweighted.** Scaling or selecting features, learning a metric, or using a representation from a trained model all change what "near" means, and are the standard repairs. None was applied here, by design, apart from the selection step.
- **One data set with low intrinsic dimension.** Data that genuinely spread in many directions, such as some text or genomic data, can be nearly as hard as the uniform case. The column count does not say which case a data set is in; the distance ratio, or an intrinsic-dimension estimate, does.
- **$k = 5$, one split scheme.** Other choices of $k$ move the numbers a little and not the pattern.

## Key points

1. For points that fill their space, distances concentrate: the nearest-to-median ratio rose from 0.03 in 2 uniform dimensions to 0.94 in 1,024.
2. Real data rarely fill their columns. The digits' 64 pixels had the ratio of about 8 uniform dimensions, and 5-NN scored 0.986.
3. Irrelevant columns do fill their dimensions. With 1,024 noise columns 5-NN fell to 0.659; logistic regression, which weights columns, fell to 0.871.
4. Selecting 64 columns by F statistic inside the folds, with the noise still present, restored 5-NN to 0.982.
5. Before blaming dimension, measure the nearest-to-median distance ratio of your data, and remove or down-weight columns that carry nothing.
