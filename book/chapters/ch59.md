# An Anomaly Score Ranks; the Contamination Setting Only Cuts

Anomaly detection is the unsupervised task with the most practical pull: fraud nobody has labelled yet, a failing machine, a data-entry error among a million rows. With no labels there is no model of the anomaly, only a model of normality, and a point is flagged for not fitting it. In scikit-learn a detector is one line, and its output is a flag on each row, anomalous or not. That output invites the sentence under test in this chapter: *an anomaly detector finds the anomalies in your data*. If the sentence is false, the rows a detector flags are not the anomalies but something else, a share fixed in advance or the points that happen to be unusual in the features supplied, and a team that investigates the flags and nothing else will both waste effort and miss what it was looking for.

The chapter first defines the two detectors used, the isolation forest and the local outlier factor, and the measures used to score them. It then plants anomalies whose number and nature are known and asks three questions separately: how many points get flagged, how well the score ranks the planted anomalies, and what in the data changes that ranking, first anomalies that arrive in a group and then anomalies hidden among many irrelevant features. Finally it replicates the ranking question on the breast-cancer data, with malignant tumours hidden among benign ones as the anomalies to find.

## Two detectors and how to score them

The *isolation forest* of Liu, Ting and Zhou builds many random trees, each on a subsample of the data. Each tree splits the data recursively by choosing a feature at random and a split value at random between that feature's minimum and maximum, until each point sits alone in a leaf or a depth limit is reached. A point that lies far from the rest is separated by few random splits; a point inside a dense mass needs many. The anomaly score is based on the average depth at which a point is isolated, normalised so that short paths give high scores. The forests here have 100 trees.

The *local outlier factor* (LOF) of Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander compares a point's local density with the densities of its neighbours. Each point's *local reachability density* is, roughly, the inverse of its average distance to its $k$ nearest neighbours. The LOF of a point is the average ratio of its neighbours' densities to its own: about 1 for a point as dense as its surroundings, much larger for a point in a sparse spot next to a dense region. A simpler score used below as a comparison is the plain *k-NN distance*, the distance from a point to its $k$-th nearest neighbour.

A detector produces two things: a continuous score and a flag obtained by cutting the score at a threshold. They are scored separately here. The ranking is scored by the *AUC*, the area under the ROC curve, which equals the probability that a randomly chosen planted anomaly receives a higher score than a randomly chosen normal point: 1 for a perfect ranking, 0.5 for a random one, and below 0.5 for a ranking that puts anomalies systematically *below* normal points. The flags are scored by *precision*, the share of flagged points that are truly anomalous, and *recall*, the share of true anomalies that are flagged.

## The contamination setting decides how many are flagged

scikit-learn's detectors take a `contamination` parameter, and it reads like an estimate of how much of the data is anomalous. It is not an estimate. It sets the threshold on the score so that exactly that share of the training points is flagged. The first simulation has 5,000 standard Gaussian points in five dimensions, with a share of them, 0.5%, 2% or 10%, replaced by anomalies shifted by 2.5 in every coordinate with random signs. The block below fits an isolation forest at two true anomaly rates with two contamination settings, 5% and the default `'auto'`, and prints the flagged share and the precision.

```python
from standarderror.ml import unsup

for r in unsup.anomaly_contamination(rates=(0.005, 0.1),
                                     settings=(0.05, "auto")):
    for c in ("0.05", "auto"):
        v = r[c]
        print(f"true {r['rate']:5.1%}  contamination={c:4s}  flagged "
              f"{v['flagged']:.3f}  precision {v['precision']:.2f}")
```

```text
true  0.5%  contamination=0.05  flagged 0.050  precision 0.10
true  0.5%  contamination=auto  flagged 0.134  precision 0.04
true 10.0%  contamination=0.05  flagged 0.050  precision 1.00
true 10.0%  contamination=auto  flagged 0.118  precision 0.84
```

With 0.5% of the points truly anomalous, a setting of 5% flagged 5% and gave a precision of 0.10: nine flags in ten were normal points. That is arithmetic, not a fault of the score: when the cut admits ten times as many points as there are anomalies, at most one flag in ten can be right. In the other direction, with 10% truly anomalous, a setting of 1% gave perfect precision and caught 10% of them; the cut was too tight to admit more. The default, `'auto'`, does not adapt to the data either. It applies a fixed threshold to the isolation forest's score, and here it flagged 10.5% to 13.4% of the points whether the truth was 0.5%, 2% or 10%. Underneath every one of those settings, the score ranked the planted anomalies with an AUC of 0.99 or more.

So the detector's real output is a ranking, and the flag is a cut through it that someone chose. Chapter 46 made the same point for classifiers: the threshold that turns a score into a decision is a decision about costs, not a property of the model. Here there is not even a calibrated probability to anchor the cut. Choosing it needs either a labelled sample, from which precision at each cut can be estimated, or a budget, the number of cases someone can afford to investigate. ## A crowd of anomalies looks normal from inside

The ranking depends on what the score compares a point with. The second simulation plants fifty anomalies, all as if from one fault, packed tightly together (standard deviation 0.1, centred at (5, 5)) far from 2,000 normal points. A local method that looks at a point's $k$ nearest neighbours sees, for any $k$ up to 49, only other anomalies, densely packed. The block compares LOF and the k-NN distance at two neighbourhood sizes, and the isolation forest.

```python
m = unsup.anomaly_masking(ks=(20, 100))      # a group of 50 anomalies
for r in m["rows"]:
    print(f"k={r['k']:3d}  LOF AUC {r['lof']:.2f}  k-NN distance AUC "
          f"{r['knn']:.2f}")
print(f"isolation forest AUC {m['iforest']:.2f}")
```

```text
k= 20  LOF AUC 0.54  k-NN distance AUC 0.17
k=100  LOF AUC 1.00  k-NN distance AUC 1.00
isolation forest AUC 1.00
```

With $k$ of 5 or 20, LOF scored the group with an AUC between 0.54 and 0.58, barely better than a coin. The plain distance to the $k$-th neighbour did worse, between 0.17 and 0.19: by that score the anomalies looked *more* normal than the normal points, because they were more tightly packed than any normal neighbourhood. Chapter 66, which reran single-draw results from across the book on fresh seeds, repeated this on five other seeds; the distance score's worst AUC ranged from 0.19 to 0.24, always far below chance.

Once the neighbourhood is larger than the group, the picture changes. At $k = 50$ each anomaly's $k$-th neighbour is outside the group, far away, and the distance score found them perfectly; LOF still did not (0.57). At $k = 100$ both did. The isolation forest, which subsamples the data and isolates points with random splits rather than comparing neighbours, scored 1.00 throughout: a tight group far from the bulk is still cut off from it by a few splits.

This is *masking*, an old problem in outlier statistics: one anomaly is conspicuous, but many similar ones form a cluster and shield one another from detection. A fault that produces many identical bad records is exactly that case. The figure below plots the AUC against the neighbourhood size.

![AUC of three detectors against the neighbourhood size k for a group of 50 anomalies. At k = 20 the distance score's AUC was 0.17: the anomalies looked more normal than the normal points.](ml159-f1-masking.png){alt="Two curves near or below 0.5 for k of 5 and 20; the distance score reaches 1 at k = 50 and the outlier factor at 100; a flat line at 1 for the isolation forest."}

## Two features that matter among many that do not

An anomaly is usually unusual in a few features, not in all of them. The third simulation shifts 40 anomalies among 2,000 points by 3.5 standard deviations in two features, and gives the data 2, 10, 50 or 200 features in all; the extra features are pure noise. Averaged over six draws of the data and the forest, the isolation forest's AUC went from 1.00 to 0.96, 0.76 and 0.58. Single draws at 200 features ranged between 0.50 and 0.68: little or no better than chance. Given only the two relevant features, it scored 0.997.

The isolation forest's construction suggests why. Each random split chooses a feature at random, so with two relevant features among 200, almost every split is spent on noise, and an anomaly is isolated little faster than a normal point. The control on the two relevant features alone supports that explanation without measuring it directly.

LOF behaved differently. Its AUC rose from 0.67 with two features to 0.87 with 50 and fell to 0.75 with 200. The fall is the dilution Chapter 33 measured for nearest neighbours: distances computed over many dimensions still carry the two shifted features, but less and less as noise dimensions are added. The low start is probably the masking of the previous section. In two dimensions the 40 anomalies form a little cluster of their own, and LOF looks at 20 neighbours, fewer than the group; extra noise features spread the anomalies apart and break the cluster up.

Zimek, Schubert and Kriegel's survey of outlier detection in high dimensions makes the general case: irrelevant attributes hide anomalies that exist only in a subspace, and which method suffers most depends on how it uses distances. The practical lesson is older than either method: a detector can only find what the features make unusual, so the choice of features is most of the model. The table below collects the averages.

## Malignant tumours among benign ones

The replication uses the breast-cancer data. It takes the 357 benign tumours, adds eight malignant ones drawn at random, standardises all 30 features, and repeats the draw 20 times. The isolation forest ranked the eight with an AUC of 0.95 on average, between 0.85 and 0.99 across draws; LOF averaged 0.93. Malignant tumours are larger and more irregular, so in these features they are unusual, and an unsupervised score finds most of them.

Turning the ranking into flags shows the cost of having no labels. Flagging 5% of the 365 tumours, about eighteen, caught 71% of the malignant ones on average, but between 38% and 88% depending on which eight were drawn and on the forest's seed, with a precision of 0.30. Which malignant cases look unusual, and which benign ones also do, decides the result, and nothing in the procedure says which it will be on a given day.

An anomaly detector, then, ranks rows by how poorly they fit a description of normality built from the features and neighbourhoods it is given, and someone else decides where to cut. The next chapter returns to clustering and asks whether the number of clusters can be chosen by how reproducible the clustering is.

## In practice

- **Treat the score as the output and the flag as your decision.** Keep the full ranking; do not let `contamination` or `'auto'` choose how many rows are flagged.
- **Choose the cut from a budget or a labelled sample.** Flag as many rows as can be investigated, or label a sample and estimate precision at several cuts. Do not read the contamination setting as an estimate of the anomaly rate.
- **Ask whether anomalies could arrive in groups.** If one fault can produce many similar records, a local method needs a neighbourhood larger than the group; check results at several values of $k$, or compare with an isolation forest.
- **Select features before detecting.** Anomalies unusual in a few features are diluted by many irrelevant ones. Use the features in which an anomaly would be expected to show, and compare with a run on that subset.
- **Inspect the top of the ranking.** Look at the flagged rows and those just below the cut, preferably with someone who knows the data, before trusting either the score or the cut.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Planted anomalies.** The simulated anomalies were shifted versions of normal points, the easiest kind to define. Real anomalies include points that are individually ordinary and unusual only in combination or in sequence, which these detectors do not look for.
- **Scores on the training data.** Every score here was computed on the data the detector was fitted to, which is how the tools are commonly used. Fitting on clean data and scoring new data would presumably remove some masking, since the group would not be among the neighbours, and none of the feature problem; neither was measured.
- **Two detectors, default settings.** Other methods, such as one-class support vector machines or autoencoders, use distances or reconstructions differently and fail differently. The masking and irrelevant-feature effects were measured for these two.
- **Breast-cancer features are good ones.** Its 30 features were chosen by people who knew what malignancy looks like. Features chosen without that knowledge would be closer to the 200-feature simulation.

## Key points

1. The contamination parameter flagged exactly its own value; `'auto'` flagged 10.5% to 13.4% whatever the true rate. The ranking underneath scored an AUC of 0.99 or more throughout.
2. Fifty grouped anomalies looked normal to local scores until the neighbourhood was larger than the group; at $k = 20$ the distance score's AUC was 0.17, and on five other seeds its worst AUC stayed far below chance.
3. Anomalous in 2 features of 200, the planted anomalies were found by the isolation forest with an average AUC of 0.58; on the two features alone, 0.997.
4. Eight malignant tumours among 357 benign ones were ranked well (average AUC 0.95), but flagging 5% caught between 38% and 88% of them depending on the draw and the forest's seed.
5. Treat an anomaly score as a ranking under assumptions about features and neighbourhoods. Choose how many to flag from a budget or a labelled sample, and check what the top of the ranking actually contains.

## Further reading

- Liu, Ting and Zhou, "Isolation forest", *ICDM* (2008): the original isolation forest and its path-length score.
- Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander, "LOF: identifying density-based local outliers", *SIGMOD* (2000): the local outlier factor and the idea of comparing a point's density with its neighbours'.
- Zimek, Schubert and Kriegel, "A survey on unsupervised outlier detection in high-dimensional numerical data", *Statistical Analysis and Data Mining* (2012): how irrelevant attributes hide anomalies and how different detectors suffer.
