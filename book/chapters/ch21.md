# Prediction Intervals and Fairness

The previous chapter asked whether a deployed model is still right on average. This chapter asks two questions that an average can hide, both of which a practitioner meets as soon as a model's outputs are used to make decisions. The first is about a single prediction: how far off might it be? The second is about the people the predictions are made for: does a model that is right overall treat each group of them the same way?

Both questions have a standard answer that is checked in aggregate. For a regression, the standard answer is a band around each prediction, sized from the spread of the residuals and read as the range that will contain the outcome with the stated probability. For a classifier that makes decisions about people, the standard fairness check is calibration within each group: a predicted risk should mean the same observed rate in every group. Each answer can pass its aggregate check and still fail in a way that matters, and the two sections test one each:

- Section 21.1: *a prediction plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covers 95% of new outcomes.*
- Section 21.2: *a model that is calibrated within every group treats the groups the same.*

Section 21.1 works with a regression whose noise grows across the range of its input. It shows why the textbook band falls short even on average, introduces split conformal prediction as the version that keeps its promise overall, and then slices coverage by input. The guarantee turns out to be marginal, an average over inputs: a band of fixed width is too wide where the noise is small and too narrow where it is large. Conformalised quantile regression, a band whose width varies, repairs this when there are enough data to learn how the spread varies, and a small real data set on diabetes progression shows what happens when there are not.

Section 21.2 makes the same move with groups of people instead of slices of an input. In a simulated world where the outcome depends on risk alone and never on the group, a model calibrated in both groups still gives them very different error rates at one threshold. The section explains why this follows from arithmetic when the groups' base rates differ, through the impossibility theorems of Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan and of Chouldechova, and measures what the two common responses cost: removing the group from the inputs, and setting a separate threshold for each group.

The sections build on each other through one idea. A property that holds on average, coverage in the first section and calibration in the second, says little about how errors fall on particular slices or groups, and the slice that fails is often the one where the stakes are highest. In both, a repair exists only by adding something the data do not give for free: an assumption that the noise's spread can be learned, or a choice, made by people, of which inequality to accept. Both sections also lean on earlier chapters: the calibration of Section 11.3, the thresholds of Section 12.1, and the drift of Section 20.1, under which the conformal guarantee itself lapses.

## A Prediction Interval Is Right on Average and Wrong Where It Matters

<!-- source: ml-62-intervals; hero: ml162-hero.png -->


A regression's prediction is a number, and anyone acting on it needs to know how far off it might be: a delivery estimate, a price, a dose. The standard answer is a band of fixed width around every prediction, plus or minus 1.96 times the standard deviation of the residuals, read as the range that will contain the outcome 95% of the time. The sentence under test is that reading: *a prediction plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covers 95% of new outcomes*. If it fails, the band misleads, and it misleads most where the outcomes are largest and least predictable.

Most of this book has been about classifiers; this section is about regression. It checks whether the textbook band and its distribution-free replacement, split conformal prediction, deliver 95% overall, then slices the bands by input to show where a fixed width fails, tests a repair whose width varies, and repeats the comparison on a small real data set.

### The test: a regression whose noise grows


The *coverage* of an interval method is the proportion of new outcomes that fall inside their intervals. It is compared with the *nominal level*, the 95% claimed.

The test uses a regression where the truth is known: $y = \sin x + x/2 + \varepsilon$, with $x$ spread evenly from 0 to 10 and the noise $\varepsilon$ having a standard deviation that rises from 0.2 at the left to 3.2 at the right. Noise whose spread changes with the inputs is called *heteroscedastic*. It is common, since larger quantities usually vary more: a large claim more than a small one.

A gradient-boosted model fits the curve on 2,000 rows. Each band is checked on 20,000 fresh rows, overall and in each tenth of the input's range, and everything is averaged over 20 data sets. The code below builds the split conformal band, defined below, on one data set and prints its coverage overall and at the two ends of the range.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from standarderror.ml import deploy

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X, y = deploy._hetero(2000, rng)        # noise sd 0.2 + 0.3 x, x in [0, 10]
Xc, yc = deploy._hetero(1000, rng)      # calibration rows
Xt, yt = deploy._hetero(20000, rng)     # fresh rows
model = GradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(X, y)
q = np.quantile(np.abs(yc - model.predict(Xc)), 0.95 * (1 + 1 / 1000))
inside = np.abs(yt - model.predict(Xt)) <= q   # split conformal
print(f"overall {inside.mean():.3f}; x < 1: {inside[Xt[:, 0] < 1].mean():.3f}"
      f"; x > 9: {inside[Xt[:, 0] > 9].mean():.3f}")
```

```text
overall 0.969; x < 1: 1.000; x > 9: 0.864
```

On this one data set the band covered every outcome with $x$ below 1 and 86.4% of those above 9. The figures below are averages over all 20 data sets.

### Overall coverage: the conformal band keeps its promise


#### Why the textbook band falls short

Built from the training residuals, the band covered 90.8% of new outcomes. The model had fitted some of the training noise, so its residuals on the training rows were smaller than its errors on new rows, and a band sized from them was too narrow.

Built from residuals on 1,000 held-out rows, it covered 93.3%. The remaining gap has a different cause. The rule "1.96 standard deviations covers 95%" is a property of the normal distribution. The residuals here are a mixture of narrow noise from the left of the range and wide noise from the right, and the 95% point of such a mixture is not 1.96 of its standard deviations. The standard deviation is the wrong summary of a distribution whose spread varies.

#### Split conformal prediction

*Split conformal prediction*, set out by Vovk and colleagues and developed for regression by Lei and colleagues, makes one change. Fit the model on one part of the data. On a separate *calibration set* of $n$ rows, compute the absolute residuals $|y_i - \hat f(x_i)|$. Instead of their standard deviation, take their empirical quantile at level

$$
(1 - \alpha)\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right),
$$

call it $\hat q$, and report $\hat f(x) \pm \hat q$ for every new $x$. The factor $1 + 1/n$ is a small finite-sample correction; it appears in the code as `0.95 * (1 + 1 / 1000)`.

The method has a guarantee that needs no model to be right. Suppose the new row and the calibration rows are *exchangeable*: their joint distribution is unchanged by reordering them, as holds when all are independent draws from one distribution. Then the new row's residual is equally likely to fall at any rank among the calibration residuals, so it exceeds the quantile chosen above with probability at most $\alpha$. The band covers at least $1 - \alpha$ of new outcomes on average, whatever the model and whatever the noise.

It delivered: 95.1%, with a standard deviation of 0.008 across the 20 data sets, about what a 1,000-row calibration set allows. On the question the sentence asks, the conformal band is the honest version of the textbook one. The table below collects the overall and worst-tenth coverage and the widths of all the bands.

### Coverage by input: a fixed width fails where the noise is


#### Marginal and conditional coverage

The conformal guarantee is about *marginal coverage*, the average over all inputs. What a user acting on one prediction usually wants is *conditional coverage*: the probability of covering the outcome given this particular $x$. A band of one width around noise of varying width must be too wide where the noise is small and too narrow where it is large, and the average hides both.

In the quietest tenth of inputs every fixed-width band covered more than 99% of outcomes, wider than it needed to be. In the noisiest tenth the training-residual band covered 70.5%, the held-out band 76.3%, a straight line with a held-out band 80.2%, and split conformal **80.9%**. One outcome in five fell outside a "95%" interval exactly where the outcomes were largest and least predictable, which in many applications is where the stakes are.

The straight line, which cannot follow the curve, had larger residuals everywhere and so a wider band, 7.91 against 7.72, and with the same recipe covered the noisiest tenth better than boosting (80.2% against 76.3%). The worse model looked safer there only because it was less sure of itself everywhere.

This is a limit of the problem. Barber, Candès, Ramdas and Tibshirani proved in 2021 that no method can guarantee conditional coverage at every input, with bands of useful width, without assumptions about the distribution. Any repair has to bring an assumption.

#### Conformalised quantile regression

*Conformalised quantile regression* (CQR), from Romano, Patterson and Candès in 2019, brings the assumption that the noise's spread can be learned. *Quantile regression* fits a model for a chosen quantile of $y$ given $x$ instead of its mean, by minimising the *pinball loss*, which penalises under-predictions with weight $\tau$ and over-predictions with weight $1 - \tau$ for quantile level $\tau$. Two boosted models estimate the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles, $\hat q_{\text{lo}}(x)$ and $\hat q_{\text{hi}}(x)$, so the band widens where the data are noisier.

A conformal step then supplies the guarantee. On the calibration rows, compute how far each outcome falls outside its band,

$$
E_i = \max\{\hat q_{\text{lo}}(x_i) - y_i,\; y_i - \hat q_{\text{hi}}(x_i)\},
$$

which is negative when the outcome is inside. Take the conformal quantile $\hat Q$ of these scores and report $[\hat q_{\text{lo}}(x) - \hat Q,\; \hat q_{\text{hi}}(x) + \hat Q]$. The band is widened or narrowed by however much it misses.

CQR covered 95.0% overall and between 93.2% and 96.0% in every tenth, with an average width of 6.76 against split conformal's 8.54: 21% narrower and right almost everywhere, because the width went where the noise was. The figure below shows coverage by tenth for the fixed-width bands and for CQR.

![Coverage in each tenth of the input. Split conformal covers 95.1% overall but **80.9%** in the noisiest tenth; the conformalised quantile band stays near 95% throughout.](ml162-f0-tenths.png){alt="Four curves. Three start at 1.0 on the left and fall steadily to between 0.70 and 0.81 on the right; the conformalised quantile curve stays flat near 0.95."}

### The repair needs data: diabetes progression


The replication uses the diabetes data bundled with scikit-learn: 442 patients, ten baseline variables, and disease progression after a year as the outcome. The level is 90%, and the patients are split into 250 to fit, 100 to calibrate and 92 to check, over 100 random splits. Without a single input to slice by, the code slices by the predicted value, reporting coverage in the thirds of patients with the lowest, middle and highest predictions.

```python
d = deploy.intervals_diabetes(reps=100)
for k in ("split conformal", "CQR"):
    v = d[k]
    print(f"{k:15s} {v['coverage']:.3f} overall; thirds by prediction "
          f"{v['low']:.3f} {v['middle']:.3f} {v['high']:.3f}")
```

```text
split conformal 0.909 overall; thirds by prediction 0.972 0.892 0.863
CQR             0.904 overall; thirds by prediction 0.944 0.916 0.852
```

Split conformal around a linear model covered 90.9% overall, on target. By thirds it covered 97.2% of the patients predicted to progress least and 86.3% of those predicted to progress most: the same pattern as the simulation, presumably because progression varies more among the patients with more of it.

CQR, with shallow boosted trees on 250 patients, covered 94.4% and 85.2% in the outer thirds, no better, with a band 11% wider. Learning how the spread varies takes data, and 250 patients with ten variables were not enough to learn it better than a constant. The repair that worked on 2,000 rows did not transfer to 250.

## Calibrated in Every Group, Unequal in Every Error Rate

<!-- source: ml-63-fairness; hero: ml163-hero.png -->


Section 21.1 found that an interval which keeps its promise on average can break it in the slice of inputs where the stakes are highest. The same question arises when the slices are groups of people. A deployed model makes decisions about people, and people come in groups that the law and the public care about. The first fairness check most teams run is calibration within each group: does a predicted risk of 30% mean 30% for everyone? Section 11.3 showed that a model calibrated overall can fail this check in a group. A model that passes it seems to have earned the sentence under test: *a model that is calibrated within every group treats the groups the same.* If that sentence is false, a team can pass its fairness review while one group bears far more false alarms than another.

This section builds a simulated world in which nobody could call the model biased in its construction, and measures what a calibrated model does to each group's error rates. It then examines the two common responses, removing the group from the inputs and setting a separate threshold for each group, and what each costs. The theorems behind the results show that the conflict is arithmetic, and that the choice among the definitions of fairness cannot be made by the data.

### A world where the model is not biased by construction


#### Five quantities to compare by group

For a binary decision made by thresholding a score, several quantities can be compared between groups. The *base rate* is the share of the group whose outcome is positive. A score is *calibrated within a group* if, among members of that group given a predicted probability $p$, a proportion $p$ have a positive outcome. At a threshold, the *false positive rate* is the share of the group's negatives that are flagged; the *miss rate* (false negative rate) is the share of its positives that are not flagged; and the *precision*, or *positive predictive value*, is the share of flagged members who are truly positive.

#### The simulation

Two groups of equal size differ in one thing: group A's latent risk is lower on average, so its outcome rate is 0.31 against group B's 0.46. The outcome depends on that latent risk alone, never on the group. The model sees a noisy measurement of the risk and, in its first version, the group itself. A logistic model is trained on 40,000 rows and tested on 40,000 more. The code prints, for each group, the outcome rate, the mean predicted probability and the false positive rate at a threshold of 0.5.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from standarderror.ml import deploy

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
g, x1, proxy, y = deploy.fairness_world(40_000, rng)
G, X1, P, Y = deploy.fairness_world(40_000, rng)
m = LogisticRegression().fit(np.c_[x1, g], y)      # group as an input
p = m.predict_proba(np.c_[X1, G])[:, 1]
for k, name in ((0, "A"), (1, "B")):
    s = G == k
    print(f"group {name}: outcome rate {Y[s].mean():.3f}, mean "
          f"prediction {p[s].mean():.3f}, false positive rate "
          f"{np.mean(p[s & (Y == 0)] > 0.5):.3f}")
```

```text
group A: outcome rate 0.310, mean prediction 0.314, false positive rate 0.106
group B: outcome rate 0.463, mean prediction 0.467, false positive rate 0.269
```

The model's probabilities were right in both groups, to within 0.004. By the calibration check, it treats the groups the same.

### A calibrated model has unequal error rates


#### What one threshold does

Apply one threshold, 0.5, to everybody. Among people whose outcome was negative, the model flagged 10.6% in group A and **26.9%** in group B: a member of B with nothing wrong was 2.5 times as likely to be flagged. Among people whose outcome was positive, it missed 60.5% in A and 35.3% in B. A flag was right 62.7% of the time in A and 67.4% in B.

Nothing is wrong with the model. Group B's people have higher latent risk on average, so even B's negatives, the people whose outcome turned out negative, have higher measured risk than A's negatives. More of them sit near the threshold, where noise in the measurement pushes some across it. In reverse, A's positives sit lower and are missed more often. A single threshold applied to two groups with different risk distributions produces different error rates, however accurate the probabilities are. The figure below shows the four comparisons.

![Calibration gaps for both groups are near zero, while the false positive rates are 0.106 and 0.269 and the miss rates 0.605 and **0.353**; positive predictive values also differ.](ml163-f0-rates.png){alt="Pairs of bars for four quantities. The calibration gaps are both near zero; the false positive rates, miss rates and positive predictive values differ between the groups."}

#### The impossibility theorems

Two results from 2016 say this is unavoidable when base rates differ and prediction is imperfect. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan proved that a score cannot be calibrated within groups and also give each group's negatives, and each group's positives, the same average score, except in degenerate cases: equal base rates or perfect prediction.

Chouldechova proved the counterpart for decisions. For any group with base rate $\pi$, the three error quantities at a threshold are tied by an identity that follows from counting the four cells of the confusion matrix:

$$
\text{FPR} = \frac{\pi}{1 - \pi} \cdot \frac{1 - \text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}} \cdot (1 - \text{FNR}),
$$

where FPR is the false positive rate, FNR the miss rate and PPV the precision. If two groups have different $\pi$ and the same PPV and FNR, their FPRs must differ. Unless prediction is perfect, equal precision, equal false positive rates and equal miss rates cannot all hold.

The dispute over the COMPAS recidivism scores in 2016 was this theorem in public, with one side measuring calibration and the other error rates, both correctly.

### Removing the group narrows the gaps and breaks calibration


#### Fairness through unawareness

The most common response is to remove the group from the model's inputs, which Kusner and colleagues called *fairness through unawareness*. Without the group, the false positive rates were 13.7% and 22.9%, and the miss rates 54.9% and 39.8%: closer, not equal.

The gaps remain because the model's score still carries the group. Measured by the *AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen member of B scores above a randomly chosen member of A, the score told the groups apart with an AUC of 0.63. The measured risk differs between the groups, and the score is built from it.

The model also lost the property it started with. Without the group, it predicted +0.028 too high on average in group A and -0.020 in group B. The same measured value means a different expected risk in each group, because the groups' latent risks are distributed differently: given a noisy reading, the best estimate of the true risk is pulled toward the group's own average. A model that cannot see the group averages over both groups, and so overestimates for the lower-risk group and underestimates for the higher-risk one.

#### Proxies restore what was removed

Dwork and colleagues had argued in 2012 that unawareness fails for a different reason: other features encode the group, which is what a *proxy* does. Here a noisy proxy for the group, standing in for something like a postcode, was given to the model in place of the group. The score's AUC for the group came back to 0.66 and the calibration gaps shrank to +0.016 and -0.008. The model rebuilt much of what was removed, at an accuracy of 0.714 against 0.716.

### Equalising error rates moves the gap to precision


#### Separate thresholds

The other response is to equalise the error rates directly, as Hardt, Price and Srebro proposed: keep the score and choose each group's threshold separately. Lowering group A's threshold from 0.5 to 0.342 brought its false positive rate to 0.269, matching B's 0.269. The threshold was chosen on these same rows, so the match is by construction. In this world the miss rates came out equal as well, 0.357 and 0.353.

The price was paid in two places. Accuracy fell from 0.716 to 0.698, because A's threshold no longer sits where its errors are fewest. And the meaning of a flag now depends on the group: a flagged member of A was truly positive 51.8% of the time, a flagged member of B 67.4%. The two groups get equal error rates and unequal precision, where the first model gave them equal calibration and unequal error rates. This is Chouldechova's identity at work: with base rates fixed, equalising FPR and FNR forces PPV apart.

#### A third definition: equal flag rates

A third definition, *demographic parity*, asks for equal shares flagged in each group. It was further away than either: the first model flagged 19.5% of group A and 44.4% of group B, the model without the group 23.4% and 40.2%, and the equal-error-rate thresholds 38.5% and 44.4%. With unequal base rates, equal flag rates would mean flagging fewer of group B's positives or more of group A's negatives than the score supports, a third distribution of errors with its own advocates.

Counting equal false positive and miss rates together as one definition, each of the three rules measured here satisfied at most one of the three definitions. Chouldechova's theorem says that, with unequal base rates and imperfect prediction, no rule can have equal precision and equal error rates together; the data say only what each choice costs. The table below sets the rules side by side.

Section 12.1 put the threshold where the costs of errors said it should be. Here the costs of errors falling on different groups are part of the decision, and they are not a quantity the data can supply. Once a model, a threshold and a definition are chosen, the choice is only as durable as the world it was made in; as Chapter 20 found, a change in the relation between inputs and outcome is visible only in labelled outcomes, and so is any change in the groups' error rates.

Much of the evidence in this chapter, as in much of this book, comes from simulated worlds built to separate one effect from another. The next chapter takes four verdicts that earlier chapters measured only on simulations and retries them on real data.

## In practice

### Prediction intervals (Section 21.1)

- **Replace $\pm 1.96$ training-residual standard deviations with split conformal on held-out rows.** It costs one quantile and delivers nominal marginal coverage under exchangeability.
- **Report coverage in slices, not only overall.** Compute it by bins of the predicted value and of the inputs you suspect drive the noise. A band that meets its level overall can miss badly in the slice where outcomes are largest.
- **Let the width vary only where there are data to learn how.** CQR fixed the slices on 2,000 rows and did not on 250. Check coverage by slice on held-out data before trusting it.
- **Treat over-coverage as a cost**, and do not prefer a worse model because its wider band looks safer in one slice.

### Fairness across groups (Section 21.2)

- **Report calibration and error rates by group together**: the outcome rate, mean predicted probability, false positive rate, miss rate and precision at the threshold in use. A calibration check alone hides the error-rate gaps.
- **Expect a gap when base rates differ.** It is arithmetic, not evidence of a defect in the model. Find out which quantity it falls on.
- **Do not treat removing the group as a remedy.** It narrows the error gaps only partly, breaks calibration within groups, and proxies restore much of what was removed. Measure how well the score predicts the group.
- **If you equalise error rates with group thresholds, report the cost**: the accuracy lost and the precision gap it creates.
- **Make the choice explicitly, with the people affected, and write it down.** If nobody chose, the default threshold chose for you.

## Limits of the evidence

### Prediction intervals (Section 21.1)

- **One shape of noise.** The simulation's noise grows smoothly with one input. Real heteroscedasticity can depend on several inputs or on unmeasured ones, which no model of the spread can follow.
- **Exchangeability.** Every conformal guarantee assumes the new rows are like the calibration rows. Under the drift of Section 20.1 the guarantee lapses, even on average.
- **Thirds of the predictions.** Coverage by predicted value is one way to slice. Slices by any variable can show different gaps, and with enough slices some will miss by chance.
- **One quantile model.** Other ways to let the band vary, such as dividing residuals by a fitted spread, behave similarly in kind. They all need the spread to be learnable.

### Fairness across groups (Section 21.2)

- **A clean world.** Here the outcome depends on risk alone and the labels are accurate. In practice the labels often come from past decisions (Section 15.2, on the outcomes of applicants who were refused and so never observed), the risk can be measured worse in one group, and the group can affect the outcome through channels that are themselves unjust. Each changes which definition is defensible.
- **Two groups, one threshold rule.** Real deployments have many groups, intersections of them, and decisions that are not one threshold.
- **Simulation only.** scikit-learn's bundled data have no protected attribute to test on. The theorem does not need data, but the sizes of the gaps do.
- **Fairness metrics chosen for the demonstration.** Calibration, error rates and precision are three of many definitions. Corbett-Davies and Goel review others, and the trade-offs among them follow the same arithmetic.

## Key points

1. A band of plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covered 90.8% with training residuals and 93.3% with held-out ones; split conformal delivered its promised 95.1%.
2. Conformal coverage is marginal. In the noisiest tenth of inputs every fixed-width band covered 70% to 81%, and in the quietest more than 99%.
3. No method can guarantee coverage at every input without assumptions; conformalised quantile regression assumes the spread is learnable. It covered 93.2% to 96.0% in every tenth with a 21% narrower band on 2,000 rows; on 250 diabetes patients it did no better than a fixed band.
4. Check an interval's coverage in slices that matter, not only overall. Use held-out or conformal calibration, and let the width vary only where there are enough data to learn how.
5. A model calibrated to within 0.004 in both groups made false alarms on 10.6% and 26.9% of their negatives at one threshold. With different base rates, that is arithmetic, not bias in the model.
6. With unequal base rates and imperfect prediction, equal error rates and equal precision cannot both hold, and calibration within groups cannot coexist with balanced scores; the confusion-matrix identity forces at least one gap.
7. Removing the group narrowed the gaps without closing them, broke calibration within groups, and a proxy restored much of what was removed.
8. Equalising false positive rates cost 0.018 of accuracy and made a flag 52% reliable in one group and 67% in the other.
9. Report calibration and error rates by group together. Decide which inequality to accept with the people affected, and write the choice down, because no model can make it disappear.
