# Initialisation Must Break Symmetry, at the Right Scale

Before a network takes its first gradient step, every weight already has a value, and the textbook explains why those values are random with an argument about symmetry. The conclusion usually drawn from it is the sentence under test: *initialisation only needs to break symmetry*. A practitioner relies on it whenever a layer is created with a default initialiser and never revisited. If it is false, a correctly coded network with a sensible learning rate can fail to train at all, for a reason settled before training begins.

This chapter opens Part V, on training a network. Part IV ended, in Chapter 20, with networks that differed only in their random seed and still disagreed on a fifth of their answers; here the random starting point itself is the subject. The chapter first confirms the symmetry argument with a network whose weights all start equal. It then draws random weights at several scales, follows the size of the activations through thirty untrained layers, derives the per-layer factor that governs that size, and shows what it does to training. A three-layer control shows that depth is what turns a mis-scaled initialisation into a failure.

## Why the weights start random

Consider one fully connected layer of 128 units. If every weight in the layer starts at the same value, every unit computes the same weighted sum of the same inputs and produces the same output. In the backward pass each unit then receives the same gradient, because its outgoing weights are equal too, and so every unit makes the same update. The weights stay equal, and the layer behaves as a single unit copied 128 times however long it trains. This is the *symmetry problem*: gradient descent preserves any symmetry between units present at the start. Random weights break the tie, because each unit begins as a slightly different function and receives a slightly different gradient.

The first half of the argument is easy to confirm. A network with 30 hidden layers of 128 units, every weight set to 0.01, is trained on the UCI handwritten digits with the best of four learning rates. It scores 0.197 on the test half. After training, the 128 units of its first layer are still exactly identical: the count of distinct units is 1. Training does not break symmetry on its own.

## Random weights at different scales

The second half of the sentence, the word *only*, is the claim to test. Every standard initialiser draws each weight independently with a standard deviation proportional to $1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}$, where the *fan-in* of a layer is the number of inputs each of its units receives (here 128 for every hidden layer after the first). The experiment draws weights from a normal distribution with standard deviation

$$
\sigma_w = \frac{g}{\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}},
$$

and varies the *gain* $g$. Real initialisers differ in detail: some draw from a uniform distribution rather than a normal one, and Glorot's averages the fan-in and fan-out, but all share this scaling. Every value of $g$ breaks symmetry, since every weight is drawn independently. The biases start at zero.

The code below builds the 30-layer network with tanh and with ReLU activations, at gains of 0.5, 1, $\sqrt{2}$ (printed as 1.41) and 2, and passes a batch of digits through it without any training. It prints the standard deviation of the activations at layers 1, 10 and 30.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

r = nets.init_depth()          # 30 hidden layers of 128 units
for row in r["rows"]:
    sd = row["stds"]
    print(f"{row['act']:4s} g = {row['gain']:.2f}: activation sd at "
          f"layers 1, 10, 30 = {sd[0]:.2g}, {sd[9]:.2g}, {sd[29]:.2g}")
```

```text
tanh g = 0.50: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.38, 0.00071, 6.7e-10
tanh g = 1.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.58, 0.22, 0.13
tanh g = 1.41: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.68, 0.56, 0.54
tanh g = 2.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.76, 0.73, 0.72
relu g = 0.50: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.29, 2.5e-05, 3.8e-14
relu g = 1.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.58, 0.026, 4e-05
relu g = 1.41: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.82, 0.83, 1.3
relu g = 2.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 1.2, 27, 4.3e+04
```

By the thirtieth layer the eight initialisations differ by many orders of magnitude: three stay roughly level, tanh at $g = 1$ fades slowly, ReLU at $g = 1$ shrinks to 4e-05, two collapse to $10^{-9}$ and below, and ReLU at $g = 2$ grows to about $4 \times 10^4$. ## Each layer multiplies the scale by a fixed factor

The ReLU lines are close to straight on the log scale, which says that each layer multiplies the scale of its input by a roughly constant factor, and thirty layers apply that factor thirty times. For a ReLU layer the factor can be derived.

Let a unit's pre-activation be $z = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j x_j$, where $n$ is the fan-in, the weights $w_j$ are independent with mean zero and variance $g^2/n$, and they are independent of the inputs $x_j$. Then

$$
\mathrm{Var}(z) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathrm{Var}(w_j)\, \mathbb{E}[x_j^2] = g^2 \cdot \overline{\mathbb{E}[x^2]},
$$

where the bar denotes the average over the $n$ inputs. The weight variance multiplies the *second moment* of the input, its mean square, by $g^2$; dividing by the fan-in in the weight variance is exactly what cancels the sum over $n$ inputs. Because $z$ is symmetric about zero, the ReLU sets half of its values to zero and leaves the other half unchanged, so

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{ReLU}(z)^2\big] = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(z).
$$

Each ReLU layer therefore multiplies the second moment of the signal by $g^2/2$. Its standard deviation, which is what the code reports, is a fixed fraction of the square root of that second moment as long as the shape of the distribution does not change from layer to layer, so it too is multiplied by about $g/\sqrt{2}$.

Measured over the 29 steps from layer 1 to layer 30, the factor is **0.719** at $g = 1$ (theory 0.707), **1.017** at $g = \sqrt{2}$ (theory 1) and **1.438** at $g = 2$ (theory 1.414). Small per-layer factors compound into enormous overall ones. At $g = 1$ the thirtieth layer's activations are 7e-05 times the first layer's; at $g = 2$, $4 \times 10^4$ times. Only $g = \sqrt{2}$, which makes the factor exactly 1, keeps the scale level. That is the value He and colleagues derived for ReLU networks in 2015, by the same argument; the resulting rule, weights with variance $2/\text{fan-in}$, is known as *He initialisation*.

### Tanh saturates, and the backward pass behaves differently

Tanh is close to the identity near zero and flattens towards ±1 for large inputs, a behaviour called *saturation*. At small scales it therefore behaves almost linearly, and at $g = 0.5$ its activations shrink geometrically, to 7e-10 by layer 30. The gradient reaching the first layer has norm 1e-09: a *vanishing gradient*, the backward-pass counterpart of the vanishing signal.

At $g = 2$ the activations cannot grow past ±1, so the forward pass stays bounded, at a standard deviation of 0.72 by layer 30. The gradient flowing back grows instead: the first layer's gradient norm is 25.4 and the last layer's 0.62. A tanh network can look well behaved in its activations while its gradients are *exploding*, growing as they pass backwards. At $g = 1$, the value to which Glorot and Bengio's 2010 analysis points for tanh (the basis of *Glorot* or *Xavier initialisation*), the activations fade slowly, to 0.13 by layer 30.

## What the scale does to training

The next block trains each of the eight networks. Each gets the best of four learning rates, 0.001, 0.003, 0.01 and 0.03, so that no initialisation loses merely for want of the right step size. The training uses stochastic gradient descent with momentum for 30 epochs. The block also prints the constant initialisation's result and its count of distinct first-layer units.

```python
for row in r["rows"]:
    print(f"{row['act']:4s} g = {row['gain']:.2f}: best test accuracy "
          f"{row['best']:.3f} at learning rate {row['best_lr']}")
print(f"all weights 0.01: {r['const_best']:.3f}; distinct units in "
      f"layer 1 after training: {r['const_distinct_units']}")
```

```text
tanh g = 0.50: best test accuracy 0.101 at learning rate 0.001
tanh g = 1.00: best test accuracy 0.949 at learning rate 0.003
tanh g = 1.41: best test accuracy 0.919 at learning rate 0.01
tanh g = 2.00: best test accuracy 0.726 at learning rate 0.001
relu g = 0.50: best test accuracy 0.101 at learning rate 0.001
relu g = 1.00: best test accuracy 0.132 at learning rate 0.001
relu g = 1.41: best test accuracy 0.919 at learning rate 0.003
relu g = 2.00: best test accuracy 0.099 at learning rate 0.001
all weights 0.01: 0.197; distinct units in layer 1 after training: 1
```

Four of the eight random initialisations do not train at all in thirty epochs; with ten balanced classes, chance accuracy is about 0.10. ReLU at $g = 1$ scores **0.132**. Its signal reaches the last hidden layer at less than a ten-thousandth of its size, and the gradients reaching the early layers are of order 1e-05, so the early layers barely move. ReLU at $g = 2$ scores 0.099; its activations and gradients are tens of thousands of times too large, and none of the learning rates tried can take a useful step through them. Tanh at $g = 0.5$ and ReLU at $g = 0.5$ also score chance.

The two failing ReLU networks at $g = 1$ and $g = 2$ sit either side of the one that succeeds by a factor of only $\sqrt{2}$ in the weights' standard deviation; $g = \sqrt{2}$ scores **0.919**.

Tanh at $g = 1$ and $g = \sqrt{2}$ both train, to 0.949 and 0.919. Tanh at $g = 2$ reaches 0.726, at the smallest learning rate tried, which is where an exploding gradient does least harm. With one seed and a coarse grid of four learning rates, the ordering among the networks that do train is not the point. The point is the gap between the networks that train and those that cannot.

## Depth is what makes the scale matter

The control repeats the whole experiment with three hidden layers instead of thirty.

```python
r3 = nets.init_depth(depth=3)  # the same, with 3 hidden layers
print("  ".join(f"{row['act']} {row['gain']:.2f}: {row['best']:.3f}"
                for row in r3["rows"][:4]))
print("  ".join(f"{row['act']} {row['gain']:.2f}: {row['best']:.3f}"
                for row in r3["rows"][4:]))
print(f"constant: {r3['const_best']:.3f}")
```

```text
tanh 0.50: 0.954  tanh 1.00: 0.960  tanh 1.41: 0.959  tanh 2.00: 0.947
relu 0.50: 0.969  relu 1.00: 0.972  relu 1.41: 0.962  relu 2.00: 0.950
constant: 0.385
```

Every random initialisation trains above 0.9. The per-layer factors are unchanged; only the number of times they are applied differs. Two multiplications by 0.707 halve the signal, which the first few updates can absorb by adjusting the weights. Twenty-nine multiplications shrink it to less than a ten-thousandth, which they cannot. The constant initialisation still fails at three layers, scoring 0.385: the symmetry problem does not depend on depth. The figure below sets the two depths side by side.

![The same gains that fail at thirty layers all train at three. The constant initialisation fails at both.](ml121-f1-bars.png){alt="Paired bars: with 3 layers every random initialisation reaches above 0.9; with 30 layers four of the eight fall to near chance, and the constant initialisation is low at both depths."}

So the sentence holds for these shallow networks and fails for deep ones. Breaking symmetry is necessary at any depth. In a deep network it is not sufficient: the scale must be chosen so that each layer passes on a signal of about the size it received, in both directions. That is the whole content of the initialisers that deep-learning libraries now apply by default, and the reason they differ by activation. Glorot's rule targets tanh and sigmoid, He's targets ReLU, and using either with the other's activation is risky, as the ReLU network at $g = 1$ shows. The table below collects the activation scale, gradient size and accuracy for every configuration at both depths.

![Every row but the last breaks symmetry. A scale per layer far from 1 stops training at thirty layers; one near 1 is not enough on its own: tanh at g = 2 has 0.998 and scores 0.726.](ml121-f2-table.png){alt="A nine-row table of activation scale, gradient size and accuracy for each activation and gain at 30 and 3 layers."}

The table also carries a qualification: tanh at $g = 2$ has a per-layer scale of 0.998, almost level, yet scores only 0.726. A level forward signal is not enough on its own; this is the network whose gradients grow on the way back. Once a network starts in a trainable state, the learning rate decides how fast it moves, and Chapter 22 examines that setting together with the batch size.

## In practice

- **Never initialise a layer's weights to a constant.** The units stay identical through training, and the layer is one unit wide in effect.
- **Match the initialiser to the activation.** Use He initialisation (variance $2/\text{fan-in}$) for ReLU layers and Glorot initialisation for tanh and sigmoid layers. Check which initialiser your layers actually use rather than assuming.
- **Measure the activations before training.** Pass one batch through the untrained network, record the standard deviation of each layer's output and plot it against depth on a log scale. A line that rises or falls steadily means the initial scale is wrong.
- **Compute the per-layer factor.** It should be close to 1; for ReLU it should be close to $g/\sqrt{2}$, and if it is far from that, suspect that the activation or the gain is not what you think.
- **Check the gradients as well.** A level forward signal can hide exploding gradients. Record the gradient norm at the first and last layers after one backward pass.
- **Treat depth as the multiplier of any mis-scaling.** A choice that is harmless at three layers can stop a thirty-layer plain network from training.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Plain stacks of layers.** Residual connections, which add each block's input to its output, and normalisation layers, which rescale activations at every layer (Chapter 24), both make a network far less sensitive to its initial scale. Most modern deep networks have both, which is why this failure is rarer in practice than the arithmetic suggests.
- **One seed and four learning rates.** The verdicts are large enough to survive both limits: the four failing networks score at most 0.132 at any rate tried, against chance at 0.10. The differences among the networks that do train are not established here.
- **Thirty epochs.** A network whose gradients are of order 1e-5 might eventually begin to learn with a much larger learning rate or many more epochs. Neither was tried, and a much larger rate would risk overflow in the layers whose activations are not small.
- **The theory describes the start.** The $g/\sqrt{2}$ factor describes the network at initialisation. Training changes the weights, and the scales with them, which is why the ReLU network at $g = \sqrt{2}$ can train at all: the initialisation only has to give it a place to start.

## Key points

1. Symmetry must be broken. With every weight equal, the 128 units of a layer were still identical after training, and the network scored 0.197.
2. Breaking symmetry is not enough in a deep network. Each layer multiplies the signal's scale by a factor set by the initial scale, about $g/\sqrt{2}$ for ReLU, measured at 0.719 for $g = 1$ and 1.438 for $g = 2$.
3. Over thirty layers those factors decided whether training happened at all: ReLU scored 0.132 at $g = 1$, 0.919 at $g = \sqrt{2}$ and 0.099 at $g = 2$.
4. Tanh saturates, so its forward signal can stay level while its gradients explode; the scale must be right in both directions.
5. With three layers every random scale trained above 0.9. Depth turns a modest mis-scaling into a failure. Use the initialiser that matches the activation, and check the activations' scale at the last layer before training.
