# Learning Rate and Batch Size Are One Knob, Until the Rate Hits a Wall

Every training script exposes a learning rate and a batch size, and they are usually treated as separate decisions: the batch size is set by the memory available, and the learning rate is tuned afterwards for that batch. The sentence under test is that division of labour: *learning rate and batch size are separate knobs*. A practitioner relies on it whenever a script moves to a machine with more memory, the batch size goes up, and the tuned learning rate is carried over unchanged. If the two settings in fact act together, that move silently changes the training run, and a learning rate tuned at one batch size says little about another.

Chapter 21 showed that a network's initial scale decides whether it trains at all. This chapter takes the next two settings. It trains one small network over a grid of learning rates and batch sizes with the number of training examples held fixed, and shows first that the best learning rate grows in proportion to the batch size, at least from batches of 8 to 32. It then shows that this is a consequence of something more exact, that the run is governed by the ratio of the two settings, and explains why. Finally it locates the point where the proportionality ends, a learning rate beyond which training fails whatever the batch size, and draws out what that wall means for large batches.

## The experiment: a grid at a fixed number of examples

*Stochastic gradient descent* (SGD) with *minibatches* of size $B$ updates the weights $w$ using the average gradient over $B$ randomly chosen training examples:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i \in \text{batch}} \nabla \ell_i(w),
$$

where $\eta$ is the *learning rate* and $\ell_i$ is the loss on example $i$. One *epoch* is one pass through the training set. The variant used here is plain SGD: no momentum, no weight decay, nothing adaptive, so that the update is exactly the formula above.

The network has two hidden layers of 128 units with ReLU activations and is trained on half the UCI digits, 898 images. Batch sizes are 8, 32, 128 and 512, and learning rates double from 0.003 to 6.144. Every run sees the same number of examples, 20 epochs' worth, so a batch of 8 takes 2,245 steps and a batch of 512 takes 35. Holding the examples fixed rather than the steps is the comparison that matters in practice, because the number of examples processed is what a training budget pays for. The score is the training loss (cross-entropy) at the end, averaged over two seeds, because the question is about optimisation rather than generalisation.

The first block prints, for each batch size, the learning rate with the lowest final training loss, that loss, and the test accuracy of the run.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

r = nets.lr_batch()     # plain SGD, 20 epochs of examples, 2 seeds
for B, lr, loss, acc in zip(r["batches"], r["best_lr"], r["best_loss"],
                            r["best_acc"]):
    print(f"batch {B:3d}: best rate {lr:.3f}  training loss {loss:.4f}  "
          f"test {acc:.3f}")
```

```text
batch   8: best rate 0.192  training loss 0.0005  test 0.972
batch  32: best rate 0.768  training loss 0.0005  test 0.974
batch 128: best rate 1.536  training loss 0.0016  test 0.968
batch 512: best rate 1.536  training loss 0.0264  test 0.964
```

The best learning rate for batches of 32 is **four times** the best for batches of 8, and the two reach the same training loss and almost the same test accuracy. This is the *linear scaling rule*: when the batch size is multiplied by $k$, multiply the learning rate by $k$. Continuing the pattern, batches of 128 and 512 would want rates of 3.072 and 12.288. Instead, both stop at 1.536, and their losses are higher. The figure below plots the final training loss against the learning rate for each batch size.

![Each fourfold increase in batch size moves the curve two grid points to the right, until the curve runs into the same wall.](ml122-f0-lr.png){alt="Four falling curves on log axes, each shifted to the right of the last, all cut off where training fails, the two right-hand ones at the same learning rate near 3."}

Because the grid doubles the rate, a fourfold change in rate is two grid points, so each curve is the previous one shifted two points to the right. The point where a curve is cut off shifts with it for the two smallest batches, but not for the two largest, whose curves are cut off at the same rate.

## The ratio of rate to batch size sets the run

"Best rate" is a coarse summary of a curve. The rule holds far more exactly when the ratio $\eta/B$ is fixed and the loss is read across batch sizes. The next block does that for three ratios, picking out, for each batch size, the grid point whose rate gives that ratio.

```python
import math

for ratio in (0.00075, 0.003, 0.012):     # learning rate / batch size
    row = []
    for i, B in enumerate(r["batches"]):
        j = round(math.log2(ratio * B / 0.003))   # grid is 0.003 * 2**j
        row.append(f"B={B}: {r['loss'][i][j]:.4f}")
    print(f"rate/batch {ratio:.5f}   " + "  ".join(row))
```

```text
rate/batch 0.00075   B=8: 0.1832  B=32: 0.1835  B=128: 0.1849  B=512: 0.1892
rate/batch 0.00300   B=8: 0.0183  B=32: 0.0183  B=128: 0.0184  B=512: 0.0264
rate/batch 0.01200   B=8: 0.0016  B=32: 0.0016  B=128: 0.0016  B=512: 6.1513
```

At a ratio of 0.00075 the four batch sizes reach losses of 0.183 to 0.189, although the batch of 8 takes 2,245 steps and the batch of 512 only 35. At 0.003 the first three agree to within 1%. Taking every pair of runs on the grid in which both the batch and the rate are four times larger, and in which both rates are at least four times below the rate at which their batch size fails (defined in the next section), the losses differ by at most **2.3%**. Closer to failure the pairs part, by up to 44%. Plotted against the ratio, as in the figure below, the four curves are one curve until each in turn leaves it.

![Away from the wall, runs with the same ratio reach the same loss to within 2.3%. The batch of 512 leaves the shared curve first.](ml122-f1-ratio.png){alt="The four curves collapse onto one falling line; the batches of 8 and 32 leave it together, the batch of 128 earlier and the batch of 512 first."}

### Why the ratio, and not each setting

Two arguments lead to the same ratio. The first is arithmetic. With a fixed number of examples, a batch four times larger means four times fewer steps. Compare four consecutive steps at rate $\eta$ on batches $b_1, \dots, b_4$ of size $B$ with one step at rate $4\eta$ on their union, a batch of $4B$. If the gradient changes little over those four steps, the four small steps move the weights by approximately

$$
\eta \sum_{k=1}^{4} \bar g_{b_k},
$$

where $\bar g_{b_k}$ is the average gradient on batch $b_k$, and the single large step moves them by

$$
4\eta \cdot \frac{1}{4} \sum_{k=1}^{4} \bar g_{b_k},
$$

which is the same quantity. The approximation is good when the learning rate is small, because then each step changes the weights, and so the gradient, very little. It degrades as the rate grows, which is consistent with the pairs parting near failure.

The second argument concerns noise. A minibatch gradient is an average of $B$ per-example gradients, so its variance about the full-data gradient is proportional to $1/B$. Smith and Le showed that the size of the random kicks SGD gives the weights, which they call the noise scale, grows in proportion to the learning rate divided by the batch size. In the continuous-time approximation behind that result, two runs with the same $\eta/B$ and the same number of examples follow the same expected path with the same amount of noise around it. Either way, the run is set by $\eta/B$, and the two "separate knobs" are one.

## The wall: a learning rate that does not move with the batch

The last line of the previous output is where the rule ends. At a ratio of 0.012 the batch of 512 needs a learning rate of 6.144, and the run ends with a mean training loss of 6.15. For this chapter a run has *failed* when its mean training loss ends above 1. For reference, a network that has learned nothing, and assigns probability one tenth to each of the ten classes, has cross-entropy $\ln 10 = 2.30$.

Every batch size fails somewhere on the grid: the batch of 8 at a rate of 0.384 (mean loss 1.92), the batch of 32 at 1.536 (2.32), and the batches of 128 and 512 both at **3.072**. At that rate the batch of 128 diverges outright, ending at a loss of 6.01. The batch of 512 is degraded rather than diverged, ending at 1.27, worse than at any smaller rate but still below a network that has learned nothing.

For the two small batches the failure point follows the ratio, as the best rate did: both fail at 0.048 per example. For the two large batches it does not; they fail at the same learning rate. That is what a limit unrelated to the batch size would look like, though with only two batch sizes and a grid of doublings the pattern is suggestive rather than shown.

There is a standard candidate for such a limit. Near a minimum, a loss surface is approximately quadratic, and gradient descent on a quadratic whose sharpest curvature (largest second derivative) is $\lambda$ is stable only for

$$
\eta < \frac{2}{\lambda}.
$$

Above that rate each step overshoots along the sharpest direction by more than it corrects, and the error grows. This limit is a property of the loss surface, not of the batch, so it binds a full-batch step and a large-batch step alike. The small batches hit a limit that scales with the batch, as a limit set by gradient noise would; the large batches hit one that does not, as a limit set by curvature would. Curvature itself was not measured here, so the attribution is an interpretation consistent with the pattern rather than a demonstration.

### What happens beyond the wall

Once a batch size needs a learning rate above the wall, the linear scaling rule has nothing left to offer: the rate cannot be raised to keep the ratio. Here the batch of 128, at its best rate, reached a training loss 3.3 times the batch of 32's, and the batch of 512 a loss 55 times as high. Test accuracy fell from 0.974 to 0.968 and 0.964. With 35 steps in total, the likeliest reading is that the batch of 512 did not travel far enough, and the only way to travel further is more steps.

This is the regime McCandlish and colleagues describe with the *critical batch size*: beyond it, a larger batch buys little reduction in the number of steps needed to reach a given loss, so it mostly spends more computation for the same progress. The table below summarises the four batch sizes.

## Reading a training script in terms of the ratio

The practical rule is not "tune both". It is to treat $\eta/B$ as the setting, and to change the batch size only together with the learning rate, in proportion, while the rate stays well below the point where training fails. Below the wall the change is free: the batches of 32 and 8 reached the same loss, 0.0005 and 0.0005, and the batch of 32 did it with a quarter of the steps. Above the wall, a larger batch is a decision to train less, and has to be paid for with more epochs.

The ratio also says how to read a tuning result. A learning rate tuned at one batch size is a ratio tuned at that batch size. Copying the rate to a run with four times the batch, as happens when a script moves to a larger machine, divides the effective setting by four. On this grid, the batch of 32's best rate, 0.768, gives the batch of 128 a training loss of 0.0050 instead of 0.0005.

Goyal and colleagues trained an ImageNet model in an hour on this principle, with batches of 8,192 and the learning rate scaled up to match. They added a *warm-up*: a few epochs in which the learning rate rises gradually to its target. Its purpose is to keep the large rate from diverging in the first steps, when the network changes fastest. In the terms of this chapter, warm-up is a way of approaching the same wall at the start of training without crossing it. Chapter 23 turns from plain SGD to the optimiser most networks are actually trained with, Adam.

## In practice

- **Treat the ratio of learning rate to batch size as the setting.** When you change the batch size, scale the learning rate by the same factor, and record the ratio alongside the rate.
- **Do not copy a tuned learning rate to a different batch size.** A rate tuned at one batch size is a ratio; moving to four times the batch at the same rate divides the effective setting by four.
- **Locate the wall before scaling up.** Run a short sweep at doubling rates and note where training fails. If the batch size you want needs a rate near or above it, the linear rule will not hold.
- **Beyond the wall, pay with epochs.** A larger batch at a capped rate takes fewer, shorter-travelling steps; budget more epochs or accept a higher loss.
- **Consider warm-up for large rates.** Goyal and colleagues raised the rate gradually over the first epochs to keep large-batch runs from diverging at the start; warm-up was not tested in this chapter.
- **Remember the rule was measured with plain SGD.** With momentum the ratio applies to the effective rate; with Adam the relation changes, as the next section explains.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Plain SGD only.** With momentum, the effective step is the learning rate divided by one minus the momentum, and the same ratio argument applies to that effective rate. With Adam, whose step size does not scale with the gradient's magnitude, the relation is closer to a square root and depends on the noise. Chapter 23 uses Adam.
- **Training loss, not test accuracy.** The question was optimisation. Test accuracy followed the training loss closely here; on larger problems it can prefer the noisier, smaller-batch runs at equal training loss, a separate question with a contested answer.
- **One network and one budget.** The position of the wall depends on the network's curvature, and the critical batch size depends on the task's gradient noise, so neither number transfers to other problems. What transfers is the shape: a ratio that sets the run, and a wall that does not move with the batch.
- **A coarse grid.** Rates double, so "best" and "fails" are each known only to within a factor of two.

## Key points

1. With plain SGD and a fixed number of examples, learning rate and batch size act through their ratio. Away from divergence, runs with the same ratio reached the same training loss to within 2.3%, from batches of 8 to 512.
2. The best rate grew fourfold from a batch of 8 to a batch of 32 (0.192 to 0.768), with the same loss and test accuracy.
3. For large batches, training failed (mean training loss above 1) at a learning rate that did not move with the batch: 3.072 for both 128 and 512, diverging at 128 and degraded to 1.27 at 512.
4. Batches that needed a rate beyond that wall reached a training loss 3 and 55 times higher than the batch of 32's.
5. Change the batch size and the learning rate together; beyond the wall, a larger batch means less training, not faster training.
