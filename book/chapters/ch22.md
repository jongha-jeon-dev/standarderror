# The Book, Measured

Every section of this book but the last took one sentence that a textbook, a course or a senior colleague states as fact, and measured where it stopped being true. The measurements were made with code, mostly on simulations built to show an effect or on small data sets of hundreds to a few thousand rows, and the numbers the prose quotes were pinned by checks in that code so that they could not drift from what the text says. This last chapter turns the book's methods on the book. A practitioner meets the same questions whenever a result from a simulation, a single split or a single training run is written into a report as a conclusion: does it travel to other data, does it survive another random draw, and what, taken together, has it established?

The chapter's three sections ask those questions in turn.

- Section 22.1 tests *a finding measured on a simulation tells you what will happen on real data*. It repeats four verdicts that earlier sections measured only on simulations, about resampling for a rare class, the optimism of a tuned score, the threshold for unequal costs and choosing a model by its AUC, on the digits and breast-cancer data bundled with scikit-learn.
- Section 22.2 tests the book's own working assumption, *a verdict measured once, and pinned so the build fails if it drifts, will hold on the next draw*. It reruns the twelve verdicts that rested on a single random draw, each on five new seeds, and sorts them by how often they held.
- Section 22.3 tests no sentence of its own. It counts the assertions that pinned the book's numbers, sorts the sixty-nine sentences into those that failed, those that held only under conditions and the one that held as written, and groups the broken ones into four families, each a habit worth unlearning.

The sections build outwards. Section 22.1 separates two questions that are easy to run together, whether an effect's direction carries over to new data and whether its size does, and finds that directions travelled better than sizes. Section 22.2 makes the same separation for single draws: an observation can be right while the word or round threshold attached to it is not, and the margin between the measured value and that word decides which. Section 22.3 relies on both results when it reads the book's verdicts as a whole, since each verdict is a claim about the setting in which it was measured, stated at the precision that setting supported. The chapter, and the book, end with the habit all three sections recommend: before acting on a sentence about machine learning, ask where it was measured, and measure it in your own setting.

## Four Simulated Verdicts, Retried on Real Data

<!-- source: ml-65-replication; hero: ml165-hero.png -->


This book has leaned on simulations, deliberately. A simulation knows the truth, so a method's error can be measured exactly, and it can isolate one mechanism at a time: a planted shortcut, a known cost, a rare class with a known rate. Where scikit-learn's bundled data allowed, sections replicated their findings on real data, but some verdicts were measured only on simulations. A practitioner reading them is entitled to ask whether *a finding measured on a simulation tells you what will happen on real data.* If it does not, the numbers in those sections are properties of the simulated worlds and say little about any data set the reader owns.

This section takes four such verdicts and repeats each on real data with the original section's models and settings. For each it asks two separate questions: whether the direction of the effect replicates, and whether its size does. It closes with what the pattern of answers says about how to use a simulation's results.

### What a replication of a method can test


Morris, White and Crowther's guide to simulation studies makes the case for simulations and states their limit: the results apply to the data-generating processes simulated. A simulation shows what a method does to data of a particular kind; it cannot say how common that kind is.

Replication has a cautionary history. The Open Science Collaboration's attempt to replicate a hundred psychology studies found effects that were, on average, about half the size of the originals. The question here is narrower, because the four verdicts are about methods, not about the world: does the method behave on real data the way it behaved on simulated data?

The four were chosen because each was measured only on a simulation and each can be re-run on the bundled data with the original section's own code. They are resampling for a rare class (Section 11.1), the optimism of a tuned score (Section 4.1), the threshold for unequal costs (Section 12.1) and the model with the best AUC (Section 12.2). Each is repeated over 20 random splits.

### Rare classes: replicated, smaller


#### The simulated verdict

Section 11.1 studied three common remedies for a rare positive class. *Oversampling* duplicates positive rows until the classes are balanced; *SMOTE* (synthetic minority oversampling) creates new positive rows by interpolating between existing ones and their nearest positive neighbours; *class weights* leave the rows alone and weight the positives more heavily in the loss. At a 2% positive rate in a simulation, all three left a logistic model's AUC unchanged and multiplied its predicted probabilities about elevenfold.

The AUC is unchanged because it depends only on how the model ranks rows, and for a logistic model rebalancing mainly shifts the intercept, which moves every score the same way and leaves the ranking nearly intact. The probabilities inflate because the model learns the prevalence it is trained on: balancing the classes tells it that positives are common.

#### The real version

The real version asks whether a handwritten digit is an 8, about 10% of the digits, with logistic regression on standardised pixels: 1,000 images to train and 797 to test. The block below runs five splits and prints each method's AUC, mean predicted probability, the actual rate and the *log loss*, the average of $-\log$ of the probability given to the true class, which penalises confident wrong probabilities. The figures quoted afterwards are over all 20 splits.

```python
from standarderror.ml import deploy

r = deploy.replicate_imbalance(reps=5)
for m, v in r.items():
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {v['auc']:.4f}  mean predicted {v['mean_p']:.3f}"
          f"  (actual rate {v['rate']:.3f})  log loss {v['log_loss']:.3f}")
```

```text
plain         AUC 0.9701  mean predicted 0.098  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.120
oversample    AUC 0.9694  mean predicted 0.171  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.178
SMOTE         AUC 0.9704  mean predicted 0.157  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.157
class weights AUC 0.9702  mean predicted 0.182  (actual rate 0.100)  log loss 0.184
```

Over the 20 splits, the AUC moved by at most 0.0009, paired across splits. The plain model's mean prediction matched the actual rate (0.098 against 0.101); the resampled ones predicted 1.6 to 1.9 times too much, and their log loss was 30% to 53% worse.

Both the mechanism and its direction carried over. The size did not, for a reason the simulation predicts. Balancing a 10% class moves the training prevalence by a factor of 5; balancing a 2% class moves it by a factor of 25. The inflation grows with that shift, though not in proportion to it, so a less rare class gives a smaller inflation.

### Tuning: replicated, a fifth of the size


Section 4.1 measured the *optimism* of hyperparameter search: the amount by which the best cross-validated score among many settings overstates how the chosen model performs on fresh data. Each setting's score is an estimate with noise, and picking the maximum picks, in part, the setting whose noise happened to be most favourable. This is the *winner's curse*. Section 4.1 searched 64 SVM settings by cross-validation on 200 simulated rows and found the best score 0.023 optimistic, with the tuned model no better on fresh data than the default.

On 200 breast-cancer tumours with the same grid, the best cross-validated score was 0.974, the tuned model scored 0.969 on the other 369 tumours, and the default also scored 0.969. The optimism was 0.005 (standard error 0.002), and tuning bought +0.000.

The direction replicated exactly: the search's best score overstated what it found, and what it found was the default. The size was a fifth, because the breast-cancer data are easy. The winner's curse needs spread among the settings' scores to exploit. When every reasonable setting scores near 97%, there is little spread. The simulation's task was chosen to be hard, with an error floor of about a quarter, which is where the winner's curse bites. Real problems lie at both ends, so Section 4.1's warning holds and its number does not travel. The table below summarises the four replications.

### Thresholds and AUC: half, and weaker


#### The cost-derived threshold

Suppose a miss costs $r$ times as much as a false alarm. A row with calibrated probability $p$ of being positive should be flagged when the expected cost of clearing it, $p \cdot r$, exceeds the expected cost of flagging it, $1 - p$, that is, when

$$
p > \frac{1}{1 + r}.
$$

Section 12.1 set a miss to cost ten false alarms, so the threshold is $1/11$. In simulation it found that flagging at 0.5 cost 2.9 to 4 times as much as simply flagging everyone, while the threshold $1/(1 + r)$ came within 0.003 of the best possible for a recalibrated model.

On breast cancer, with 300 tumours to train, 269 to test, malignancy as the positive class and a missed malignancy costing ten false alarms, the cost-derived threshold lowered every one of the six models' costs. Logistic regression's fell from 0.330 to 0.147 per tumour. But at 0.5, five of six models were already cheaper than flagging everyone (0.623); only 15-nearest-neighbours was not. And the recalibrated model's cost at $1/(1 + r)$ was 0.158 against 0.112 at the best threshold in hindsight, a gap of 0.046, though a threshold chosen in hindsight on 269 tumours flatters itself, for the same winner's-curse reason as above.

The advice replicated; the drama did not. A task where the models are 97% accurate leaves little room for any threshold to be catastrophic. The figure below shows each model's cost at both thresholds against the cost of flagging everyone.

![Cost per tumour for six models at the default and cost-derived thresholds, against the cost of flagging everyone. Logistic regression costs 0.330 at 0.5 and **0.147** at 1/11.](ml165-f1-costs.png){alt="Grouped bars for six models. The cost-derived threshold lowers every model's cost; most models at 0.5 are below the flag-everyone line."}

#### The best AUC and the cheapest model

Section 12.2 found, in simulation, that the model with the best AUC was never the cheapest when misses cost ten times more. On breast cancer, the AUC winner was boosted trees in 7 of 20 splits and the cheapest at $1/(1 + r)$ only 2 times; logistic regression, the AUC winner in 4, was cheapest in 11. The same model won both in 6 of 20 splits. Choosing by AUC would usually have chosen a model that was not the cheapest, as the simulation said, but not always.

The mechanism is the one Section 12.2 described. The AUC averages a model's ranking over every possible threshold, while a 10:1 cost ratio cares only about the region where malignant tumours might be cleared, and the model that ranks best overall need not rank best there. On a task this easy the top models' AUCs differ in the third decimal, so which one wins varies from split to split. Across 20 splits the cheapest was still most often logistic regression, which won on AUC only 4 times.

#### The pattern

All four verdicts kept their direction, or part of it, and every effect was smaller or weaker on these data. Both bundled data sets are easy, and on easy data the failures the earlier sections measured have less room to do damage. Section 22.2 turns the same scrutiny on the book's own measurements, with a different question: not whether a verdict travels from a simulation to real data, but whether a verdict measured on one random draw survives another.

## The Book, Rerun With Five More Seeds

<!-- source: ml-66-rerun; hero: ml166-hero.png -->


Section 22.1 asked whether simulated verdicts carry over to real data. A more basic question can be asked of any measured result, simulated or real: would it come out the same on another random draw? Every number in this book was produced by code, and every section's claims were checked by that code each time the book was built. The sentence under test is the book's own working assumption: *a verdict measured once, and pinned so the build fails if it drifts, will hold on the next draw*. A practitioner makes the same assumption whenever a result from one split, one simulated data set or one training run goes into a report as a conclusion. If it is false, some conclusions describe one draw rather than the method or the data.

This section first explains what a pinned assertion does and does not protect against. It then reruns the twelve verdicts in the book that rested on a single draw, each on five new seeds, and sorts them by how often they held. It closes with what the failures share: how close the measured number sat to the word chosen for it.

### A pinned assertion guards against drift, not against luck


Each section's build ends with a list of *assertions*: statements in the code that check a computed value against what the prose says. Some pin a number the text quotes; others pin a qualitative claim, such as "the knee falls below the good radii". If a library update or an edit to the code changes a result, the assertion fails, the build fails, and the section cannot be published with a stale number.

That protection is real but narrow. An assertion checks that the code still produces what it produced when the section was written, not that what it produced was typical. Many verdicts were averaged over repeated draws inside their section, so their spread was already measured. Some, however, came from a single split, a single simulated data set or a single random initialisation. For those, the assertion pinned whatever that one draw showed, and a verdict that was partly luck was pinned as firmly as one that was not.

A *random seed* is the integer that initialises a pseudo-random number generator. Fixing it makes a computation reproducible; changing it gives a new, equally legitimate draw. Rerunning on several seeds is the cheapest way to see how much of a result belongs to the method and how much to the draw.

### How the twelve verdicts were rerun


Twelve verdicts qualified: each rested on one draw, and each section's function takes a seed. Every one was rerun with seeds 1 to 5, using the section's own code and the conditions its assertions state. Seed 0 reproduces every published value, so the comparison is purely between draws, with the code, the settings and the claim's threshold held fixed.

The code below shows the mechanism for Section 17.3's verdict about DBSCAN's radius. `course.CLAIMS` holds, for each claim, the number the series gave its section (58 for Section 17.3), the verdict as written, what is measured, and a `check` function that takes a seed and reports the value and whether the verdict held.

```python
from standarderror.ml import course

ep, verdict, what, check = next(c for c in course.CLAIMS if c[0] == 58)
print(verdict)
for seed in range(3):
    r = check(seed)
    print(f"seed {seed}: {what} {r['value']:.3f}, held: {r['held']}")
```

```text
the k-distance knee falls below the half-moons' good radii
seed 0: knee's score 0.573, held: True
seed 1: knee's score 0.960, held: False
seed 2: knee's score 0.798, held: False
```

Seed 0 gives the published 0.573 and the verdict holds; seeds 1 and 2 give 0.960 and 0.798, and it fails on both.

### Seven verdicts held on every seed, and two on four of five


Seven verdicts held on all five new seeds: those of Sections 3.1, 11.1, 18.1, 3.2, 13.1, 16.2 and 17.2. Some held with essentially the same number every time. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC), which Section 17.2 showed counts Gaussian components rather than clusters, chose 9 components for three uniform squares on every draw. t-SNE, which Section 16.2 showed keeps neighbourhoods but not sizes, drew a cluster with ten times another's spread at 0.997 to 1.015 times the narrow one's size.

Others moved but stayed far from their threshold. In Section 13.1, dropping the test result inflated the treatment's apparent effect 1.62 to 1.67-fold, against a claim of more than 1.6. In Section 3.2, a target encoding fitted on the training rows ranked its own labels at an AUC of 0.773 to 0.781. (The *AUC* is the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative; one half is chance.)

Two verdicts held on four of five. Section 7.1 said that none of 200 *bootstrap trees*, trees refitted on resamples of the rows drawn with replacement, reproduced the full-data tree's top three levels; in one new split, 3 did. Section 16.1 said only the tenth principal component predicted, at accuracy above 0.9; in one new simulation it scored 0.889. Both points survive in substance: three trees in 200 is still almost none, and 0.889 is still the only component that predicts. What failed was the threshold written into the prose, "none" and "above 0.9", which was tighter than one draw could support. The table below lists all twelve verdicts.

### Three verdicts did not hold as written


In each of the remaining three, the underlying observation was correct; the word or number attached to it was not.

#### The k-distance knee on the half-moons (Section 17.3)

DBSCAN groups points that have enough neighbours within a radius, and its result depends heavily on that radius. A common heuristic is the *k-distance plot*: sort every point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour and take the radius at the knee, where the curve bends sharply upwards. Section 17.3 found that on two half-moons the knee fell just below the window of good radii and scored 0.57.

On five other draws of the moons it fell inside the window every time, scoring 0.80 to 0.96. The published miss was that draw's: the knee sits close to the window's lower edge, and which side it lands on depends on the noise. Section 17.3's broader point, that the heuristic picked a wrong radius on the digits, stands; the moons no longer illustrate it.

#### The share of tumours whose top feature changed (Section 14.4)

A SHAP value attributes a prediction relative to a *background*, a reference set of rows that defines what the prediction is compared with. Section 14.4 found that explaining tumours against benign ones instead of all of them changed the top feature for 58% of tumours, and wrote "most". On five other splits the share was 33% to 53%: still large, and the real claim, that the explanation depends on the background, is untouched. But "most" was that split's.

#### Grouped anomalies and a round AUC threshold (Section 18.2)

A simple anomaly score is a point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour. If anomalies form a group larger than k, each one's k nearest neighbours are other anomalies, so it looks normal. Section 18.2 asserted that grouped anomalies then scored an AUC below 0.2. On the new seeds the worst AUC ranged from 0.18 to 0.24: always far below chance, the section's real claim, but above the 0.2 its assertion wrote down in three of five.

All three sections have been revised to report the range from this rerun. The figure below counts the seeds on which each verdict held.

![Seven of 12 verdicts held on every new seed. Numbers in the figure are the series' episode numbers; Appendix B maps them to sections.](ml166-f1-held.png){alt="Twelve bars: seven at five, two at four, and bars at two, one and zero for episodes 59, 18 and 58."}

### What the failures have in common: a small margin


The verdicts that broke were not the surprising ones. Each was a correct observation whose number sat close to the word the prose attached to it: a knee just below a window, a share just above a half, an AUC just under a round threshold. Near a threshold, a small draw-to-draw variation is enough to carry a value across, and a single draw says nothing about how large that variation is.

Bouthillier, Laurent and Vincent made the same observation about deep-learning papers in 2019: results reported from one seed often fail to reproduce under another, not because the method is wrong but because the margin was smaller than the run-to-run variation. Section 1.2 measured that variation for networks that differ only in their seed; this section measured it for the book's own claims.

The verdicts that held had one of two protections: they were averages over many draws inside their section, or the effect was far larger than any plausible variation, such as nine components for three squares or a tenfold size difference drawn as none. A single draw is enough evidence for a large effect and not for a precise number, and a pinned assertion is only as good as the margin between the measured value and the threshold it checks.

The cost of finding this was small. The twelve reruns took about six minutes of computer time, less than writing any one of the sections they checked, and they changed three of them. Whenever a number is about to become a word, such as *most*, *none*, *always* or *below* a round threshold, rerun it with a few seeds and see whether the word survives the range.

Seeds test one source of fragility, the random draw. A result can also depend on the analyst's choices, made one after another while watching the scores; Section 4.2 measured how far such a sequence can move a reported result. The last section steps back from single verdicts and sorts all of them.

### How each verdict was classified


Sorting a verdict needs a rule, and this is the one used:

- a sentence **failed** if it was false in the setting its section measured;
- it **held under conditions** if it was true in some measured settings and false in others, or true for one half of a two-part sentence;
- it **held** if it was true in every setting measured.

The classification is the author's reading of each section's own conclusion, and some calls are close. The list of calls is in this section's code, for anyone who would sort differently.

### Thirty-eight failed, thirty held under conditions, one held


By that rule, 38 sentences failed as stated, 30 held under conditions, and 1 held as written. The one that held was Section 3.1's: *fit every preprocessing step inside the folds*. Even that came with a measured scale. Violations ranged from nothing, on real data with strong features, to coin flips scored at 0.88 on noise, but no measured setting made the rule wrong.

The failures were spread across the book. The series the book grew from grouped its sentences five at a time by topic, and every group had at least one failure. Three groups had four or more of their five: what a score means (the sentences of Sections 1.1, 2.1, 3.1, 5.1 and 11.1), turning predictions into decisions (Sections 12.1, 12.2 and 13.1 to 13.3, all five) and grading a language model (Sections 19.1 to 19.5). Those are the groups whose sentences are rules for reading a number or acting on it: the higher score is better, the threshold is 0.5, the benchmark measures the model. Such a rule compresses a judgement into a default, and a default is what a measurement in a new setting tends to break.

The group with the fewest failures was trees and ensembles (Sections 7.1, 14.1, 7.2, 8.1 and 7.3), with one. Its sentences, about forests, boosting and out-of-bag error, were mostly true within limits the curriculum seldom states. The figure below shows every verdict, arranged in those groups.

### Four families of broken sentences


Read together, the failures and the conditional verdicts fall into a few families. Each family is a single habit worth unlearning.

#### A score answers a narrower question than the one asked of it

Cross-validation estimates the average error of a training procedure, not the error of the particular model fitted on the data in hand (Section 2.1). A test score has an error bar wider than most gains (Section 1.1). The best score of a search reflects the search's luck as well as the model's quality (Sections 4.1 and 4.2). A benchmark score includes its prompt and, for multiple choice, the distractors it offered (Sections 19.3 and 19.4). An AUC measures ranking; it does not choose a decision (Section 12.2). In each case the number is correct, but it answers a narrower question than the one a reader puts to it.

#### A method reports on the model or its assumptions, not on the data

Importance measures what a model uses, not what matters in the world or what could be changed (Sections 14.2 and 13.1). Partial dependence and SHAP answer questions about rows that do not exist, or about a background someone chose (Sections 14.3 and 14.4). The BIC counts Gaussians, and t-SNE keeps neighbours (Sections 16.2 and 17.2). Each method is faithful to something, but the thing it is faithful to is the model or the method's own assumptions.

#### The rows are not what they seem

Rows from the same people, applicants who were refused, a category's own labels and a time series' remembered errors (Sections 2.4, 15.2, 3.2 and 2.3) all make the data appear to say more than they hold. Section 5.3 showed that more rows of the same kind did not fix the first two: they were problems of the data's design, not of its size.

#### A prediction is not a decision

The threshold, the cost of a mistake, who is helped by an intervention, and what a deployed rule does to the data it later learns from (Sections 12.1, 12.2, 13.1, 13.2, 13.3 and 21.2) are not contained in the predictions. In Section 12.3, a single project run through every check, the threshold mattered more than the choice of model.

### What the book got wrong about itself


The book held itself to the same standard, and did not fully meet it. As Section 22.2 above showed, of the twelve verdicts that had rested on a single random draw, seven held on every new seed and two held four times in five, where a threshold written into the prose was tighter than the evidence. Three did not hold as written: a heuristic radius that missed in the published draw and hit in five others (Section 17.3), a share described as "most" that was 33% to 53% on other splits (Section 14.4), and an anomaly score's AUC that crossed the round threshold its assertion wrote down (Section 18.2). Those sections now say so.

Section 22.1 above took four of the book's simulated findings to real data and found that the directions replicated while the sizes often did not. Section 4.2 showed how a sequence of defensible choices can produce a number nobody chose. Both apply to this book too: its sentences were chosen by one author, and its settings were chosen to make each effect visible.

This is the last chapter of *Machine Learning, Taught Through What Breaks*. Its argument has been the same throughout: a sentence about machine learning is a claim about some setting, and it can be measured there. What the book offers in place of its sixty-nine sentences is the habit of asking where each one was measured. ## In practice

### Using a simulation's results (Section 22.1)

- **Trust a simulation for whether and why a method misbehaves.** All four verdicts carried over in direction, at least in part.
- **Do not quote a simulation's effect size as a forecast for your data.** Re-measure how much on data like yours; every size in Section 22.1 shrank.
- **Expect effects to depend on difficulty and rarity.** Tuning optimism and threshold costs were small on an easy task; probability inflation was smaller at a 10% than at a 2% positive rate.
- **Still apply the safe remedies**: report probabilities from an unresampled or recalibrated model, choose thresholds from costs, and select models on the cost you will incur, not on AUC.
- **Write up a failed replication.** A direction that fails to hold on your data is the more important finding.

### Single draws and the words attached to them (Section 22.2)

- **Rerun single-draw results before they become conclusions.** A model comparison, feature ranking, tuned setting or cluster count from one split or one initialisation should be rerun on a few other seeds, varying both the split and the model's own randomness.
- **Report the range, not the draw**, and choose words the whole range supports.
- **Watch the margin.** Before writing "above 0.9", "none" or "most", compare the distance to that threshold with the spread across seeds. If they are comparable, the word is not supported.
- **Pin your numbers, and rerun them.** Automated checks keep reported numbers from going stale; rerunning on new seeds keeps them from being lucky. Neither replaces the other. If you pin results in tests, take the thresholds from several seeds, so that a check fails on a real change rather than an ordinary draw.

### Sentences about machine learning (Section 22.3)

- **Ask where a sentence was measured** before acting on it, and whether your setting resembles that one.
- **Measure the rules your team repeats.** Rules for reading a number or acting on it, such as which score is better or where the threshold sits, are the sentences that broke most often here, so test those first.
- **Rewrite conditional truths as conditions.** When a rule holds only in some settings, state which, rather than keeping the unconditional version.

## Limits of the evidence

**The replications (Section 22.1).**

- **Two bundled data sets.** The digits and the breast-cancer data are clean, small and easy. Most real problems are harder, which on the evidence here would move the replications towards the simulations' larger effects, not away from them.
- **Four verdicts of many.** These were chosen because they could be re-run with the original sections' own code. Verdicts about time, people and selection (Sections 2.3, 2.4 and 15.2) need data with that structure, which the bundled sets lack.
- **Twenty splits of a small data set.** The splits share most of their rows, so the twenty results are not independent, and their spread understates how a new data set of the same kind could differ.
- **The same choices on both sides.** The replications reuse the original sections' choices of models and settings; an independent replication would choose its own.

**The reruns (Section 22.2).**

- **Twelve of many.** These were the verdicts that rested on one draw and whose functions take a seed. Verdicts averaged inside their section were not rerun, on the reasoning that their spread was already measured; some may still depend on choices other than the seed.
- **Five new seeds.** Enough to catch a verdict that fails often, not one that fails one time in twenty.
- **Seeds change different things.** For some functions the seed draws a new data set, for others only a new split or a new model initialisation; claims about real data cannot be given new data this way.
- **The same author checking the same code.** A rerun cannot find a mistake in the code itself, only in how much one draw was trusted. Likewise, assertions pin what was measured: they cannot catch a mistake in the code that produced a number, only a change in it.

**The sorting (Section 22.3).**

- **The sentences were chosen to break.** Each was picked because it is stated as fact and could be tested on a laptop. The count of failures says how often such sentences stop short, not how often the curriculum is wrong.
- **One author's classification.** The line between "failed" and "held under conditions" is a judgement. A different reader would move a handful of sections, and the code lists every call so that they can.
- **Small data and simulations.** Most sections used data sets of hundreds to a few thousand rows, or simulations built to show an effect. Sections 22.1 and 5.3 measured how far such findings travel; the answer was: in direction, usually; in size, not always.

## Key points

1. Resampling for a rare class left the AUC unchanged on real digits and inflated the probabilities 1.6 to 1.9 times, against eleven in the simulation at a rarer prevalence.
2. Tuning 64 settings on 200 real tumours was 0.005 optimistic and bought nothing over the default; the simulation's optimism was 0.023. A cost-derived threshold cut every model's cost on real data, but 0.5 was not the disaster the simulation found, and the best-AUC model was the cheapest in 6 of 20 splits.
3. Directions replicated; sizes shrank on easy, less imbalanced data. Trust a simulation for whether and why a method misbehaves, and re-measure how much on data like yours before quoting a number.
4. A pinned assertion protects a number against drift in the code, not against its having been a lucky draw. 936 assertions kept the book's numbers from going stale.
5. Of twelve single-draw verdicts, seven held on five of five new seeds and two on four of five, where a threshold in the prose was tighter than the evidence. Three did not hold as written: a heuristic's radius that missed in one draw and hit in five (Section 17.3), a share that was "most" in one split and 33% to 53% in others (Section 14.4), and an AUC threshold crossed in three of five reruns (Section 18.2).
6. The broken verdicts were observations close to the words chosen for them; the ones that held were averages or very large effects. Before writing a number into a claim, rerun it with a few seeds, write down the range, and choose words the range supports.
7. Of sixty-nine sentences, 38 failed as stated, 30 held under conditions and 1 held as written: fit every preprocessing step inside the folds. Most of the curriculum is true somewhere; the work is finding where.
8. The failures clustered in sentences about how a score is read, how a prediction becomes a decision, and how a language model is graded.
9. The broken sentences fall into four families: a score answering a narrower question than the one asked, a method reporting on the model rather than the data, rows that are not what they seem, and a prediction mistaken for a decision.
10. Before acting on a sentence about machine learning, ask what setting it was measured in, and measure it in yours.
