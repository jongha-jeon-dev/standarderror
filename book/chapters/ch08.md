# Boosting, Averaging and Stacking

Chapter 7 measured single trees and the forests that average them. This chapter turns to the other common ways of combining models. *Gradient boosting* combines trees in sequence, each fitted to what the trees before it still get wrong, and is among the most widely used tools for tabular data. *Averaging* combines finished models of different kinds by taking the mean of their predicted probabilities. *Stacking* combines them by fitting a second model on their predictions. A practitioner meets all three when the last point of performance is being pushed for: tuning the number of boosting rounds, assembling an ensemble from the models already trained, or putting a meta-model on top of several. Each comes with a sentence repeated so often that it sounds like a result, and each sentence decides something in practice: when to stop training, whether to ship the ensemble, how to fit the stack.

The sentences tested are these:

- Section 8.1: *boosting overfits if you run it long enough*;
- Section 8.2: *averaging several models gives a better model than any of them*;
- Section 8.3: *stacking learns the best way to combine models*.

None of them turns out to be simply true or simply false. Section 8.1 finds that the answer for boosting depends on how the output is scored: on the breast-cancer data, a boosted model's error rate is still at its lowest after 2,000 rounds, while its probabilities were at their best within the first few dozen rounds and grow steadily worse afterwards. Section 8.2 finds that an average of probabilities is not a vote of equals but weights each member by its confidence, and that the theorem behind averaging guarantees an improvement over the mean member, not over the best one. Section 8.3 finds that a meta-model fitted on the base models' in-sample predictions hands its decisions to whichever model memorised its training rows, and that one fitted honestly, on out-of-fold predictions, mostly selects the best model rather than combining several.

Two ideas run through the sections and tie them together. The first is the difference between a model's decisions and its probabilities. Section 8.1 introduces log loss as the score of a probability and shows that a model's confidence can run away while its decisions hold. Section 8.2 shows what an overconfident member then does to an average: it wins every disagreement. Section 8.3 meets the same overconfidence again, in a base model whose in-sample predictions are all 0 or 1 and which the meta-model therefore trusts completely. The second idea is that every combination must be compared, on data it has not seen, with the obvious alternatives. A stopping rule has to watch the metric by which the output will be judged; an average has to beat its best member, not merely the members' mean; and a stack has to beat both the best member chosen by validation and the plain average, whose equal weights cost nothing to estimate. Section 8.2 ends where Section 8.3 begins: once the weakness of equal weights is clear, the natural next step is to learn the weights from the data, and Section 8.3 measures what learning them costs and what it buys.

## Boosting Overfits Its Probabilities Long Before Its Accuracy

<!-- source: ml-14-boosting; hero: ml114-hero.png -->


Gradient boosting builds a model one tree at a time, each new tree fitted to what the current model still gets wrong. Run that process long enough and the model will fit the training data perfectly, noise included. Hence the standard warning, repeated in tutorials and library documentation: *boosting overfits if you run it long enough*, so the number of rounds must be tuned or stopped early. A practitioner meets this sentence whenever a number of rounds is chosen, an early-stopping rule is configured, or a boosted model's validation curve is read to decide whether it has overfitted. Whether the sentence is true depends on what "fits" means. If the curve being watched is the wrong one, early stopping may never trigger, and the model shipped will be the one whose probabilities are worst.

This section first defines the two ways a classifier's output can be scored, by its decisions and by its probabilities, and runs one boosted model for 2,000 rounds while scoring it both ways. It then examines shrinkage, the usual remedy, and measures how the learning rate moves the best round and the best achievable score. Next it asks what an early-stopping rule picks depending on the metric it watches, and what the trade between the two candidates is. Finally it adds label noise, which makes the error rate turn up as well, though far less than the probabilities degrade.

### Two ways to score a classifier


#### Gradient boosting in brief

*Gradient boosting* builds an additive model. For binary classification the model produces a score $F(x)$ on the log-odds scale, and the predicted probability of the positive class is $p(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-F(x)})$. Starting from a constant, each round $m$ fits a small regression tree $h_m$ to the *pseudo-residuals*, the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current scores, which for log loss are simply $y_i - p(x_i)$. The tree's prediction is added with a scaling factor $\nu$, the *learning rate* or *shrinkage*:

$$
F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu\, h_m(x).
$$

Each round moves the scores in the direction that most reduces the training loss. Nothing in the procedure stops it from continuing to reduce that loss once the training data are fitted; it can keep pushing the training scores further from zero.

#### Decisions and probabilities

A classifier makes two kinds of output. One is a decision, the predicted class, here whether $p(x)$ is above or below 0.5; it is scored by the *error rate*, the share of rows on which the decision is wrong. The other is the probability itself, scored by *log loss*, the average negative log-likelihood of the true labels:

$$
\text{log loss} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\Big],
$$

measured in *nats* when the logarithm is natural. Log loss rewards confident correct predictions and charges heavily for confident wrong ones.

The experiment uses scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier` with depth-3 trees on the breast-cancer data used in Chapter 7, split in half, stratified by class, ten times with different seeds. At the common learning rate of 0.1 the model runs for 2,000 rounds, and its staged predictions are scored both ways on the held-out tumours at eight checkpoints. The block prints the averages over the ten splits.

```python
from standarderror.ml import trees as tr

# depth-3 trees, learning rate 0.1, 10 half splits, clean labels
c = tr.boost_curves(lr=0.1, flip=0.0)
for r, e, ll in zip(c["rounds"], c["error"], c["log_loss"]):
    print(f"round {r:>5,}  test error {e:.4f}  test log loss {ll:.3f}")
```

```text
round     1  test error 0.3719  test log loss 0.582
round     3  test error 0.0691  test log loss 0.470
round    10  test error 0.0561  test log loss 0.275
round    30  test error 0.0565  test log loss 0.161
round   100  test error 0.0498  test log loss 0.190
round   300  test error 0.0474  test log loss 0.348
round 1,000  test error 0.0463  test log loss 0.416
round 2,000  test error 0.0463  test log loss 0.416
```

#### The error never turns up; the log loss does

By the error rate there is nothing to stop. The test error falls to 0.046 and is still at its lowest after 2,000 rounds. Apart from a tick of 0.0004 between rounds 10 and 30, under a third of a tumour per split, it never rises at any checkpoint. By that measure, over 2,000 rounds, the warning does not hold for this model.

By log loss the model peaked long ago. Of the checkpoints, the log loss is lowest at 30 rounds, at 0.161. Computed round by round within each split, the best round has a median of 46 across the ten splits. By the end the log loss is 0.416, 2.6 times worse than its best.

#### Why the two scores disagree

The two numbers disagree because they look at different things. The error counts only which side of 0.5 each prediction falls. Log loss charges for confidence: a tumour predicted at 0.999 benign that turns out malignant costs $-\log(1 - 0.999)$, almost seven nats, while the same mistake made with little confidence costs a small fraction of that.

As the rounds go on, the model presumably pushes its training probabilities towards 0 or 1; the training loss was not tracked here, but that is the usual way a training log loss keeps falling once the training labels are already fitted. On test tumours the model mostly keeps the same answers, so the error stands still. The few it gets wrong, it gets wrong more and more confidently, and the rising log loss records exactly that. The figure below shows the log-loss curves at three learning rates.

![At learning rate 0.1 the log loss bottoms at 0.161 after 30 rounds and reaches 0.416 by 2,000. Cutting the rate from 1 to 0.1 lowered the bottom; cutting it again to 0.01 only reached the same bottom later.](ml114-f0-logloss.png){alt="Three U-shaped curves, shifted right as the learning rate falls; the two smaller rates bottom out at about the same level, the largest rate higher."}

### Shrinkage slows overfitting without stopping it


#### Where the bottoms of the curves fall

The usual remedy is *shrinkage*: a small learning rate $\nu$ scales each tree's contribution, so the model approaches the training data slowly, in many small steps rather than a few large ones. It works in the sense Friedman intended when he introduced it: the curves in the figure above are smoother at smaller rates and their bottoms wider. But look at where the bottoms are:

- learning rate 1: best log loss 0.203, after about 3.5 rounds;
- learning rate 0.1: best 0.161, after about 46;
- learning rate 0.01: best 0.162, after about 400.

Each tenfold cut in the learning rate moves the best round about tenfold later. To a first approximation that is what the update rule suggests: with $\nu$ ten times smaller, each tree moves the scores about a tenth as far, so it takes about ten times as many trees to travel the same distance.

#### What each cut buys

The two cuts differ in what else they do. The first, from 1 to 0.1, lowered the best log loss from 0.203 to 0.161, plausibly because at a rate of 1 each tree takes a full step, and the curve is past its bottom within a handful of rounds; the steps are too coarse to stop near the best point. The second, from 0.1 to 0.01, did not lower it at all: 0.161 against 0.162. Below 0.1, a smaller learning rate buys a wider window in which stopping is safe, and it costs proportionally more trees. It does not make running forever safe. At 2,000 rounds the model with learning rate 0.01 is already past its best, at a log loss of 0.272.

That is why early stopping is part of every serious boosting library, and why what it monitors matters. A validation set scored by accuracy would, here, never trigger it, and the model shipped would be the one with the worst probabilities of any checkpoint after the first few dozen. The figure below shows the error curves, which tell the opposite story to the log loss.

![With clean labels the error at 2,000 rounds is the lowest it has been. With flipped labels it rises from 0.071 to 0.088, under two points, while the log loss rises 3.3-fold.](ml114-f1-error.png){alt="Solid lines falling and flattening near 0.04 to 0.05; dashed lines higher, near 0.07 to 0.10, rising slightly at the longest runs."}

### What a stopping rule picks depends on what it watches


#### Two candidate models

*Early stopping* takes the round at which a validation metric is best and keeps the model at that round. Which metric it watches decides which model is shipped. On the clean runs at learning rate 0.1, watching the error rate picks the last checkpoint, 2,000 rounds, because the error is still at its lowest there. That model's test log loss is 0.416. Watching log loss picks a model near the 30-round checkpoint (round 46 in the median split), with test log loss 0.161 and a test error of 0.056, which is 0.010 worse than the long run.

So the trade is real but lopsided: about a point of accuracy against a 2.6-fold difference in the quality of the probabilities. If the output is a decision at a fixed threshold, the long run is the better model here, though a point on about 285 test tumours per split is close to the noise (Section 1.1 shows how wide an error bar such a difference carries). If the output is a probability, and most deployed classifiers are read as risks, ranked, or combined with costs, the short run is better by a wide margin, and only a stopping rule on log loss will find it.

#### The margin explanation

Why does the error keep improving while confidence runs away? The usual answer is the *margin* explanation of Schapire and colleagues, developed for AdaBoost and not tested here. The margin of a training point is, roughly, how far on the correct side of the decision boundary the model's score places it. On this account, later rounds mostly push correctly classified training points further from the boundary, increasing their margins, which changes probabilities a great deal and decisions very little. The boundary itself barely moves near the test points that sit on it, so the error holds; while on test points the model gets wrong, the pushed-out probabilities make each mistake more expensive.

### With noisy labels, the error turns up as well


#### Flipping a tenth of the training labels

Clean labels flatter the error rate: with nothing to memorise but the truth, fitting the training data harder cannot cost many decisions. The next run flips 10% of the training labels at random, leaving the test labels untouched, so boosting has real noise to chase. The block prints the checkpoints from round 30 on.

```python
c = tr.boost_curves(lr=0.1, flip=0.1)   # 10% of labels flipped
for r, e, ll in list(zip(c["rounds"], c["error"], c["log_loss"]))[3:]:
    print(f"round {r:>5,}  test error {e:.4f}  test log loss {ll:.3f}")
```

```text
round    30  test error 0.0709  test log loss 0.240
round   100  test error 0.0804  test log loss 0.211
round   300  test error 0.0856  test log loss 0.248
round 1,000  test error 0.0891  test log loss 0.531
round 2,000  test error 0.0881  test log loss 0.690
```

Now the error turns up as well, from 0.071 at its best to 0.088, a rise of 0.017, less than two points over nearly two thousand extra rounds. The log loss goes from 0.211 to 0.690, more than three times worse. The flipped training labels are fitted as faithfully as the true ones, because to the algorithm a flipped label is just another residual to reduce. Every test tumour that resembles a flipped training tumour then inherits a confident wrong answer.

#### Accuracy and probability are separate questions

Mease and Wyner made the accuracy half of this point in 2008: boosted classifiers often keep improving in error long after the statistical view says they should be overfitting. The probability half is the one that matters whenever the output is used as a risk, such as a credit default probability, a diagnosis likelihood or a click-through rate, because there the model's confidence is the product. The table below collects the runs.

![Read the last two columns and boosting barely overfits. Read the two before them and it overfits badly. Both are true of the same models.](ml114-f2-table.png){alt="A six-row table: the best round grows about tenfold per tenfold drop in learning rate; log loss at 2,000 rounds is far above its best, error at 2,000 rounds close to its best."}

Boosting builds one model out of many trees of the same kind. The next two sections combine finished models of different kinds, first by averaging their probabilities and then by learning how much each should count; the overconfidence measured here will matter in both.

## An Average of Models Beats the Average Model, Not the Best One

<!-- source: ml-41-averaging; hero: ml141-hero.png -->


Section 8.1 combined trees of one kind, in sequence, into a single model. The most common way of combining models of different kinds is simpler still. Train a few different models, average their predicted probabilities, and report the average, on the strength of a sentence that sounds like a theorem: *averaging several models gives a better model than any of them.* A practitioner meets it whenever an ensemble is assembled from the models to hand and adopted without being compared with its strongest member. If the sentence is false, the ensemble can be worse than a model the team already had, and costlier to run.

This section tests the sentence on six classifiers and the handwritten digits. It counts how many averaged combinations beat their best member, traces the worst failures to one mechanism, confidence weighting, and then states the theorem that does stand behind the sentence and shows on a regression problem what it compares the average with. It closes with the conditions under which averaging pays.

### Few averages beat their best member


The data are the UCI handwritten digits bundled with scikit-learn, 1,797 images. Six classifiers are trained on half the images and scored on the other half, over five stratified half splits: regularised logistic regression (C = 0.1), an RBF support vector machine, five-nearest-neighbours (5-NN), a random forest of 300 trees, Gaussian naive Bayes and a single, fully grown decision tree. Their accuracies range from 0.825 for the tree to 0.981 for nearest neighbours. The SVM's probabilities come from *Platt scaling*, a logistic regression fitted from its decision values to the labels on held-out folds of the training data; the other models output class probabilities natively.

For every combination of two or more models, 57 in all, the code averages the members' class probabilities with equal weights, predicts the class with the largest average, and compares the result's accuracy with two reference points: the best member's accuracy and the members' mean accuracy.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

t = combine.averaging_table()   # 57 combinations, 5 half-splits
best = sum(r["acc"] > r["best_acc"] for r in t)
below = sum(r["acc"] < r["mean_acc"] for r in t)
print(f"beat their best member: {best} of {len(t)}")
print(f"below their members' mean accuracy: {below} of {len(t)}")
top = max(t, key=lambda r: r["acc"])
print(f"best combination: {' + '.join(top['members'])}, "
      f"{top['acc']:.4f} against its best member's {top['best_acc']:.4f}")
```

```text
beat their best member: 11 of 57
below their members' mean accuracy: 8 of 57
best combination: SVM + 5-NN + forest, 0.9873 against its best member's 0.9813
```

Only 11 of the 57 averages beat the best model they contain. The best average, of the SVM, nearest neighbours and the forest, gained 0.0060 over nearest neighbours alone, about half a point. And 8 averages did worse than the *mean* accuracy of their own members, a reference point an average is widely assumed to beat by construction. The figure below plots each combination against its best member; most of the damage is done by combinations containing the tree.

![One of the 31 combinations containing the decision tree beat its best member, pairing it with naive Bayes; 11 of 57 combinations did.](ml141-f0-combos.png){alt="Scatter of 57 combinations. Combinations containing the decision tree range from about 0.83 to 0.98, all below the line but one; those with naive Bayes but no tree sit around 0.85 to 0.98; those with neither sit near or slightly above the line."}

### Averaging probabilities weights each model by its confidence


#### A certain model wins every disagreement

The worst averages contain the decision tree, and the worst of those are pairs. Averaged with logistic regression, the SVM or the forest, the pair scored exactly the tree's accuracy, 0.825.

The reason is in what a fully grown tree predicts. It splits until every leaf holds one class, and the probability it reports is the class proportion in the leaf a new image reaches. Every leaf is pure, so the tree gives probability 1 to one digit and 0 to the rest, on 100% of the digits. The other three models are almost never that certain; the forest is certain on 0.3% of digits. The next block averages the SVM and the tree and records, where the two disagree, whose label the average adopted.

```python
p = combine.averaging_pair("SVM", "tree")
print(f"SVM + tree: accuracy {p['acc']:.4f}")
print(f"on {p['disagree']} disagreements the average sided with "
      f"the tree {p['with_b']:.0%} of the time")
```

```text
SVM + tree: accuracy 0.8251
on 819 disagreements the average sided with the tree 100% of the time
```

On all 819 disagreements the average sided with the tree. The arithmetic makes this inevitable. Write $p_k$ for the tree's probability of class $k$ and $q_k$ for the SVM's; the average gives class $k$ the value $(p_k + q_k)/2$. The tree's class has $p_k = 1$ and so receives at least one half. Any other class $j$ has $p_j = 0$ and receives $q_j/2$, which reaches one half only if the SVM is certain of $j$. An average of probabilities is therefore not a vote of equals: a member's influence on a row grows with how far its probabilities are from uniform, and a member certain about everything has the largest possible influence everywhere.

#### Larger averages dilute the certain member, but not enough

In an average of $M$ members, the tree's class is guaranteed only a share of $1/M$, and confident agreement among the others can outweigh it. Averages containing the tree recover as members are added, but only one of the 31 combinations containing the tree beat its best member: the tree with naive Bayes, the two weakest models of the six.

Naive Bayes shows the same mechanism to a lesser degree. It is certain about 52% of digits; averaged with the SVM it won 86% of their disagreements, and the pair scored 0.857. The table below collects each member's accuracy, log loss and share of certain predictions, and four averages with the share of disagreements each second member won.

![The tree is certain about every digit, so paired with a model that is almost never certain it wins every disagreement. Calibrating it removes that, but the pair still trails the SVM alone.](ml141-f2-table.png){alt="An eleven-row table: seven members with accuracy, log loss and share of certain predictions, then four averages with the share of disagreements won by the second model."}

#### Calibration removes the certainty, not the weakness

*Isotonic recalibration* fits a non-decreasing step function from a model's scores to observed class frequencies. Done by internal cross-validation, as here with five folds, the mapping is learned on data the tree was not fitted on, so it reflects how often the tree's confident predictions are right (Section 11.3 examines how many rows such a recalibration needs). Recalibrated, the tree is certain about no digit, and the SVM-plus-tree average rises to 0.973.

That is far better, and still below the SVM alone, 0.977. Calibration fixed the tree's influence but not its quality: averaging with a weaker model is a cost unless it is right where the stronger one is wrong.

### What the theorem guarantees: the average member, not the best


#### The ambiguity decomposition

There is a real theorem behind the sentence, and it says less. Take $M$ regression models $f_1, \dots, f_M$ and their equally weighted average $\bar f = \frac{1}{M}\sum_m f_m$. Krogh and Vedelsby showed in 1995 that at every point with target $y$,

$$
(\bar f - y)^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(f_m - y)^2 \;-\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(f_m - \bar f)^2 .
$$

The first term is the mean member's squared error; the second is the members' spread around their average, which they called the *ambiguity*. The identity follows from writing $f_m - y = (f_m - \bar f) + (\bar f - y)$ and squaring: the cross terms vanish because the deviations $f_m - \bar f$ sum to zero. Averaged over test points it holds for mean squared error too. Since the ambiguity cannot be negative, the average is never worse than the average member, and it improves on that member by exactly as much as the members disagree. The best member does not appear.

The next block checks the identity on the diabetes data of Efron and colleagues, one half split, with four regressors: ridge regression with a cross-validated penalty, 10-nearest-neighbours, a random forest and a single tree.

```python
a = combine.ambiguity()          # diabetes, four regressors
print(f"mean member {a['mean_member']:.1f} - spread "
      f"{a['ambiguity']:.1f} = average {a['ensemble']:.1f} "
      f"(gap {a['identity_gap']:.0e})")
print("members: " + ", ".join(f"{k} {v:.0f}"
                              for k, v in a["members"].items()))
```

```text
mean member 4018.6 - spread 576.3 = average 3442.4 (gap 9e-13)
members: ridge 3041, 10-NN 3152, forest 3552, tree 6329
```

The identity holds to twelve decimal places. The average improves on the mean member by 576, all of it the members' disagreement, and is still worse than ridge regression alone, 3442 against 3041. The tree's error was so large that the mean member started far behind the best one, and the spread did not close the gap, as the figure below shows.

![The ambiguity decomposition on diabetes: 4019 − 576 = 3442, to within 9e-13; ridge alone scored 3041.](ml141-f1-ambiguity.png){alt="Bars for ridge, 10-NN, forest and tree, the tree much higher; a bar for the mean member and a lower bar for the average, which is still above ridge."}

#### Log loss has a similar guarantee; accuracy has none

Log loss, introduced in Section 8.1 above, is the mean of $-\log \hat p_i$, where $\hat p_i$ is the probability given to row $i$'s true class. Because $-\log p$ is convex, its value at an average of probabilities is at most the average of its values (Jensen's inequality), so the averaged probabilities' log loss cannot exceed the members' mean. On the digits it was at or below the members' mean in all 57 combinations, and below the best member's in 13. Accuracy, which depends only on which class has the largest probability, is not convex and has no such guarantee; that is how 8 combinations fell below their members' mean.

### When averaging pays


The combinations that beat their best member share two properties: members of similar quality, wrong on different digits. The SVM and nearest neighbours, 0.977 and 0.981 alone, disagreed on 123 digits across the five splits and averaged to 0.9867, better than either. By analogy with the squared-error decomposition, when members have similar errors the mean member is close to the best, so even modest disagreement can carry the average past both. Dietterich's account of why ensembles work rests on exactly these conditions, *accuracy* and *diversity*, and random forests are built to meet them: many trees of equal quality, made different on purpose.

The useful version of the sentence is conditional. An average of models that are about equally good and differently wrong is usually better than each; an average that includes a much weaker or much more confident model is usually worse than the best. Calibrating members first stops confidence from deciding the vote, and weighting them by validated performance stops a weak model diluting a strong one. Learning those weights is stacking, the subject of the next section.

## Stacking Learns to Pick the Best Model, Given Honest Predictions

<!-- source: ml-42-stacking; hero: ml142-hero.png -->


Section 8.2 above ended with an average of models dragged down by a member that was too weak or too sure of itself. The obvious repair is to stop weighting members equally and let the data decide how much each one counts. Wolpert called this *stacked generalisation* in 1992: fit a second model, the *meta-model*, whose inputs are the base models' predictions and whose target is the label. Stacking is a standard ingredient of competition-winning pipelines, and it is sold with the sentence this section tests: *stacking learns the best way to combine models.* If the sentence is false, a stack can quietly hand its decisions to the worst model in the pool, or cost several times the compute of a single model for no gain.

This section fits a meta-model in two ways, on the base models' in-sample predictions and on their out-of-fold predictions, and compares both with the members. It then reads the meta-model's weights to see what each version trusted, asks whether the honest version actually combines or merely selects, and sets it against the plain average. It ends with the two baselines a stack has to beat and the price of fitting it honestly.

### Two ways to build the meta-model's inputs


The task is the simulated one from Section 5.1: two Gaussian classes in 10 dimensions with a curved Bayes boundary and a Bayes error of 0.268, the lowest error any classifier can achieve. Training sets have 100, 300, 1,000 and 3,000 rows (20, 10, 6 and 3 data sets of each size), and every model is scored on the same 20,000 separate test rows.

There are four base models: logistic regression, an RBF support vector machine with Platt-scaled probabilities, a random forest of 200 trees, and 1-nearest-neighbour (1-NN). The meta-model is a logistic regression with a very weak penalty (C = 10,000) whose inputs are the four base models' predicted probabilities of the positive class. The question is which predictions it is fitted on.

- **In-sample predictions.** Each base model is fitted on the training rows and then predicts those same rows. This is quick, and it is what a naive implementation does.
- **Out-of-fold predictions.** The training rows are split into five stratified folds. For each fold, a copy of each base model is trained on the other four folds and predicts the held-out fold, so every training row's prediction comes from a model that never saw that row. The base models are then refitted on all rows to produce test-time inputs.

The code below runs both versions at each training size and prints the accuracy of the SVM, of 1-NN and of the two stacks.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

for r in combine.stacking_study(with_1nn=True):
    acc = r["acc"]
    print(f"{r['n']:5d} rows: SVM {acc['SVM']:.3f}, 1-NN {acc['1-NN']:.3f}"
          f" | stacks {acc['stack, in-sample']:.3f} (in-sample), "
          f"{acc['stack, out-of-fold']:.3f} (OOF)")
```

```text
  100 rows: SVM 0.597, 1-NN 0.566 | stacks 0.566 (in-sample), 0.589 (OOF)
  300 rows: SVM 0.672, 1-NN 0.577 | stacks 0.577 (in-sample), 0.670 (OOF)
 1000 rows: SVM 0.702, 1-NN 0.594 | stacks 0.594 (in-sample), 0.702 (OOF)
 3000 rows: SVM 0.718, 1-NN 0.600 | stacks 0.600 (in-sample), 0.718 (OOF)
```

On in-sample predictions the stack's accuracy equals 1-NN's at every size, to the last digit: 0.594 at 1,000 rows, the worst of the four models, while the SVM scores 0.702. On out-of-fold predictions the stack scores 0.702, the SVM's accuracy. The same procedure, fed two kinds of input, became two different single models, as the figure below shows.

![On in-sample predictions the stack became 1-NN (0.594 at 1,000 rows); on out-of-fold ones, the SVM (0.702 against 0.702). Numbers in the figure are the series' episode numbers; Appendix B maps them to sections.](ml142-f0-stack.png){alt="The in-sample stack's line lies exactly on the 1-NN line, lowest of all; the out-of-fold stack's line lies on the SVM's from 300 rows on; the average lies between."}

### The meta-model rewards whichever model has seen the answers


#### In-sample, memorisation looks like skill

The meta-model's weights explain the result. A 1-NN classifier predicts each training row from its nearest neighbour in the training set, and each training row is its own nearest neighbour. On in-sample predictions 1-NN is therefore right about every training row, and its probabilities, like the single tree's in Section 8.2, are all 0 or 1. The forest, whose trees are grown deep, is nearly as perfect on its own training rows.

The meta-model thus sees one input that predicts the label without error. Because that input separates the training labels perfectly, the unpenalised likelihood would push its weight towards infinity, as Section 6.1 showed for logistic regression on separable data; the weak penalty keeps it finite, at +12.9. On test rows the meta-model then reproduces 1-NN, which there is the weakest model of the four. Breiman made the point for regression in 1996: the inputs to the meta-model, which he called the level-one data, must come from predictions the base models did not fit, or the combination rewards overfitting.

The mechanism is not specific to 1-NN. Replace 1-NN with 25-NN, which averages over 25 neighbours and cannot memorise its training rows, and the same mistake picks the next best memoriser: the in-sample meta-model gives the forest a weight of +28.5. Its log loss, 1.089 at 1,000 rows, is nearly twice the SVM's 0.570 and worse than any member's. The meta-model is not learning which model is good; it is learning which model has seen the answers.

#### Out-of-fold, the weight goes to the best model

On out-of-fold predictions every base model is judged on rows it did not see, so its inputs to the meta-model are as noisy as its test predictions will be. The weights are then logistic regression −0.24, SVM +4.56, forest +0.80 and 1-NN −0.18. Almost all the weight is on the SVM, and 1-NN gets nothing. ### Honest stacking is mostly selection


Out-of-fold stacking avoids the trap. Whether it *combines* the models, producing something better than any of them, is a separate question. The next block uses the pool with 25-NN in place of 1-NN, so that no member is certain, and compares the out-of-fold stack with the best member at each size (the one with the highest test accuracy) and with a plain average of the four members' probabilities.

```python
for r in combine.stacking_study(with_1nn=False):   # 25-NN instead
    acc = r["acc"]
    best = r["best"]
    print(f"{r['n']:5d} rows: best member {best} {acc[best]:.3f}"
          f" | out-of-fold stack {acc['stack, out-of-fold']:.3f}, "
          f"average {acc['average']:.3f}")
```

  300 rows: best member SVM 0.672 | out-of-fold stack 0.669, average 0.660

 3000 rows: best member SVM 0.718 | out-of-fold stack 0.718, average 0.710

#### From 300 rows, the stack selects the SVM

From 300 rows up, in both pools, the out-of-fold stack is within 0.0035 of the best member and never more than 0.001 above it. The meta-model has learned to pick the SVM, which choosing the model with the best validation score would also have done.

#### At 100 rows, estimating the weights costs more than it gains

At 100 rows the stack is worse than the best member: 0.589 against the forest's 0.602 in the pool with 1-NN. That comparison flatters the best member slightly, since a practitioner would have had to identify the forest by validation, with its own noise. But the stack's loss has a clear source. With 100 rows the out-of-fold predictions are noisy, each made by a model trained on four fifths of them, and the meta-model's four weights are estimated from those same 100 rows. The stack pays for estimating the combination.

#### A plain average costs nothing to estimate

The plain average shows the other side of that trade. In the pool without 1-NN, at 100 rows, it scores 0.606, above every member and 0.016 above the stack, because equal weights cost nothing to estimate. From 300 rows on, logistic regression and 25-NN fall well behind the SVM, and equal weights let them drag the average below it, 0.694 against 0.702 at 1,000 rows, the dilution Section 8.2 described.

Forecasters know this pattern as the *forecast combination puzzle*: a simple average of forecasts often beats the weights estimated to combine them optimally. Smith and Wallis traced the puzzle to the estimation error in the weights, exactly the cost visible at 100 rows here. The table below collects the comparison at all four sizes.

### When stacking earns its place


Stacking improves on its best member when the members are good in different places: one model better in one region of the inputs, another elsewhere, and enough rows for the meta-model to learn where. On this task the SVM is best almost everywhere, so the right combination is close to "use the SVM", and the stack found it. On tasks where diverse models of similar quality each capture part of the structure, as in many competition data sets, out-of-fold stacking can beat every member; the measurement here says what to compare it with before believing that it has.

The comparison has two baselines, not one. The stack should beat the best member chosen by validation, or it is an expensive way of choosing. And it should beat the plain average, or its weights cost more to estimate than they gain.

Honest stacking also has a price that is easy to forget. With five folds, every base model is fitted once per fold and once more on all the rows, six fits instead of one. And the stack's own score needs data that played no part in building it; here that was 20,000 separate test rows. A stack scored on the same folds that produced its inputs would add to everything measured above the optimism Section 4.1 measured, where the best score in a search reflects the search's luck as well as the model's quality.

Comparing a stack with its best member, or any two models with each other, is itself a statistical comparison, and Section 2.2 showed why the folds of a cross-validation cannot be treated as independent samples when it is made. All three combinations in this chapter were built from trees, linear models, nearest neighbours and support vector machines; Chapter 9 turns to the neural network, and to what it takes to train one.

## In practice

### Stopping a boosted model

- **Stop on the metric the output will be judged by.** If the probabilities are used, as risks, rankings or inputs to a cost calculation, early-stop on validation log loss or another *proper scoring rule*, a score that is optimised in expectation by reporting the true probabilities (the Brier score is another). Do not stop on accuracy.
- **Plot both curves.** For any boosted model, plot staged validation error and staged validation log loss against the number of rounds. If they bottom out far apart, the choice of stopping metric decides which model you ship.
- **Treat the learning rate as a speed, not a safeguard.** A smaller rate moves the best round later in proportion and widens the window around it, but the model still passes its best. Going below 0.1 bought nothing in the best log loss in Section 8.1.
- **Expect label noise to make matters worse.** With noisy labels, long runs cost accuracy as well, and probabilities much more.

### Confidence and calibration

- **Check calibration before using a boosted model's probabilities**, and recalibrate on held-out data if needed; Section 11.3 measures how many rows recalibration requires.
- **Check each ensemble member's confidence.** Compute the share of predictions each makes with probability above 0.99. A member certain about most rows will dominate an average wherever it disagrees.
- **Calibrate members before averaging probabilities,** especially single trees and naive Bayes. Calibration removes overconfidence; it does not make a weak member strong.

### Judging a combination

- **Keep two baselines.** Score the ensemble or stack, the single model with the best validation score and a plain average on the same held-out rows, not only against the members' mean. Keep the combination only if it beats both baselines; an average that does not beat its best member is cost without gain.
- **Measure diversity directly:** the share of rows on which each pair of members disagrees. Averaging helps when members of similar quality disagree, and hurts when the disagreement comes from a much weaker member.
- **Do not cite the ambiguity decomposition as a promise of gains over the best model.** It guarantees improvement over the mean member for squared error, as convexity does for log loss; nothing is guaranteed for accuracy.

### Fitting a stack

- **Fit the meta-model on out-of-fold predictions only.** In-sample predictions reward memorisation; the meta-model will hand its decisions to whichever base model fitted its training rows most closely.
- **Print the meta-model's weights.** If they concentrate on one model, the stack is selecting, and that model alone is the baseline to beat. A large weight on 1-NN, a deep tree or a deep forest can be a sign of leaked in-sample predictions.
- **Be sceptical of stacks on small data.** With few rows, the noise in the weights can cost more than the combination gains; a plain average or a single validated model may do better.
- **Budget for the cost.** Count the extra fits, and keep a test set that neither the base models nor the meta-model ever saw.

## Limits of the evidence

### Boosting (Section 8.1)

- **One dataset.** Breast cancer is easy, with a test error under 5%. On harder data, where many test rows sit near the boundary, the error can be expected to turn up sooner and further, as it did here once labels were flipped. That the ordering, log loss first and error later, carries over to other data is a reasonable expectation, not something measured here.
- **Probabilities can be repaired afterwards.** Calibrating a booster on held-out data, by Platt scaling or isotonic regression (both described in Section 8.2), improved its log loss substantially in Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana's comparison, though the boosting they studied distorted probabilities towards the middle rather than towards 0 and 1, and recalibration was not run here. It needs a held-out set, and it repairs the symptom, not the training.
- **Checkpoints, not every round.** The curves are sampled at eight round counts, and the best log loss quoted is the lowest of those checkpoints on the curve averaged over splits. The best round is computed from every round, per split, and its median is reported. The two need not coincide: at a learning rate of 0.1 the best checkpoint is 30 rounds and the median best round 46.
- **Other boosters regularise more by default.** Libraries with leaf-weight penalties, row subsampling and minimum leaf sizes would be expected to reach the same place more slowly; none was run here. scikit-learn's classic implementation, with its defaults, is the plainest version of the algorithm.

### Averaging (Section 8.2)

- **One data set where a single model is excellent.** Nearest neighbours alone scores 0.98 on the digits, leaving little room for any combination to gain. Harder tasks with more balanced members may leave more room; that was not measured here.
- **Unweighted averages.** Every member counted equally. Weighted averages and stacking are the obvious refinements, and Section 8.3 measures their failure modes.
- **A deliberately bad member.** A fully grown single tree is not something anyone would put in an ensemble on purpose; it is here because its certainty makes the mechanism visible. Any overconfident member, such as an unregularised boosted model late in training, does the same in a milder form.
- **Five splits.** The counts of combinations above and below their best member come from one set of five half splits and could shift with others; the tree's capture of every pair it joined with a less certain model follows from its probabilities and would not.

### Stacking (Section 8.3)

- **One task with one dominant model.** The SVM's lead from 300 rows on is large, which makes selection the right answer. With members of equal quality and complementary errors, the out-of-fold stack has more to gain and the average is harder to beat.
- **A linear meta-model on probabilities.** Meta-models on log-odds, with non-negativity constraints on the weights (as Breiman recommended), or with the original features as extra inputs, behave differently; the in-sample trap is the same for all of them.
- **Few repetitions at large sizes.** There are three data sets at 3,000 rows, and differences of a thousandth there are within noise. The in-sample stack's exact match to 1-NN is not.
- **Accuracy and log loss only.** A stack tuned for AUC or for a cost-weighted metric would choose differently, by the same mechanism.

## Key points

1. With clean labels, the test error of gradient boosting never turned up from round 30 to round 2,000, ending at 0.046, while test log loss was lowest within the first few dozen rounds and 2.6 times worse at 2,000. The model's decisions held; its confidence ran away.
2. Each tenfold cut in the learning rate moved the best round about tenfold later. Cutting it from 1 to 0.1 lowered the best log loss; cutting it from 0.1 to 0.01 left it where it was. Shrinkage widens the window; it does not remove the need to stop.
3. With 10% label noise the error rose by under two points and the log loss more than tripled. Stop boosting on the metric the output will be judged by: if the probabilities are used, that is log loss or another proper score, not accuracy.
4. Of 57 averaged combinations of six classifiers on the digits, 11 beat their best member and 8 fell below their members' mean accuracy.
5. Averaging probabilities weights each model by its confidence. A tree certain about every digit won all 819 disagreements with the SVM, the pair scored 0.825, and only one of the 31 combinations containing the tree beat its best member.
6. The guarantee behind averaging is against the mean member: for squared error, the average's error is the mean member's minus their spread, exactly, and on diabetes that still left it behind ridge regression alone. Log loss has a similar guarantee by convexity; accuracy has none.
7. Fitted on in-sample predictions, a meta-model gave its largest weight to the model that had memorised the most: 1-NN (weight +12.9), whose accuracy it reproduced exactly, or the forest when 1-NN was absent.
8. Fitted on out-of-fold predictions, it chose the SVM and matched it to within 0.0035 from 300 rows on, never more than 0.001 above it; at 100 rows it lost 0.013 to the best member. Honest stacking on this task was selection.
9. A plain average beat every member at 100 rows when no member was certain, and trailed the best member once the weak members fell behind.
10. Combine models of similar quality that make different mistakes, calibrate them first, and keep a combination only if it beats both the best single model and the plain average on data neither has seen.
