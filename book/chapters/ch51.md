# A Benchmark Is a Sample of Questions

Language models are compared by their benchmark scores, and the comparison rests on one sentence: *the model with the higher benchmark score is the better model.* A practitioner meets it when choosing between two hosted models, when deciding whether a fine-tuned version beats its base, and whenever a leaderboard is read from the top. If it fails, models are switched, fine-tunes shipped and claims published on the strength of which questions happened to be in the benchmark. Chapter 1 showed that a test score on 360 digits is an estimate with an error bar, and every chapter since has evaluated a model the reader trained on a test set the reader drew. Part XI changes both: it evaluates language models that most readers did not train, on benchmarks they did not write, and asks whether the habits of the earlier chapters survive.

This chapter first sets up a family of models whose true scores are known exactly. It then shows that each model's error bar is right while the leaderboard built from the scores is mostly noise; that comparing two models on the same questions recovers a real difference an unpaired test almost never finds; and that asking each question several ways multiplies the rows without multiplying the evidence.

## A family of models whose true scores are known

The models are small arithmetic transformers trained in this book's repository, each with 804,096 parameters, trained on generated arithmetic lines with held-out test problems. Tiny models suit the question: every answer can be checked exactly, so grading involves no judgement, and every model can be graded on thousands of questions in a minute. Each evaluation habit can therefore be compared with the quantity it is trying to estimate.

There are ten models: the *main model*; its *leaky twin*, trained on the same lines in a different order; five copies of the main model, each trained for 600 more steps under a different seed, the way several teams might each fine-tune one base model (*fine-tuning* is continuing to train an already trained model, usually briefly); and three snapshots of a separate, much shorter run of 6,000 steps.

The truth is a *pool* of 2,500 problems, 500 from each of five tasks (multiplication, order of operations, linear equations, subtraction and a probe of three-digit carries). Each model's *pool score*, the fraction of the pool it answers correctly, is treated as its true score. A benchmark is a sample of questions from this pool, as a real benchmark is a sample from the questions that could have been written. The code builds the ten-by-2,500 matrix of right-or-wrong results and prints each pool score.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import llmeval as le

names, m, tasks = le.item_matrix()     # 10 models x 2,500 questions
for name, row in zip(names, m):
    print(f"{name:8s} {row.mean():.4f}")
```

```text
clean    0.6888
cont0    0.6504
cont1    0.6636
cont2    0.6584
cont3    0.6464
cont4    0.6516
leaky    0.6752
s1_6000  0.2284
s1_4500  0.1136
s1_3000  0.0548
```

`clean` is the main model, `cont0` to `cont4` its fine-tuned copies, `leaky` the twin, and the last three rows the short run. The seven strong models are close: the main model leads the next best by 0.014, and the copies sit between 0.646 and 0.664. Extra training made every copy slightly worse than its starting point, probably because the main run had ended its learning-rate schedule near zero and the copies restarted at a constant rate. That matters here only because it leaves the copies close to one another and a little below the main model, the situation in which leaderboards are hardest to read.

## Each score's error bar is right; the ranking is not

### The interval for one model's score

A benchmark score is a proportion. If $n$ questions are drawn independently, each is a Bernoulli trial succeeding with probability $p$, the pool score, and the observed score $\hat p$ has the binomial standard error of Chapter 1. The textbook 95% interval, the *Wald interval*, is

$$
\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}.
$$

Its *coverage* is the fraction of repeated benchmarks on which it contains the true value; a correct 95% interval covers 0.95 of the time.

Across 2,000 benchmarks of 100 questions, the ten models' intervals covered their pool scores 94.2% of the time on average, and 91.3% to 95.3% for individual models. The low end belongs to the weakest models: the Wald interval undercovers for proportions near zero, where its normal approximation is poor. At every size from 50 to 2,500 questions the average stayed between 94.2% and 95.4%. One score's error bar is, near enough, right.

### The leaderboard built from those scores

The next block draws four benchmarks of 100 questions and prints the top four models on each.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for _ in range(4):                     # four benchmarks of 100 questions
    idx = rng.integers(0, m.shape[1], 100)
    s = m[:, idx].mean(1)
    print(" > ".join(names[i] for i in np.argsort(-s)[:4]))
```

```text
clean > cont2 > cont0 > cont3
leaky > cont0 > cont1 > cont2
clean > cont1 > cont2 > leaky
leaky > clean > cont1 > cont4
```

Four draws give four leaderboards, and on two of them the model with the highest pool score is not first. Over many draws, on 100 questions the best model came first in **50%** of benchmarks. Among the seven close models, 27% of pairs came out in the wrong order and the full order was right in 0.4% of benchmarks. Even on a resample the size of the pool, 2,500 questions drawn with replacement, the best model came first 94% of the time and the seven were in true order in 14%.

Nothing is wrong with the intervals; a leaderboard asks a different question. An interval answers "where is this model's score?" A leaderboard answers "which of these models is higher?" for every pair at once. Whether a pair comes out in the right order depends on the gap between their true scores relative to the noise in the difference of their observed scores, and for close models that noise is large compared with the gap, so the two intervals overlap almost entirely. A correct full ranking needs every pairwise comparison to land the right way at once, which is why it is so much rarer than a correct winner. Reading a ranking off overlapping intervals is reading noise. The figure below shows the three quantities against benchmark size.

![On 100 questions the models' intervals covered their true scores 94% of the time on average, and the best model came first in 50% of benchmarks.](ml151-f0-board.png){alt="Four lines against benchmark size. Interval coverage is flat near 0.95. The best model ranked first rises from about 0.44 at 50 questions to 0.95 at 2,500; the seven close models in true order stays at or below about 0.14."}

## Comparing two models on the same questions

### The difference has its own standard error

The right question for a pair of models is about their difference. The usual mistake, one of the first that Miller's 2024 guide to error bars for language-model evaluations warns against, is to compute its standard error as if the two scores came from different questions, adding the variances: $\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_a^2 + \mathrm{SE}_b^2}$. That is the *unpaired* standard error, correct only for independent scores. Here both models answered the same questions and failed on the same hard ones: the correlation between the main model's right-or-wrong column and its fine-tuned copy's was 0.79.

A *paired* comparison uses that correlation. Let $D_i$ be $+1$ if only the first model answers question $i$ correctly, $-1$ if only the second does, and $0$ if they agree. The paired standard error is the standard error of the mean of the $D_i$, $\mathrm{sd}(D)/\sqrt{n}$. Because $\mathrm{Var}(a_i - b_i) = \mathrm{Var}(a_i) + \mathrm{Var}(b_i) - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(a_i, b_i)$, a strong positive correlation removes most of the variance the unpaired formula counts, and only questions where the models disagree contribute. *McNemar's test* (1947), met in Chapter 1, is the same idea as a test: in its exact form, a binomial test of whether the disagreements split evenly.

The block below draws one benchmark of 300 questions and compares the main model with its first fine-tuned copy both ways.

```python
a, b = m[names.index("clean")], m[names.index("cont0")]
idx = np.random.default_rng(7).integers(0, len(a), 300)
r = le.paired_difference(a[idx], b[idx])
print(f"gap {r['diff']:+.3f}  unpaired SE {r['se_unpaired']:.3f}  "
      f"paired SE {r['se_paired']:.3f}")
print(f"right only for main {r['only_a']}, only for copy {r['only_b']}"
      f", McNemar p = {r['mcnemar_p']:.4f}")
```

```text
gap +0.063  unpaired SE 0.038  paired SE 0.019
right only for main 27, only for copy 8, McNemar p = 0.0019
```

On this draw the paired standard error is half the unpaired one. The main model alone was right on 27 questions and the copy alone on 8; on all the others they agreed, and McNemar's test gives p = 0.0019.

### How often each test finds a real gap

On the pool the main model beat its fine-tuned copy by 0.0384, a real difference by construction. Across 4,000 benchmarks of 300 questions the measured gap varied with a standard deviation of 0.0176. The paired standard error at that size is 0.0178, about right; the unpaired one is 0.0384, more than twice too large. A test that overstates the noise that much rarely declares a difference: the unpaired test found the real gap in 2.0% of benchmarks and the paired test in **60%**, as the figure below shows.

![Against the fine-tuned copy, the paired standard error matches the actual spread and the paired test detects the gap far more often than the unpaired one. Standard errors are scaled from the pool to 300 questions; 'error corr.' is the correlation between the two models' right-or-wrong columns.](ml151-f1-paired.png){alt="Two rows. Against the fine-tuned copy, the paired standard error matches the actual spread and the paired test detects the gap far more often than the unpaired one."}

Pairing is not magic. The leaky twin is 0.0136 behind the main model on the pool, so here it is known to be behind; but had the 2,500 questions been a sample from a larger population, McNemar's test on all of them would have given p = 0.12, and a 300-question benchmark put the twin ahead in 26% of draws. Chapter 44 met the opposite failure, a t-test on cross-validation folds that found differences between equal algorithms because the folds share training data and are not independent. Here the questions are independent, the paired test is the right one, and it can still correctly say "not enough evidence".

## Five prompts per question are not five questions

Language-model evaluations often ask each question several ways: several prompt templates, several samples at a nonzero temperature, several orderings of the options. The rows multiply, and an error bar computed as if each row were independent shrinks. To measure what that buys, the main model answered 600 questions, 200 each from multiplication, mixed operations and three-digit carries, five times, each with a different context of preceding lines.

The answers barely depended on the context: 42% of questions were answered right all five times and 45% wrong all five times, leaving 12% that the context could change. The five rows from one question are nearly copies of one another. The *cluster-robust standard error* accounts for this by treating each question's rows as one *cluster* and measuring variation between clusters rather than between rows; with equal rows per question, it amounts to averaging each question's rows and taking the ordinary standard error over questions. When rows within a cluster are highly correlated, extra rows add little information, and this standard error stays close to the one-row-per-question value.

A benchmark of 100 such questions has 500 rows. Treating them as independent gave a standard error of 0.0223; the actual spread of the score across 4,000 redrawn benchmarks was 0.0462, **2.1 times** larger. The cluster-robust standard error gave 0.0475, matching the spread. With 20 questions the three numbers were 0.0488, 0.1062 and 0.1048. This is Chapter 37's lesson, where rows from the same people were not new people, applied to questions: rows from the same question are not new questions.

![Of 600 questions, 42% were answered right in all five contexts and 45% in none, so the naive standard error is about half the actual spread and the cluster-robust one matches it.](ml151-f2-cluster.png){alt="Two groups of three bars. In each, the naive bar is about half the height of the other two, which match."}

A benchmark is a sample of questions, and its score inherits that sample's noise. Chapter 52 asks what happens when the questions are not a fresh sample at all, because the model saw them in training.

## In practice

- **Report a comparison as a difference with a paired interval.** Export per-question results for both models on the same questions and report the mean difference with its paired standard error, not two headline scores with separate error bars.
- **Count the disagreements.** The questions only one model got right, in each direction, carry all the evidence; McNemar's exact test is the test on them.
- **Treat leaderboard positions inside each other's intervals as ties.** A correct interval for each score says nothing about the correctness of the ranks.
- **Cluster by question.** When a question is asked under several prompts, samples or option orders, compute the standard error from per-question averages or with a cluster-robust estimator. Do the same for families of near-duplicate questions written from one template.

## Limits of the evidence

- **A pool, not a population.** The truth here is a fixed pool of 2,500 problems from five tasks. A real benchmark is a sample from a population of questions nobody has written down, and its questions are often chosen rather than drawn, which adds a bias that no error bar describes.
- **Exact answers.** Grading was by exact match, so every error bar here is pure question sampling. Graders that disagree with one another add their own variance.
- **Models of one family.** All ten models were trained on the same lines, and six are the main model or copies of it, so their errors are more correlated than those of models from different developers. The more correlated the errors, the more pairing gains; for unrelated models the gain is smaller, but the paired test is still the right one.
- **Questions drawn independently.** Benchmarks often contain families of near-duplicate questions written from one template, which cluster in the same way as the repeated prompts here.

## Key points

1. The models' 95% intervals covered their true scores about 95% of the time on average at every benchmark size; one score's error bar is right.
2. The ranking is not: on 100 questions the best of ten models ranked first in only 50% of benchmarks, and seven close models came out in true order in 0.4%.
3. On 300 shared questions the paired test detected a real 0.038 gap in 60% of benchmarks and the unpaired one in 2.0%; the paired standard error matched the actual spread.
4. Five prompts per question gave a true standard error 2.1 times the naive one, and the cluster-robust standard error matched it.
5. Report a comparison as a difference with a paired interval, cluster rows by question, and treat leaderboard positions inside each other's intervals as ties.

## Further reading

- McNemar, "Note on the sampling error of the difference between correlated proportions or percentages", *Psychometrika* (1947): the original paired test on discordant counts.
- Dietterich, "Approximate statistical tests for comparing supervised classification learning algorithms", *Neural Computation* (1998): a comparison of tests for classifier differences, and the background to Chapter 44.
- Miller, "Adding error bars to evals" (Anthropic, 2024): standard errors, paired differences and clustered questions for language-model evaluations.
- Madaan et al., "Quantifying variance in evaluation benchmarks" (2024): how much benchmark scores vary, and what that means for comparing models.
