# Generalisation in a Network

Chapter 9 was about training a network: initialising it, choosing its learning rate, batch size and optimiser, and adding normalisation and dropout. This chapter asks what the trained network has learned, and what decides it. A practitioner faces that question every time a training run ends. The training curve says how well the network fits the data it was given; the decisions to stop, to trust the model, or to enlarge the training set with transformed copies rest on beliefs about how that fit relates to performance on data the network has not seen. Three of those beliefs are tested here:

- Section 10.1: *a network that generalises could not have fit noise*;
- Section 10.2: *once training accuracy reaches 100%, learning is over*;
- Section 10.3: *data augmentation adds data*.

Each is false in an instructive way. Section 10.1 trains one network on the handwritten digits, with their true labels and with random ones, and finds that it fits both perfectly; what lets it generalise on the true labels is therefore not a limit on its capacity but the structure in the data. Given a mix of true and random labels, the network fits the rows it can generalise from before the rows it can only memorise, which is why early stopping works, and why only a held-out set can say when to stop. Section 10.2 takes a task with an exact rule and no noise, addition modulo 97, and finds the opposite timing: the network fits its training pairs with a memorised table long before, under weight decay, it finds the rule that answers every unseen pair. Section 10.3 compares augmented copies of the digits with real extra digits and finds that the copies do not behave like data at all. They assert that the label survives a transformation: an assumption that pays where the test data vary in that way, costs where they do not, and does harm where the transformation changes the label.

The sections build on each other. All three are about what training accuracy cannot see. Section 10.1 shows that a perfect training score is compatible both with having learned the digits and with having learned nothing; Section 10.2 shows that it is compatible with two quite different functions, between which training can move while the score stays fixed. Together they show that what happens after the training set is fitted depends on the data and on what pushes the network: in Section 10.1 continued training memorised noise and cost accuracy, while in Section 10.2 it found the rule. Section 10.2 also introduces the idea the third section develops. Weight decay encodes a preference that the training data cannot express, and that preference decides which of the many functions fitting the data the network ends with. Section 10.3 reads data augmentation as a preference of the same kind, expressed through the inputs rather than the weights, and asks when it is worth having.

## The Network That Memorises Noise Learns the Signal First

<!-- source: ml-26-random-labels; hero: ml126-hero.png -->


The question of what a trained network has learned begins with an argument every student of statistics meets in some form. A model flexible enough to fit any labelling of its training data has learned nothing by fitting this one; a model that generalises must therefore be constrained, by its size, architecture or regularisation, so that it could not have fitted noise. The sentence under test is that argument's conclusion: *a network that generalises could not have fit noise*. A practitioner leans on it whenever a high training accuracy is read as evidence of learning, or a network is trusted because its capacity looks modest. If it is false, training accuracy says nothing about generalisation, and on data with noisy labels only a held-out set can tell when the network has stopped learning the task.

This section trains one network on the true labels of a digit data set and on entirely random labels, and shows that it fits both. It argues that capacity therefore cannot be what makes it generalise. It then mixes true and random labels and follows training step by step, finding that the network fits the rows it can generalise from before the rows it can only memorise. Finally it draws the consequence for early stopping.

### The capacity argument and the random-label test


The *generalisation gap* is the difference between a model's error on its training data and its error on new data from the same distribution. Classical learning theory bounds the gap in terms of the *capacity* of the model class, a measure of how many different labellings of a data set the class can fit. A class that can fit only a few labellings cannot fit the training data well by accident, so a good fit means it has found real structure. A class that can fit every labelling offers no such guarantee. The bounds therefore promise a small gap only when the model class is too small to fit arbitrary labels.

Zhang, Bengio and colleagues tested that premise for image networks in 2017 by replacing the labels with random ones. The test is cheap to repeat on the UCI handwritten digits, split in half, stratified by class, into 898 training and 899 test images, with the 64 pixel features standardised on the training half. The network has two hidden layers of 512 ReLU units (64-512-512-10), about 300,000 parameters for 898 training images.

It is trained with Adam, the adaptive optimiser of Section 9.3, at learning rate 1e-3 for 400 steps. Training is *full-batch*: each step uses the gradient on all 898 images, so one step is one pass over the data and the order of examples plays no part. Four label sets are used: the true labels; labels in which 20% or 50% of rows were given a class drawn uniformly at random; and labels that are entirely random. The code reports the step at which training accuracy first reaches 100%, and the final test accuracy.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

runs = nets.random_labels()     # 2 x 512 network, full-batch Adam
for r in runs:
    print(f"{r['fraction']:4.0%} of labels randomised: 100% training "
          f"accuracy at step {r['epoch_100']:3d}, test {r['test_acc']:.3f}")
```

```text
  0% of labels randomised: 100% training accuracy at step  41, test 0.968
 20% of labels randomised: 100% training accuracy at step 101, test 0.836
 50% of labels randomised: 100% training accuracy at step  82, test 0.604
100% of labels randomised: 100% training accuracy at step  73, test 0.120
```

### The same network fits true and random labels


On the true labels the network reaches 100% training accuracy in 41 steps and scores 0.968 on held-out digits. On entirely random labels there is nothing to learn, since each label bears no relation to its image. The network reaches 100% there too, in 73 steps, and scores 0.120, chance for ten classes. Fitting pure noise took it less than twice as long as fitting the truth, with no change to architecture, optimiser, learning rate or number of steps.

So the same network, unchanged, can memorise an arbitrary labelling of these 898 images, and by the capacity argument its perfect fit to the true labels should mean nothing. It means a test accuracy of 0.968. The figure below shows the four training curves; the partly random runs are taken up later.

![Random labels take 73 steps to fit perfectly, against 41 for the true ones. Partly random labels take longest.](ml126-f0-train.png){alt="Four rising curves, all reaching 1.0 before step 110: true labels first, then random labels, then 50% and 20% randomised."}

### Why capacity cannot be the explanation


The point Zhang and colleagues made, and this measurement repeats, is that for a network of ordinary size the premise of the classical bounds is false. The model class can fit arbitrary labels, and doing so takes no special effort. A bound that depends only on the model class must allow for the worst case, the random labels, and so cannot promise better than chance. Yet the network generalises well on the true labels.

The two runs differ in one respect only: the labels. Architecture, initial weights, optimiser and schedule are the same. Whatever explains generalisation on the true labels must differ between the runs, and the only candidate is the relation between images and labels. The explanation is a statement about the *data*.

The gradient shows how. On the true labels the images of a class share structure: two images of a 3 have ink in many of the same places. A change to the weights that raises the correct score on one image tends to raise it on the others of that class, so each image's contribution to the gradient pushes in a direction that helps the rest, and the network quickly finds a function that fits many images at once. On random labels no such function exists. Two images of a 3 may carry the labels 5 and 8, and a change that helps one does nothing for the other. The network has to carve out each label individually, which is why it took 73 steps instead of 41. Both end at 100%; the difference is not whether the network can fit the labels but how.

Training accuracy cannot tell the two apart. Only held-out data distinguish a network that has learned the digits from one that has learned a list of image–label pairs.

### With mixed labels, the network fits the signal first


Real data sets usually contain some *label noise*, rows whose recorded label is wrong, so the partly random runs are closest to practice. Here the noise is *uniform*: each affected row receives a class drawn at random, independently of its image.

#### Test accuracy rises, then falls

With 20% or 50% of labels randomised the network still reaches 100% training accuracy, in 101 and 82 steps, longer than either pure case. Its test accuracy, though, does not climb steadily. The next block reports each run's best test accuracy, the step where it occurred, and the final value.

```python
for r in runs:
    step, _, best, *_ = max(r["curve"], key=lambda c: c[2])
    print(f"{r['fraction']:4.0%} randomised: best test {best:.3f} at "
          f"step {step:3d}, final {r['test_acc']:.3f}")
```

```text
  0% randomised: best test 0.970 at step 215, final 0.968
 20% randomised: best test 0.928 at step  20, final 0.836
 50% randomised: best test 0.895 at step  15, final 0.604
100% randomised: best test 0.131 at step   1, final 0.120
```

On clean labels the best and final scores are almost equal, 0.970 and 0.968. The partly random runs behave differently. With a fifth of the labels randomised the network reaches 0.928 at step 20, close to the clean network, and ends at 0.836. With half randomised it reaches 0.895 at step 15 and ends at 0.604. Test accuracy rises quickly to an early peak and falls for the rest of training.

#### Splitting the training rows by whether their label was changed

To see why, the training rows of the 20% run are divided into unchanged rows, which kept their true label, and changed rows, which were given a random one. The block prints training accuracy on each group, against the labels being trained on, and test accuracy, at five steps.

```python

for step, train, test, kept, changed in fifth["curve"]:
    if step in (5, 20, 50, 75, 100):
        print(f"step {step:3d}  unchanged rows {kept:.3f}  "
              f"changed rows {changed:.3f}  test {test:.3f}")
```

```text
step   5  unchanged rows 0.880  changed rows 0.030  test 0.835
step  20  unchanged rows 0.973  changed rows 0.024  test 0.928
step  50  unchanged rows 0.999  changed rows 0.358  test 0.901
step  75  unchanged rows 1.000  changed rows 0.891  test 0.850
step 100  unchanged rows 1.000  changed rows 0.994  test 0.833
```

Training falls into two phases. In the first the network learns the unchanged rows and almost ignores the changed ones: by step 20 it is right on 97.3% of the unchanged rows and on 2.4% of the changed ones. That second figure is *below* chance. The natural reading is that the network is predicting the changed rows' true class rather than the random label it is trained on; the curve records accuracy only against the training labels, so this is an inference, not a measurement. It fits the mechanism above: the 80% of correctly labelled rows would define, for each class, a direction of improvement that the changed rows follow too, because they look like the other members of their true class. The network has learned the digits from the rows that teach them and is overruling the noise.

In the second phase it memorises the changed rows one by one: their training accuracy climbs from 0.358 at step 50 to 0.994 at step 100. Test accuracy falls as it does, plausibly because each memorised random label is a small wrong rule about what that digit looks like. The first figure below shows test accuracy for all four runs; the second splits the 20% run into its two groups.

### Why early stopping works, and what it needs


Arpit and colleagues documented the same ordering on larger networks: real patterns are fitted before noise, and the switch is visible in held-out accuracy or in training accuracy split into clean and noisy examples. It is one practical reason *early stopping* works. Early stopping halts training at the step where performance on a held-out validation set is best, rather than at the end of a fixed schedule. A network stopped at the peak has learned the shared structure and not yet the exceptions.

The measurement also says what early stopping needs. The peak came at step 20 in the 20% run and step 15 in the 50% run, and the drop after it was 0.091 and 0.291. Training accuracy gave no sign of either; it was still climbing through the peak, because memorising the changed rows raises training accuracy while it lowers test accuracy. Only a held-out set could see the peak. With noisy labels, which most real data sets have in some proportion, a validation set is not a formality for reporting; it is the only instrument that can tell when the network has stopped learning the problem and started learning the noise.

The ordering also explains why a network trained on clean labels does not suffer from its capacity. With no noise to memorise, the late phase of training has nothing harmful to fit: the clean run reached 0.968 and stayed there through 400 steps.

Here, what the network did after fitting its training data was harmful. Section 10.2 examines a network that has already reached perfect training accuracy, on clean labels, and goes on learning something useful long afterwards.

## Perfect Training Accuracy Was Not the End of Learning

<!-- source: ml-27-grokking; hero: ml127-hero.png -->


Section 10.1 above trained a network until it fitted every training label, and watched its test accuracy fall as it memorised noise; the lesson there was to stop early. This section tests the belief that makes stopping at a perfect training score feel natural: *once training accuracy reaches 100%, learning is over*. Every training example is classified correctly, so the gradient has nothing left to correct except confidence, and nothing of substance should change. Practitioners act on this whenever they end training, or set an early-stopping rule, at the point where the training set is fitted. If it is false, a run stopped there may discard a model that would have generalised far better given more steps.

This section uses a task with an exact rule and no label noise, addition modulo 97. It first shows a network fitting its training pairs early with no ability to answer unseen ones, and then, thousands of steps later, answering almost all of them. It then shows that weight decay drives the change, by following the size of the network's weights and by switching weight decay off. Finally it varies the amount of training data, and closes with why the result does not license training past 100% in general.

### A task with a rule and nothing else


*Modular addition* takes two whole numbers and returns the remainder of their sum after division by a fixed modulus; with modulus 97, $(a + b) \bmod 97$ is $a + b$ if that is below 97 and $a + b - 97$ otherwise. Every pair $(a, b)$ of numbers from 0 to 96 has a label, 9,409 pairs in all. A small network is trained on a random 3,763 of them, 40%, and asked about the other 60%. Because the labels follow an exact rule, a model that learned the rule would answer every unseen pair correctly, and there is no noise in the training labels to memorise.

The network knows nothing about numbers. Each of $a$ and $b$ is represented by a learned *embedding*, a vector of 128 parameters looked up by index and adjusted during training, so that the number 5 is at first just a row of random values. The two embeddings are concatenated and passed through one hidden layer of 256 ReLU units, and the output is one of 97 classes. Training is full-batch, with every training pair used at every step, for 10,000 steps.

The optimiser is AdamW, a variant of Adam with *decoupled weight decay*. At every step, besides the Adam update driven by the loss, each parameter $\theta$ is shrunk towards zero in proportion to its own value,

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\lambda\,\theta,
$$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate (here 1e-3) and $\lambda$ the weight decay (here 1, or 0 for a control). Weight decay is a steady pressure towards small parameters, which the loss gradient opposes only where the training loss needs larger ones.

The full runs take long enough that their training curves are stored and read back. The first block reads them and reports, for four settings of training fraction and weight decay, when training accuracy first reached 100%, when test accuracy first passed 99%, and the test accuracy after 10,000 steps. The second block reruns the first 200 steps of the first setting from scratch and prints its accuracies beside the stored ones, as a check that the stored curves are what the code produces.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

runs = nets.grokking_runs()   # written by scripts/train_grokking.py
for r in runs:
    print(f"{r['frac']:.0%} of pairs, weight decay {r['weight_decay']:g}: "
          f"100% training at step {r['train_100']}")
    reach = f"at step {r['test_99']}" if r["test_99"] else "not reached"
    print(f"    99% test {reach}; test after 10,000 steps "
          f"{r['final_test']:.3f}")
```

```text
40% of pairs, weight decay 1: 100% training at step 200
    99% test at step 4900; test after 10,000 steps 1.000
40% of pairs, weight decay 0: 100% training at step 200
    99% test not reached; test after 10,000 steps 0.001
30% of pairs, weight decay 1: 100% training at step 200
    99% test not reached; test after 10,000 steps 0.056
60% of pairs, weight decay 1: 100% training at step 200
    99% test at step 1500; test after 10,000 steps 1.000
```

```python
for a, b in zip(short["history"], runs[0]["history"]):
    print(f"step {a['step']}: train {a['train']:.4f} / {b['train']:.4f}, "
          f"test {a['test']:.4f} / {b['test']:.4f}")
```

```text

```

The rerun matches the stored curve to four decimals at both checkpoints. The first setting, 40% of pairs with weight decay 1, is the one to follow. Its training accuracy reaches 100% at step 200. At that moment test accuracy is 0.000: not chance, which would be about 0.010 with 97 classes, but below it. The network has learned a table of the 3,763 answers it was shown and nothing that transfers to any other pair.

![With weight decay, training accuracy reaches 100% at step 200 and test accuracy 99% at step 4,900. Without it, test accuracy is 0.1% at step 10,000.](ml127-f0-curves.png){alt="Two dashed training curves reach 1.0 within a few hundred steps; the solid test curve with weight decay stays near zero for about two thousand steps and then climbs to 1.0; the test curve without weight decay stays low."}

### The network memorises first and generalises later


By the sentence under test, training is over at step 200. It continues anyway, and for a long time nothing visible happens, as the figure above shows. Training accuracy stays at 100% at every checkpoint. Test accuracy stays under 10% until step 2,300, 12 times as long as it took to fit the training set. Then it climbs steeply and passes 99% at step 4,900. By the end of the run the network answers every one of the 5,646 pairs it never saw.

Power and colleagues, who first reported this behaviour on small algorithmic tasks, named it *grokking*: generalisation that arrives long after the training set has been fitted. Nanda and colleagues later examined the internals of a small transformer trained on modular addition (modulo 113 rather than 97) and found what it had learned. Its embeddings place the numbers on circles, and it adds angles, which is modular addition done with trigonometry. Whether this section's network found the same solution was not checked; what is measured is that it found one that answers the unseen pairs.

The important point is that a memorised table and a rule both fit the training pairs perfectly. Only the rule answers the rest. "100% training accuracy" therefore does not identify what the network has learned: two quite different functions score identically on the training set, and training moved the network from the first to the second while its training score never changed. Training accuracy is a measure of fit to the training set, not a description of the function that achieves it.

### Weight decay moves the network from the table to the rule


Once every training pair is classified correctly, the labels have no errors left to correct, only confidence to add; if they were not what moved the network from the table to the rule, something else was. With weight decay, every step shrinks every parameter slightly, and the gradient pushes back only where the training loss needs it. The network's *parameter norm*, the length of the vector of all its parameters, summarises the result. With weight decay it falls steadily, from 112 when the training set is first fitted to 76 when the rule is found, and on to 65 at the end of the run.

The reading Liu, Michaud and Tegmark give, and that this curve fits, is that the memorised table is expensive in weight norm and the rule is cheap. A table must store a separate answer for each of thousands of pairs, which takes many large weights; a rule that works for every pair can be represented compactly. Both fit the training pairs. Weight decay keeps charging for size, so the network drifts, step by step, towards the solution that fits the data with the smallest weights, and that solution is the rule.

The control is to switch weight decay off and change nothing else, which is the second run. It also fits the training set at step 200. Its parameter norm grows instead of shrinking, from 131 at that step to 248, and after 10,000 steps its test accuracy is 0.1%. Without the pressure on size, the memorised table is as good a resting place as any, and nothing moves the network away from it. The figure below shows the two norm curves.

![With weight decay the parameter norm falls from 112 when the training set is fitted to 76 when the rule is found. Without it, the norm ends at 248.](ml127-f1-norm.png){alt="The weight-decay curve falls steadily from about 110 to about 65; the curve without weight decay rises instead."}

### The less data, the longer the rule takes


The last two runs keep weight decay and change the share of pairs in the training set. With 60% of the pairs the rule arrives sooner, test accuracy passing 99% at step 1,500 against 4,900 with 40%. With 30% it does not arrive within 10,000 steps, and test accuracy ends at 0.056.

That is the shape Power and colleagues reported: the delay between memorising and generalising grows quickly as the training fraction shrinks. The account above offers a reason. The fewer pairs there are to fit, the cheaper a table that fits them, and the longer weight decay takes to make the rule the cheaper of the two. The table below collects all four runs.

![Every run fits its training pairs within a few hundred steps. When, and whether, it then finds the rule depends on weight decay and on how many pairs it sees.](ml127-f2-table.png){alt="A four-row table of when each run reaches 100% training accuracy and when its test accuracy passes 10% and 99%."}

Sections 10.1 and 10.2 have changed the labels and the regularisation while leaving the training inputs alone. Section 10.3 changes the inputs themselves, by data augmentation, and asks whether transformed copies of the training data amount to more data.

## Augmentation Adds an Assumption, Not Data

<!-- source: ml-28-augmentation; hero: ml128-hero.png -->


In Section 10.2 above, weight decay encoded a preference that the training data could not express, for small weights, and that preference decided what the network learned. The most common way of encoding a preference about the data themselves is *data augmentation*: enlarging a training set with transformed copies of its examples. Each training image is shifted, rotated, flipped or overlaid with noise, and the copies are trained on under the original's label. The usual description of it is the sentence under test: *data augmentation adds data*. Four copies of every image make five times the training set, for free. Practitioners act on this when they reach for augmentation as a substitute for collecting more examples, or apply a standard list of transformations without asking whether each is appropriate to their data. If the sentence is false, augmentation can cost accuracy where it was expected to add it, and its benefit can lie somewhere the ordinary test set never looks.

This section compares augmented copies with real extra examples on the handwritten digits, scoring both on an ordinary test set and on a test set whose digits have been moved. It then shows what shifted copies teach, measures what happens when a transformation changes the label, and closes by reading augmentation as a prior assumption about the world the model will meet.

### Augmented copies against real extra examples


If augmentation literally added data, augmented copies would do what the same number of new, real examples does. On the digits that can be tested directly, because the data set holds far more real images than a small experiment needs. The experiment takes 150 training digits and trains six networks, each with one hidden layer of 128 units:

- *base*, on the 150 digits alone;
- *real*, on 750 real digits;
- *shift*, on the 150 plus four copies of each moved by up to a pixel;
- *noise*, on the 150 plus four copies with random noise added to the pixels;
- *flip*, on the 150 plus mirrored copies;
- *rot180*, on the 150 plus copies turned through half a revolution.

Every network takes the same number of gradient steps, exactly 300 minibatches of 32 with SGD and momentum, so none wins by training longer. All are scored on 700 held-out digits, and on the same 700 moved by up to a pixel in each direction. The whole comparison is repeated over ten random splits, and the code prints the average accuracies.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

r = nets.augmentation()      # 150 real digits, 4 copies each, 10 splits
for k in ("base", "real", "shift", "noise", "flip", "rot180"):
    print(f"{k:7s} test {r[k]:.3f}   moved test {r['moved'][k]:.3f}")
```

```text
base    test 0.917   moved test 0.367
real    test 0.957   moved test 0.383
shift   test 0.889   moved test 0.687
noise   test 0.918   moved test 0.369
flip    test 0.825   moved test 0.302
rot180  test 0.794   moved test 0.292
```

Read the first column, the ordinary test set. Four times more real digits raised test accuracy by 0.041. Four shifted copies of each image changed it by -0.027: they made the network worse, by more than five standard errors (the standard error is 0.005). Noisy copies changed it by +0.001, which is nothing. Mirrored and half-turned copies made the network substantially worse, by 0.092 and 0.123. On the ordinary test set, none of the augmentations behaved like real data. The figure below shows what the copies look like.

![The shifted and noisy copies keep the label. The mirrored 3 is no longer a 3, the half-turned 6 is a 9, and the mirrored 0 is still a 0.](ml128-f0-grid.png){alt="A five-by-five grid of 8 by 8 pixel digits: the shifted and noisy copies still read as the same digit; the mirrored 3 and the half-turned 6 and 7 read as something else or nothing."}

### Shifted copies teach one fact, which the ordinary test set never asks


The second column changes the picture. On test digits moved by up to a pixel, the network trained on 150 digits scores 0.367. It has hardly seen a digit off centre, because the digits in this data set barely move within their frame. The centre of each image's ink has a standard deviation of 0.30 of a pixel across and 0.39 down, only 0.7% of images have it more than a pixel from the average, and the leftmost column of pixels holds ink in 0.2% of images. A network with one hidden layer on raw pixels treats each pixel as a separate input with its own weights, so a one-pixel move puts strokes on pixels it has learned to ignore and takes them off the pixels it relies on.

More real digits do not fix this: 750 centred digits score 0.383 on the moved test set, a change of +0.016. Shifted copies of 150 digits score 0.687, a gain of 0.320.

So the two kinds of extra data taught different things. The real digits taught more about what digits look like: more writers, more ways of drawing a 4. The shifted copies taught nothing about that. They taught one fact, that a digit moved by a pixel is the same digit, and the ordinary test set never asked about that fact, because nothing in it was moved. It charged for the lesson anyway: 0.027 of accuracy on centred digits. A likely reason is the training budget. Every network took the same 300 steps, and for the shift network four-fifths of what it saw was shifted, so it spent most of its training on positions the test set never uses.

That is what an augmentation is. It is not data in the sense of new observations of the world. It is a statement, made by the person training the model, that the label is *invariant* under some transformation: unchanged when the transformation is applied to the input. The network cannot learn that statement from centred digits, because centred digits contain no evidence about moved ones either way. The augmentation supplies it from outside the data, as an assumption, and the assumption is worth exactly as much as the test data's variation along it. The figure below sets the gains on the two test sets side by side.

![Shifted copies changed accuracy by -0.027 on the test digits and +0.320 on the moved ones; four times more real digits by +0.041 and +0.016.](ml128-f1-bars.png){alt="Paired bars: real data helps on clean test digits and barely on moved ones; shifted copies cost a little on clean digits and add about 30 points on moved ones; mirrored and half-turned copies lose on both."}

### When the transformation changes the label


Mirrors and half-turns are ordinary augmentations for photographs, where a mirrored cat is still a cat. They do not preserve the label for digits. To measure how far they fail, a network trained on clean digits is shown mirrored or half-turned test digits, and the code prints, for each true class, how often it gives an answer other than that class.

```python
f = nets.flip_confusions()
for kind in ("flip", "rot180"):
    print(f"{kind:6s} " + "  ".join(f"{d}:{v:4.0%}"
                                    for d, v in f[kind].items()))
```

```text
flip   0:  3%  1: 36%  2: 80%  3: 99%  4:  9%  5:100%  6:100%  7: 89%  8: 15%  9: 65%
rot180 0:  3%  1: 30%  2: 58%  3:100%  4: 14%  5: 55%  6:100%  7:100%  8: 18%  9:100%
```

A mirrored 3, 5 or 6 is almost never read as itself, and neither is a half-turned 3, 6, 7 or 9: each is read as its own class in at most 1% of cases. To a human eye a half-turned 6 is a 9. Zeros survive both transformations, and eights mostly do. Training on these copies under their original labels therefore tells the network, among other things, that the shape of a 9 is evidence for a 6. That is false, and the network pays for it: -0.092 from mirrors and -0.123 from half-turns, against the network that saw only the 150 originals. On the moved test digits they cost too, -0.065 and -0.075.

Pixel noise is the control. Its copies preserve the label, and it changed nothing: +0.001 on the ordinary test set and +0.002 on the moved one. Neither test set contains noisy pixels, so the invariance it teaches is never asked for, and at a standard deviation of 2 on a 0-to-16 scale it does not disturb the image enough to get in the way. Copies that assert something true but irrelevant are simply wasted. The table below collects the results.

### Augmentation as a prior


Chen, Dobriban and Lee make this reading precise. Suppose the transformations form a *group* $G$, a set closed under composition and inversion, such as all shifts or all rotations, and the true label is invariant under every $g$ in $G$. The *orbit* of an input $x$ is the set of all its transformed versions $gx$. Training on augmented data is then equivalent to replacing the loss on each example by its average over the orbit,

$$
\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \ell\big(f(gx),\, y\big),
$$

for a finite group, where $f$ is the model and $\ell$ the loss. When the label really is invariant, this averaging can only reduce the variance of what is learned, and it introduces no bias. When the label is *not* invariant, the same averaging introduces bias, because it trains the model on a distribution that does not exist: one in which a half-turned 6 is labelled 6.

Both halves were visible here, along with a cost the theory does not count. Shifts are close to an invariance of handwriting, and the shifted copies bought 0.320 where shifts actually occurred. On a fixed budget of training they still cost 0.027 where shifts did not. A true assumption may be free in the limit, but on 300 steps it was not, plausibly because of the budget; giving every network an equal number of epochs was not tried. Mirrors and half-turns break the label for at least three classes, and their cost was large wherever they were scored.

The practical reading is that an augmentation should be chosen the way a *prior* is chosen in Bayesian statistics, as an assumption brought to the data rather than learned from it: by asking what is true of the world the model will see, not what makes the training set bigger. A shift augmentation suits a camera that may not be centred; a colour jitter suits a model that will meet different lighting. Text should not be mirrored, digits should not be turned through half a revolution, and a sequence with a direction should not be reversed in time. The question is never how many copies, but whether the test data will vary in the way the copies do, and whether the label survives that variation.

An augmentation is a guess about how test inputs will differ from training inputs. What happens when the inputs really do move between training and test, and what that movement costs, is measured in Section 15.3.

## In practice

### Reading a training score

- **Do not read training accuracy as evidence of learning.** A network of ordinary size can reach 100% on labels that mean nothing. 100% training accuracy is a statement about fit: it says the network fits the training set, not which function it fits. Only held-out performance separates learning from memorisation, and a rule from a table.
- **Do not argue from small capacity.** If your network can fit random labels, as networks of ordinary size often can, the evidence that it generalises has to come from data it did not see. To find out, shuffle the training labels and retrain with the same settings.

### Deciding when to stop

- **Monitor a validation set at every epoch and keep the best checkpoint.** With noisy labels, validation accuracy can peak early and then fall while training accuracy keeps rising.
- **Report the gap between the peak and the final validation score.** It measures what memorising the noisy rows cost, and what early stopping is buying.
- **Do not stop a run because the training set is fitted.** Base stopping on a validation curve, and when the task plausibly has a simple underlying rule, look at what happens well past the point of fit before deciding.
- **Set early-stopping patience with this in mind.** A validation score flat at its floor for a long stretch is not proof that nothing more will happen; in Section 10.2 it stayed there for over a thousand steps before rising.
- **Track the parameter norm alongside validation accuracy.** In Section 10.2 the norm kept falling under weight decay after the training set was fitted, while the network moved from the table to the rule; a norm that is still changing is a hint that the run may not be finished.

### Choosing augmentations

- **Do not count augmented copies as data.** They add an assumption, not observations. Judge an augmentation by a held-out comparison with and without it, not by the size of the enlarged training set.
- **Build a test set that varies the way you expect deployment to vary.** An augmentation's benefit may be invisible on an ordinary test set and large on a shifted one. Score with and without the augmentation on both.
- **Check that each transformation preserves the label.** Look at a sample of augmented images with their labels. Mirrors, half-turns, crops and time reversals can all change the class of some examples.
- **Watch the training budget.** With a fixed number of steps, augmented copies displace the original examples, and a true but unneeded invariance can cost accuracy on the ordinary test set.
- **Drop augmentations whose invariance the test data never exercise.** They are wasted at best.

## Limits of the evidence

### Random labels (Section 10.1)

- **One small network, one data set.** The ordering of signal before noise is a tendency of gradient descent on data with shared structure, not a theorem. It depends on the learning rate, optimiser and architecture, and can be weakened, for instance by very large learning rates or by training on each example many times in a row.
- **Uniform random labels.** Real label noise is rarely uniform. Annotators confuse a 4 with a 9 more often than with a 0, and structured noise of that kind can look like signal to the network and be fitted early.
- **No regularisation.** No weight decay, dropout or augmentation was used. Each would be expected to slow the memorisation of noise and move or flatten the peak; none was tried here. Zhang and colleagues found that such regularisers did not stop their networks fitting random labels.
- **Full-batch training.** One step per pass over the data keeps the comparison clean. With minibatches the same phases would be expected to appear, measured in epochs rather than steps and with more noise; that was not run here.

### Grokking (Section 10.2)

- **A task with an exact rule.** Modular addition has a compact rule that fits every pair, including the unseen ones. Most real data have no such rule, only a noisy relationship, and Section 10.1's network, trained on real digits with noisy labels, got worse after fitting, not better. Grokking shows the sentence is false; it is not evidence that training past 100% is generally wise.
- **Strong weight decay, full batches.** A weight decay of 1 with AdamW is far stronger than usual, and full-batch training removes the noise of minibatches. Both make the slow drift easy to see. Power and colleagues report a longer delay with weaker decay; without any, here, the rule did not arrive at all.
- **Early stopping would have failed.** A validation set drawn from the unseen pairs would have shown accuracy at 0 and stayed there for over a thousand steps after the training set was fitted. Any reasonable patience setting would have stopped the run long before the rule arrived. The same tool that rescued Section 10.1's network discards this one.
- **One seed per setting.** Each row of the table is one run, and the timings would move with the seed. The pattern, fitting first and generalising much later under weight decay, is the one reported across many seeds in the literature.

### Augmentation (Section 10.3)

- **A small network on centred images.** One hidden layer on raw pixels has no built-in tolerance for position, which is why a one-pixel move costs it so much. A convolutional network with *pooling*, which summarises each small neighbourhood of a feature map by its maximum or mean, builds part of the shift invariance into its architecture and needs the augmentation less. The point about what augmentation supplies is the same; the size of the effect is specific to this model.
- **Equal steps, not equal epochs.** Every network took exactly 300 gradient steps, so each image in the augmented and the larger real sets was seen fewer times. Giving every network the same number of epochs instead would have given the 150-digit network a fifth of the steps, a different comparison.
- **One kind of shift.** The moved test set uses exactly the transformation the shift augmentation uses. Test data that vary in a different way, by rotation of a few degrees or by thickness of stroke, would reward a different augmentation and not this one.
- **Small data.** With 150 training images every source of information shows. With tens of thousands, real data already contain much of the variation, and augmentations matter mostly for the invariances the real data never exhibit.

## Key points

1. A network that generalises can also fit noise. The same network reached 100% training accuracy on true labels in 41 steps (test 0.968) and on random labels in 73 (test 0.120). Capacity therefore does not explain generalisation here; the labels do, because on real data what fits one example of a class fits the rest.
2. Given both signal and noise, the network fitted the signal first. With 20% of labels randomised, by step 20 it was right on 97.3% of unchanged rows and 2.4% of changed ones, and test accuracy peaked at 0.928.
3. Memorising the noise afterwards cost 0.091 of test accuracy with 20% noise and 0.291 with 50%, invisible in training accuracy and visible only on held-out data.
4. 100% training accuracy says the network fits the training set, not how. On modular addition a memorised table and the rule both fitted it perfectly.
5. Learning continued after the training set was fitted. Training accuracy reached 100% at step 200 with test accuracy 0.000, and test accuracy passed 99% at step 4,900.
6. In these runs weight decay drove the switch, the parameter norm falling from 112 to 76 on the way. Without it, in the one control run, test accuracy was 0.1% after 10,000 steps. More training data brought the rule sooner: step 1,500 with 60% of the pairs.
7. Neither "stop at 100%" nor "train past it" is a rule. What happens after the fit depends on whether there is a simpler solution to find and whether anything pushes the network towards it.
8. Augmented copies are not new observations. On the ordinary test digits, four times more real data added 0.041; four shifted copies of each image changed accuracy by -0.027, and noisy copies by +0.001.
9. An augmentation asserts that the label survives a transformation. Shifted copies added 0.320 on test digits moved by a pixel, where four times more centred real digits added 0.016; mirrors and half-turns, which change the label of at least three digit classes, cost 0.092 and 0.123.
10. Choose augmentations by the variation the test data will have and the label will survive, not by how much they enlarge the training set.

## Exercises

1. Take a classifier you trained, shuffle its training labels completely, and retrain with exactly the same settings. Record how long it takes to reach the training accuracy the real run reached. If it gets there, explain why the real run's test score is the only evidence that it did not memorise its data.
2. Flip 10% of the real labels at random, train once more, and plot validation accuracy against epoch. The gap between the peak and the end is what early stopping is buying you.
3. In the 20% run of Section 10.1 the network was right on fewer changed rows than chance would give. Explain why this suggests it was predicting their true class, and why the recorded curve cannot confirm it. Then explain why structured label noise, such as confusing 4 with 9, might be fitted early, and what that would mean for early stopping.
4. Take a small model you train to 100% training accuracy and keep training it for ten times as many steps, with weight decay, recording validation accuracy and the parameter norm every few hundred steps. If validation accuracy rises again after the fit, your early-stopping rule would have thrown that away; if it only falls, your data are more like Section 10.1's than Section 10.2's, and stopping early was right.
5. Explain why test accuracy of 0.000 at step 200 in Section 10.2 is evidence of a memorised table rather than of a partly learned rule.
6. Using the account of weight decay in Section 10.2, explain why removing weight decay should leave the network at the memorised table, and why a smaller training fraction should delay the rule.
7. Take an image or text model you trained with augmentation and build two extra test sets: one where each test example has been transformed by one of your augmentations, and one where it has not. Train once with the augmentation and once without, and score both models on both test sets. If the augmentation helps only on the transformed test set, state what invariance it is buying and whether your real test data need it.
8. Look at a sample of your augmented training images with their labels and count how many you would label differently yourself. Explain, using the orbit-averaging view, what those examples do to the model.
9. Explain why pixel noise preserved the label and still changed nothing, and describe a test set on which it would be expected to help.
