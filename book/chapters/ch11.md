# Probabilities and Calibration

A classifier hands over more than its labels. Most hand over a number between 0 and 1 for every case, and that number is read in two quite different ways. Read as a *ranking*, it says which cases are more likely positive than which others; read as a *probability*, it says how likely each case is, and it can be multiplied by a cost, compared with a business rule, shown to a patient or fed to another model. Accuracy and the AUC measure only the first reading. A model can rank well and still report probabilities that are far too high, far too extreme, or right on average and wrong for a whole group of rows, and none of the usual scores will notice. This chapter opens Part III, which follows a model's numbers past evaluation to the decisions made with them, and it starts with the number those decisions rest on: the predicted probability, and whether it can be trusted.

A practitioner meets the question whenever probabilities leave the model. The class-imbalance fixes taught in most courses change them without saying so; some models, naive Bayes the best known, produce them badly by construction; and the standard repair, recalibration on held-out rows, is usually presented as if it always worked. Each of the three sections tests one sentence that practitioners rely on:

- Section 11.1: *imbalance is a problem in the data, and resampling fixes it.*
- Section 11.2: *naive Bayes works well even though its independence assumption is wrong.*
- Section 11.3: *Platt scaling or isotonic regression fixes a model's probabilities.*

The sections build on each other. Section 11.1 separates the three things a classifier produces, its ranking, its probabilities and its decisions, and uses that separation to show that oversampling, SMOTE, undersampling and class weights leave the ranking no better, move the decisions in a way a threshold could move them directly, and, for logistic regression, inflate the probabilities; a one-line prior correction undoes the damage. There the probabilities were broken from outside, by changing the training data. Section 11.2 shows a model that breaks them from inside: naive Bayes adds up correlated evidence as if it were independent, which leaves the sign and order of its log-odds intact and pushes its probabilities towards 0 and 1. Copying features, all of them or one family at a time, separates the two effects, and Platt scaling of the log-odds repairs the scale without touching the order. Section 11.3 then asks how far such repairs can be trusted in general. It measures how many held-out rows Platt scaling and isotonic regression need before they help, shows that recalibrating a model that was already calibrated makes it worse, and ends with a limit that no amount of data removes: a model calibrated on average and miscalibrated within a group, which no correction of the score can fix.

Together the sections give the chapter's working rule. Evaluate ranking, probabilities and decisions separately; check calibration, overall and within the groups that matter, before correcting it; and when it does need correcting, use the simplest map the calibration set can support. Chapter 12 then turns from the probabilities to the decisions made with them.

## Resampling Moves the Threshold and Breaks a Logistic Model's Probabilities

<!-- source: ml-5-imbalance; hero: ml105-hero.png -->


When the positive class is rare, as with fraud, a disease or a manufacturing defect, a classifier trained on the data as it comes predicts the negative class almost everywhere, and at the default cut of 0.5 it catches few positives. The first fix most courses teach is to balance the classes before training. The sentence behind it is that *imbalance is a problem in the data, and resampling fixes it.* A practitioner meets this sentence in nearly every tutorial on rare-event classification and in many production pipelines. If it is false, the resampling step buys nothing that a different threshold would not, and it may damage the model's probabilities, which other systems then consume as if they were real.

This section separates three things a classifier produces and measures what each correction does to each of them. It starts with logistic regression on a simulated task at 2% prevalence: first the ranking, then the decisions, then the probabilities, including a correction that undoes the damage. It then repeats the comparison with boosted trees, where the picture is different in both directions, and checks the ranking result on the digits.

### Ranking, probabilities and decisions


The usual corrections come in four forms:

- *Oversampling*: duplicating minority-class rows until the classes are balanced.
- *SMOTE* (synthetic minority over-sampling technique): creating new minority rows by interpolating between a minority row and one of its nearest minority-class neighbours.
- *Undersampling*: discarding majority-class rows until the classes are balanced.
- *Class weights*: keeping the data as it is but weighting the minority class up in the training loss.

To see what each changes, it helps to separate three things a classifier produces.

Its **ranking** is the order in which it places cases from most to least likely positive. It is measured by the *ROC AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative; by *average precision*, a summary of the precision–recall curve that weights the region where positives are found; or by precision at a fixed recall. *Recall* is the fraction of true positives that are flagged; *precision* is the fraction of flagged cases that are truly positive.

Its **probabilities** are *calibrated* if a predicted 10% comes true 10% of the time. *Log loss*, the average negative log of the probability assigned to the true class, rewards good ranking and good calibration together, and so penalises probabilities that are systematically too high or too low.

Its **decisions** are which cases get flagged at whatever threshold is applied to its scores.

A simulated task makes all three measurable at once. Each training set has 5,000 rows of eight standard-normal features, with positives at 2%, about 100 positives, differing from negatives in the means of three features and the spread of a fourth. The test set has 200,000 rows at the same prevalence, large enough that its own noise is negligible. The five versions of logistic regression, plain and with each correction, are trained on the same rows in each of twenty replicates.

```python
from standarderror.ml import imbalance as im

lo = im.compare(model="logistic", reps=20)   # 2% positive
for m, v in lo["summary"].items():
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {v['auc']:.4f}  mean prob {v['mean_p']:.3f}  "
          f"log loss {v['log_loss']:.3f}  recall@0.5 {v['recall_half']:.2f}")
```

```text
plain         AUC 0.9252  mean prob 0.020  log loss 0.061  recall@0.5 0.19
oversample    AUC 0.9240  mean prob 0.215  log loss 0.334  recall@0.5 0.83
SMOTE         AUC 0.9240  mean prob 0.192  log loss 0.313  recall@0.5 0.82
undersample   AUC 0.9213  mean prob 0.232  log loss 0.359  recall@0.5 0.84
class weights AUC 0.9240  mean prob 0.216  log loss 0.336  recall@0.5 0.84
```

The first column, the ranking, barely moves. The second and third, the probabilities, move a lot. The last column, recall at the 0.5 cut, is the one resampling is used for, and it moves from 0.19 to 0.82 or more. The following subsections take the three in turn.

### The ranking: no better, slightly worse


Because every version trains on the same rows in each replicate, the differences from the plain model are *paired*, in the sense of Section 1.1: replicate-to-replicate variation common to all versions cancels in the difference, and the standard errors of the differences are small enough to show their sign. The code prints the mean paired difference from the plain model in AUC and in precision at 80% recall, each with its standard error.

```python
for m, d in lo["paired"].items():
    a, p = d["auc"], d["precision_r80"]
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {a['mean']:+.4f} +- {a['se']:.4f}   "
          f"precision at 80% recall {p['mean']:+.4f} +- {p['se']:.4f}")
```

```text
oversample    AUC -0.0012 +- 0.0004   precision at 80% recall -0.0023 +- 0.0007
SMOTE         AUC -0.0012 +- 0.0004   precision at 80% recall -0.0023 +- 0.0008
undersample   AUC -0.0039 +- 0.0006   precision at 80% recall -0.0066 +- 0.0011
class weights AUC -0.0012 +- 0.0005   precision at 80% recall -0.0022 +- 0.0009
```

Oversampling, SMOTE and class weights each cost about 0.0012 of AUC: tiny, and consistently negative. None of them improved the ranking in the measurement, and precision at 80% recall fell slightly too. Undersampling, which discards roughly 98% of the majority rows and so trains on far less information, costs about three times as much.

That is what the algebra predicts for logistic regression. The model's score for a row is its log-odds, a linear function of the features plus an intercept. Duplicating positives, or giving them more weight, tells the fit that positives are more common than they are, and the fit responds mostly by raising the intercept: every row's log-odds goes up by roughly the same amount. A shift applied equally to every row cannot reorder them, so the ranking is unchanged. What little reordering there is comes from the slopes being fitted to a reweighted sample, and here it does not help. The figure below shows the precision–recall curves of the plain and oversampled models.

![The precision-recall curves are the same. The default cut puts the two models at different points, and the plain model reaches the oversampled one's point with a threshold of 0.021 instead of 0.5.](ml105-f0-pr.png){alt="Two precision-recall curves lying on top of each other, with the oversampled model's default operating point far to the right of the plain model's and a square marking the plain model at a lower threshold on the same spot."}

### The decisions: a threshold does the same job


If the ranking did not change, the jump in recall must have come from somewhere else, and it did. Raising every log-odds by the same amount and then cutting at 0.5 is equivalent to leaving the log-odds alone and cutting lower. The direct test is to take the plain model and move its threshold until it flags as many positives as each corrected model does at 0.5, then compare precision at that matched recall.

```python
for m in ("oversample", "SMOTE", "class weights"):
    v = lo["matched"][m]
    print(f"{m:13s} recall {v['recall']:.3f}: precision "
          f"{v['method_precision']:.3f} at 0.5, plain "
          f"{v['plain_precision']:.3f} at {v['plain_threshold']:.3f}")
```

```text
oversample    recall 0.834: precision 0.105 at 0.5, plain 0.106 at 0.021
SMOTE         recall 0.819: precision 0.112 at 0.5, plain 0.113 at 0.023
class weights recall 0.836: precision 0.104 at 0.5, plain 0.105 at 0.021
```

At the same recall the plain model is as precise as every corrected one, or slightly more. The oversampled model's cut at 0.5 corresponds to a plain-model threshold of about 0.021, almost exactly the prevalence.

That value has a meaning. For a calibrated model, decision theory gives the threshold that minimises expected cost: flag a case when its probability exceeds $C_{\mathrm{FP}} / (C_{\mathrm{FP}} + C_{\mathrm{FN}})$, where $C_{\mathrm{FP}}$ is the cost of a false alarm and $C_{\mathrm{FN}}$ the cost of a missed positive. A threshold near the prevalence is what this rule gives when a missed positive is weighted like 49 false alarms. Balancing the classes at 2% prevalence imposes that weighting implicitly.

That is the real content of "fixing" imbalance: choosing a different trade-off between missed positives and false alarms. It is a decision about costs, and a threshold expresses it directly, can be changed after training as costs change, and does not touch the model. Section 12.1 develops the threshold as a cost ratio in full.

### The probabilities: what resampling breaks


For logistic regression, the cost of making that choice by resampling shows up in the probabilities. The plain model's mean prediction is 0.020 against a true rate of 0.02: it is calibrated, as maximum-likelihood logistic regression with an intercept tends to be on average. The oversampled model's mean prediction is 0.215, about 11 times too high, and its log loss is 5.4 times worse. It was trained on a world in which positives are as common as negatives, and it reports probabilities for that world.

Anyone who reads those numbers as probabilities, to rank cases by expected cost, to set a threshold by a business rule, to combine the model with another, or to tell a patient a risk, is reading the wrong world. Van den Goorbergh and colleagues found exactly this in clinical risk models: imbalance corrections improved no discrimination measure and produced strongly overestimated risks.

#### The prior correction

For logistic regression the damage is reversible, because it is mostly an intercept shift. If the model was trained at a positive rate $\pi_{\mathrm{train}}$ and will be used where the true rate is $\pi$, each predicted probability $\hat p$ can be mapped back by multiplying its odds by the ratio of the true prior odds to the training prior odds:

$$
\frac{\tilde p}{1 - \tilde p} = \frac{\hat p}{1 - \hat p} \cdot \frac{\pi / (1 - \pi)}{\pi_{\mathrm{train}} / (1 - \pi_{\mathrm{train}})}.
$$

This is the adjustment for a change in class priors described by Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker. It is a monotone transformation, so it leaves the ranking exactly as it was. The code applies it to the oversampled model.

```python
c = lo["corrected"]
print(f"oversampled, prior-corrected: mean prob {c['mean_p']:.4f}  "
      f"log loss {c['log_loss']:.4f}  AUC {c['auc']:.4f}")
```

```text
oversampled, prior-corrected: mean prob 0.0206  log loss 0.0620  AUC 0.9240
```

Mean prediction and log loss are back to the plain model's, and the AUC is the oversampled model's, unchanged. That is a long way round to arrive where the plain model started. The figure below shows the three models' calibration.

![Oversampled, a logistic model's predicted probability is about ten times the real rate: mean prediction 0.215 against a prevalence of 0.02. One line of algebra puts it back.](ml105-f1-calibration.png){alt="A plain-model line on the diagonal, an oversampled line far below it, and a corrected line back on the diagonal."}

### For boosted trees it is not even neutral


Logistic regression can only shift and tilt a hyperplane, so resampling has little room to change its ranking. A flexible model has more room. The next block runs the same paired comparison with gradient-boosted trees over ten training sets, printing the change in AUC with its standard error and the change in average precision.

```python
bo = im.compare(model="boosted trees", reps=10)
for m, d in bo["paired"].items():
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {d['auc']['mean']:+.4f} +- {d['auc']['se']:.4f}"
          f"   avg precision {d['ap']['mean']:+.4f}")
```

```text
oversample    AUC -0.0093 +- 0.0011   avg precision +0.0006
SMOTE         AUC -0.0133 +- 0.0031   avg precision -0.0457
undersample   AUC -0.0013 +- 0.0030   avg precision -0.0562
class weights AUC -0.0099 +- 0.0012   avg precision -0.0060
```

#### The ranking gets worse

Oversampling, SMOTE and class weights each lower the AUC by about 0.01, well outside their standard errors, and SMOTE loses 0.046 of average precision. Undersampling barely moves the AUC (-0.0013, inside its standard error) but loses 0.056 of average precision, which matters because average precision concentrates on the top of the ranking, where a rare-class problem is decided. Why the trees lose ranking was not measured here. A likely part of it is that a flexible model can fit the duplicated or synthetic positives themselves rather than the region they came from. At its own operating point the SMOTE model reaches 0.221 precision; the plain model with its threshold moved to the same recall reaches 0.288.

#### The probabilities can get better

The probabilities tell a different story from logistic regression's. The plain trees are *under*-confident about positives: their mean prediction is 0.008 against a prevalence of 0.02, and their log loss, 0.102, is worse than the plain logistic model's. Oversampling and class weights move the mean to 0.017 and 0.018, closer to the truth and not past it, and cut the log loss to 0.087 and 0.087. SMOTE and undersampling overshoot, to log losses of 0.124 and 0.495. So "resampling breaks the probabilities" is a statement about a model that starts well calibrated. For one that starts miscalibrated, resampling can move it either way, and the only way to know is to measure.

The same fact explains why the prior correction does not help the trees. It assumes the corrected model's probabilities are inflated by the change in priors, as logistic regression's were. The oversampled trees' probabilities were never inflated: their mean, 0.017, is below the prevalence. Dividing their odds by about 49 therefore pushes them far too low, and the log loss gets worse, not better. Section 11.3 below treats recalibration methods that learn the correction from data instead of assuming it.

#### The digits agree on the ranking

The digits, recast as the question "is this an 8?" at about 10% positive, with logistic regression over 30 splits, agree with the simulation. The plain model has the highest AUC (0.9749) and the highest precision at 80% recall (0.746), against 0.707 oversampled and 0.649 undersampled. The table below collects the results.

![No column on the left improves on the plain row. The right-hand column, the one resampling is used for, is a threshold.](ml105-f2-table.png){alt="A ten-row table: ranking columns that never improve on the plain rows, mean probabilities ten times the prevalence for resampled logistic regression, and recall at 0.5 that rises with resampling."}

In this section a model that started with calibrated probabilities had them broken from outside, by a change to the data it was trained on. The next section turns to a model that miscalibrates its own probabilities, from inside, while its ranking survives.

## Naive Bayes Counts Correlated Evidence Twice, and Only Its Probabilities Notice

<!-- source: ml-43-naive-bayes; hero: ml143-hero.png -->


Section 11.1 above separated a classifier's ranking from its probabilities and found that resampling leaves the first nearly alone while it inflates the second. Naive Bayes separates the two by construction. Section 8.2 combined models by averaging their probabilities and found that the most confident member decides; naive Bayes combines too, but inside a single model: it treats each feature as a separate, independent piece of evidence about the class and adds up what each one says. That is exactly right if the features are independent within each class, and wrong otherwise, and they almost never are. The sentence that keeps the method in every textbook is its defence: *naive Bayes works well even though its independence assumption is wrong.* A practitioner meets it whenever naive Bayes is used as a fast baseline, a text classifier or a component whose scores feed later decisions. If the defence covers less than it seems to, those scores can be wildly overconfident while the accuracy looks respectable.

This section tests the defence on a data set where the assumption is about as wrong as it gets. It first compares naive Bayes with logistic regression on accuracy and on the quality of its probabilities. It then copies every feature several times, which scales the model's evidence without changing its direction, and copies only one group of features, which changes the balance of the evidence. Finally it tries three repairs.

### How naive Bayes combines evidence


For a two-class problem, naive Bayes predicts with the *log-odds*, the logarithm of the ratio of the two classes' posterior probabilities. Under the assumption that the features $x_1, \dots, x_d$ are independent given the class, Bayes' rule gives

$$
\log\frac{P(y=1 \mid x)}{P(y=0 \mid x)} \;=\; \log\frac{P(y=1)}{P(y=0)} \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^{d} \log\frac{p(x_j \mid y=1)}{p(x_j \mid y=0)} .
$$

The first term is the prior; each term of the sum is that feature's *log-likelihood ratio*, its evidence for one class over the other. In *Gaussian* naive Bayes each class-conditional density $p(x_j \mid y)$ is a normal distribution with its own mean and variance, estimated separately for each feature. The predicted probability is the logistic function of the log-odds.

The formula adds each feature's evidence as if no other feature had already reported it. If two features measure the same thing, the same evidence enters twice.

### On correlated features, naive Bayes is accurate but overconfident


The data are the Wisconsin diagnostic breast-cancer set bundled with scikit-learn: 569 tumours and 30 features. Ten measurements of each tumour's nuclei are recorded three times each, as a mean, a standard error and a worst value, and three of the ten, radius, perimeter and area, measure the same thing, size, in different units. The comparison model is logistic regression on standardised features, which fits one weight per feature jointly and so accounts for overlap between them: if two columns carry the same information, the fit can split the weight between them.

The code scores both models by five repetitions of five-fold cross-validation and reports accuracy, log loss (Section 11.1 above) and the share of errors made with confidence above 0.99.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

b = combine.nb_baseline()      # breast cancer, 5 x five-fold CV
for name in ("nb", "logistic"):
    r = b[name]
    print(f"{name:9s} accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}, log loss {r['nll']:.3f}, "
          f"errors made at > 0.99 confidence {r['confident_errors']:.0%}")
```

```text
nb        accuracy 0.940, log loss 0.611, errors made at > 0.99 confidence 67%
logistic  accuracy 0.981, log loss 0.073, errors made at > 0.99 confidence 9%
```

Naive Bayes is four points less accurate, 0.940 against 0.981, which is the kind of gap that keeps it in use as a fast baseline. Its log loss is 8 times larger, and that is a different kind of gap. Log loss punishes confident mistakes severely, and 67% of naive Bayes' mistakes were made with more than 99% confidence, against 9% of logistic regression's.

### Copying every feature scales the evidence


#### Why the ranking survives

Domingos and Pazzani explained in 1997 why naive Bayes classifies well despite the assumption. Classification needs only the sign of the log-odds, and ranking needs only their order; dependence between features distorts the *size* of the log-odds far more than their sign.

The cleanest test of that explanation copies the features. With every one of the thirty features present $k$ times, every log-likelihood ratio in the sum appears $k$ times, so the sum is multiplied by $k$. The prior is added once, whatever $k$ is. The log-odds are therefore multiplied by $k$, apart from the prior term. The block below fits naive Bayes on the copied data for several $k$ and reports accuracy, the AUC (here, the probability that a randomly chosen malignant tumour is scored above a randomly chosen benign one) computed both from the log-odds and from the predicted probabilities, and log loss.

```python
for r in combine.nb_duplicates():   # every feature copied k times
    print(f"k = {r['k']:2d}: accuracy {r['acc']:.4f}, AUC log-odds "
          f"{r['auc_log_odds']:.4f}, AUC probs {r['auc']:.4f}, "
          f"log loss {r['nll']:.3f}")
```

```text
k =  1: accuracy 0.9395, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9876, log loss 0.611
k =  2: accuracy 0.9385, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9830, log loss 0.982
k =  3: accuracy 0.9385, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9807, log loss 1.191
k =  5: accuracy 0.9381, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9734, log loss 1.479
k = 10: accuracy 0.9385, AUC log-odds 0.9876, AUC probs 0.9549, log loss 1.789
```

Multiplying log-odds by a positive number does not change their order, and the AUC computed from the log-odds is 0.9876 at every $k$. It agrees to within 3 millionths, the difference being one near-tie resolved differently, by rounding or by the prior term that does not scale with $k$. Accuracy barely moves, from 0.9395 to 0.9385; the small change again comes from the prior term no longer keeping pace with the rest. On order the textbook defence holds exactly, and on sign up to the prior term.

#### Why the probabilities do not

The probabilities are another matter. Scaling the log-odds by $k$ pushes every probability towards 0 or 1, and log loss climbs with every copy, to 1.789 with ten copies. At that point 80% of the predicted probabilities are exactly 0 or 1 in floating point: once the log-odds reach a few dozen, the probability rounds to exactly 1 in double precision, and sufficiently negative log-odds underflow to exactly 0.

That rounding shows up even in a ranking metric. The AUC computed from the probabilities falls to 0.955, because tumours that differ in log-odds all tie at a probability of 1.0 and can no longer be ordered. The figure below shows the flat accuracy and log-odds AUC beside the falling probability AUC and the climbing share of saturated probabilities.

![At k = 10 the AUC of the log-odds is still 0.9876; the AUC of the probabilities is 0.955, because 80% of them are exactly 0 or 1.](ml143-f0-copies.png){alt="Accuracy and the AUC of the log-odds are flat lines; the AUC of the probabilities drifts down; the dashed share of probabilities that are exactly 0 or 1 climbs from about 0.14 to 0.80."}

So the defence is right about what it claims and silent about the rest. The real breast-cancer features are not ten copies of each other, but the size features come close, and the model counts them as separate witnesses saying the same thing.

### Copying some features reweights the evidence


Copying every feature scales the evidence uniformly. Copying only one group, or *family*, of features changes the balance between families, and that does change the model's verdicts. The next block copies either the size family (the nine features derived from radius, perimeter and area) or the texture family (three features) $k$ times, leaving the rest as they are.

```python
for r in combine.nb_family():       # one family copied k times
    print(f"{r['family']:7s} x{r['k']:<2d}: accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}, "
          f"AUC of log-odds {r['auc_log_odds']:.4f}, "
          f"log loss {r['nll']:.3f}")
```

```text
size    x2 : accuracy 0.933, AUC of log-odds 0.9864, log loss 0.924
size    x5 : accuracy 0.922, AUC of log-odds 0.9803, log loss 1.931
size    x10: accuracy 0.914, AUC of log-odds 0.9746, log loss 2.639
texture x2 : accuracy 0.945, AUC of log-odds 0.9895, log loss 0.549
texture x5 : accuracy 0.950, AUC of log-odds 0.9905, log loss 0.461
texture x10: accuracy 0.936, AUC of log-odds 0.9854, log loss 0.771
```

The nine size features copied ten times push the model towards judging tumours by size alone: the AUC of its log-odds falls to 0.9746, so the ranking itself has changed, and accuracy falls to 0.914. The three texture features copied five times push it the other way, and accuracy *rises*, to 0.950, with lower log loss than the original model (0.461 against 0.611).

That second result is a reminder of what the real data already do. Naive Bayes on the original features is not a neutral model with an independence flaw; it is a model that weights each group of correlated features by how many columns the group happens to occupy. Size occupies nine columns and texture three: each statistic of size is counted three times, once per unit it is measured in, and each statistic of texture once. Upweighting texture helped at two and five copies, which is consistent with the original features overcounting size; at ten copies texture was overcounted in turn, and accuracy fell back to 0.936. The table below collects these runs with the repairs described next.

![Copying everything rescales the log-odds; copying one family reweights it. Only the second changes which tumour ranks above which.](ml143-f1-table.png){alt="A fifteen-row table. Copying all features leaves accuracy and AUC flat while log loss triples; copying the size family lowers both; recalibration brings log loss to 0.149."}

### Three repairs, and one that failed


Three repairs follow from the mechanism.

**Recalibrate the log-odds.** The first repair accepts the order and fixes the scale. A logistic regression is fitted with naive Bayes' log-odds as its single input, using out-of-fold log-odds inside each training set so that the recalibration is learned on predictions the model did not fit; this is Platt scaling (Section 11.3 below). The fitted map is increasing, so the ranking is untouched; accuracy stays at 0.940, while log loss falls from 0.611 to 0.149 and the share of errors made at above 0.99 confidence from 67% to 2%. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana found naive Bayes among the models that gain most from calibration.

**Remove the redundancy.** The second repair keeps only three weakly related features, mean radius, mean texture and mean smoothness. It gives log loss 0.199 with accuracy 0.911: better probabilities, fewer correct answers.

**Decorrelate first.** The third repair looks principled and failed. Decorrelating the features with *whitened principal components*, which rotates the data onto uncorrelated axes and rescales each axis to unit variance, gave accuracy 0.850. Two things may explain it, neither isolated here. Whitening removes the correlation across all tumours, not within each class, so it does not make the features independent given the class, which is the only independence naive Bayes assumes. And it inflates the smallest components, mostly noise, to the same variance as the informative ones.

None of the three beats logistic regression, which solves the double-counting problem by fitting the weights jointly. The case for naive Bayes is speed, tiny data and text, where it remains a strong baseline; the case against it is any use of its probabilities as probabilities.

Here, as in Section 11.1, a correction applied after training put the probabilities right: the prior correction there, Platt scaling of the log-odds here. The next section asks how far such corrections can be trusted in general.

## Recalibration Fixes the Average, Given Enough Rows

<!-- source: ml-34-calibration; hero: ml134-hero.png -->


Sections 11.1 and 11.2 above found two ways for a model's probabilities to go wrong while its ranking holds: inflated by resampling, and pushed towards 0 and 1 by evidence counted twice. In both, a map applied to the model's scores afterwards repaired them. Earlier chapters met other models whose probabilities were too confident: boosting whose test error held steady while its log loss deteriorated (Section 8.1), and dropout's weight scaling, which kept dropout's answers but lost its doubts (Section 9.5). The standard repair is recalibration, and the sentence under test is how it is usually presented: *Platt scaling or isotonic regression fixes a model's probabilities*. A practitioner relies on it whenever probabilities feed a decision, a threshold set from costs, or a risk shown to a person. If it is false, a recalibration step can make good probabilities worse, or leave a whole group of rows with wrong probabilities while the overall check looks perfect.

This section first recalls how calibration is measured and defines the two methods, then measures how many held-out rows each needs before it helps an overconfident model. It then recalibrates a model that is already nearly calibrated, looks at what recalibration does to ranking and to the usual calibration error, and finally shows a limit that no amount of data removes: a model calibrated on average and badly miscalibrated within a group.

### Calibration and two ways to repair it


Recall from Section 11.1 above that a classifier is calibrated if, among the rows to which it assigns probability $q$, a fraction $q$ are positive. Two scores measure departures from it. Log loss, also met in Section 11.1, rewards probabilities that are both well ranked and well calibrated, and needs no binning. The *expected calibration error* (ECE) sorts rows into bins by predicted probability, here ten equal-width bins, and averages the gap between each bin's mean prediction and its observed positive rate, weighting bins by their number of rows.

Both repair methods fit a second, small model on rows the first model did not train on, the *calibration set*, mapping the first model's score to a new probability.

- *Platt scaling* fits a logistic regression to the score's *logit*, $\log\{q/(1-q)\}$. The map is $\sigma(a \cdot \mathrm{logit}(q) + b)$, with $\sigma$ the logistic function: two parameters, and an increasing curve whenever $a$ is positive.
- *Isotonic regression* fits any non-decreasing step function from score to probability, chosen to fit the calibration labels as closely as possible in squared error. It has as many free steps as the data allow.

Both need held-out rows, and the question is how many.

#### How many rows each method needs

The task is the simulated two-class problem of Section 5.1: two Gaussian classes in 10 dimensions. Two models are trained on 2,000 rows: a naive Bayes model and boosted trees. Each is recalibrated on fresh calibration sets of 50 to 5,000 rows, twenty sets at each size, and every version is scored on 20,000 test rows. The code prints each model's uncalibrated log loss and calibration error, then the mean log loss after each method at each size.

```python
from standarderror.ml import around

c = around.calibration_size()        # 20 calibration sets per size
for name, row in c.items():
    print(f"{name}: uncalibrated log loss {row['raw']['nll']:.3f}, "
          f"calibration error {row['raw']['ece']:.3f}")
    for p, i in zip(row["platt"], row["isotonic"]):
        print(f"   {p['n']:5d} rows   Platt {p['nll']:.3f}   "
              f"isotonic {i['nll']:.3f}")
```

### Recalibrating a calibrated model makes it worse


The naive Bayes model here, unlike the one on the breast-cancer data in Section 11.2 above, starts nearly calibrated, with an error of 0.023. Recalibrating it can help only a little, and estimating the correction has a cost. On 50 rows Platt scaling raises its log loss from 0.626 to 0.647; it needs 1000 rows before it improves on doing nothing, and then by 0.0010. Isotonic regression is worse than doing nothing at every size up to 5,000 rows, where it still costs 0.0005.

The reason is the usual one for any fitted model: variance. A correction estimated from 50 labelled rows is noisy, and when the probabilities were already right, any error in the correction moves them away from right; there is no bias to remove that could pay for it. Isotonic regression, with its many free steps, has more variance than Platt scaling's two parameters, which is why Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana recommended Platt scaling for small calibration sets and isotonic regression only when there are thousands of rows. The measurement agrees, and adds a point that is easy to miss: recalibration is not free even when it is unnecessary, so the calibration should be checked before it is corrected.

#### What recalibration does to the ranking

Recalibration also touches the ranking of rows, measured by the AUC. Platt scaling with a positive slope, as here, is a strictly increasing map, so it preserves the order of the scores exactly, and the AUC after it is identical to the uncalibrated AUC at every size. Isotonic regression maps whole ranges of scores to the same value, creating ties among rows that the original model had ordered, and the information in that order is lost. It lowered the AUC at every one of the 6 sizes for both models: for the boosted trees on 50 rows, to 0.7424 from 0.7558. The table below collects the log losses and AUCs.

![Platt scaling left AUC exactly as it was. Isotonic regression lowered it, most on small calibration sets.](ml134-f2-table.png){alt="A fourteen-row table: for boosted trees log loss falls with either method given enough rows; for naive Bayes it rises at 50 rows; AUC is unchanged by Platt and lowered by isotonic."}

### The calibration error tells the same story with larger swings


Log loss is the honest score for probabilities, but the number usually reported is the calibration error. It tells the same story with larger swings. For the boosted trees, calibration error falls from 0.157 uncalibrated to 0.082 after Platt scaling on 50 rows and 0.013 on 5,000; isotonic regression reaches 0.107 and 0.014. For the naive Bayes model the direction reverses at the small end: from 0.023 uncalibrated to 0.079 after Platt scaling on 50 rows, 3.4 times worse, and 0.107 after isotonic regression.

There is a structural reason why the error cannot reach zero on a finite calibration set. The correction is estimated from the calibration rows' labels, which are noisy 0s and 1s. An observed rate estimated from $m$ labels has a standard error proportional to $1/\sqrt{m}$, and each region of scores the map has to fit gets only a share of the $n$ calibration rows, so the fitted map is off by at least roughly $1/\sqrt{n}$ there. Two parameters spread that noise thinly across the whole range; a step function with a step wherever the data allow puts few rows behind each step and so concentrates it. The calibration set therefore has to be large compared with the precision the probabilities are expected to have, and a few hundred rows buy precision of a few hundredths at best.

### Calibrated on average, miscalibrated within a group


The deeper limit has nothing to do with sample size. A calibration curve compares predicted and observed rates across all rows, so calibration in that sense is a property of the average. The next simulation has two groups, holding 70% and 30% of the rows, with different base rates and a shared dependence on one feature. A logistic model is trained on the feature but not told the group. The code prints its calibration error over all rows and, for each group, the true positive rate, the mean prediction and the calibration error within the group.

```python
g = around.subgroup_calibration()
print(f"calibration error, all rows: {g['blind']['all']:.3f}")
for grp in g["groups"]:
    k = grp["group"]
    print(f"group {k} ({grp['share']:.0%} of rows): true rate "
          f"{grp['rate']:.3f}, mean prediction {grp['blind_mean']:.3f}, "
          f"error {g['blind'][k]:.3f}")
```

```text
calibration error, all rows: 0.005
group 0 (70% of rows): true rate 0.399, mean prediction 0.481, error 0.083
group 1 (30% of rows): true rate 0.669, mean prediction 0.478, error 0.191
```

Over all rows the model is calibrated to 0.005. Within the smaller group, 30% of the rows, its error is 0.191: it predicts 0.48 on average for a group whose rate is 0.67. In the larger group it predicts 0.48 for a rate of 0.40. The two errors have opposite signs, and in the overall average they cancel. The mechanism is that a model which cannot see the group can at best predict, for each feature value, the pooled rate among rows with that value, which lies between the two groups' rates. That pooled rate is right on average and wrong for both groups.

No recalibration of the model's score can fix this. The score is the same function of the feature in both groups, so two rows with the same feature value, one from each group, get the same score, and any monotone correction moves both groups' predictions in the same direction. What fixes it is information: the same logistic model given the group is calibrated to 0.005 and 0.007 within the two groups. Hébert-Johnson and colleagues call calibration that holds within every identifiable subgroup *multicalibration*. It is a property of the model and its inputs, not of a correction applied afterwards. The figure below compares the two models overall and within each group.

![Overall calibration error is 0.005; in the smaller group it is 0.191. Chapter 12 puts calibrated probabilities to work: it derives the decision threshold that the costs of the two errors imply, and measures what happens when the probabilities fed to it are not calibrated.

## In practice

### Evaluating a model's probabilities

- **Separate ranking, probabilities and decisions.** When evaluating an imbalance correction, a recalibration or a new model, report AUC or average precision for the ranking, calibration or log loss for the probabilities, and precision and recall at the operating point for the decisions.
- **Check calibration before correcting it.** Compute log loss and a calibration curve on held-out data first. A model that is already close to calibrated is likely to be made worse by recalibration on a small set.
- **Check what consumes the probabilities.** If a resampled or reweighted logistic model, or a naive Bayes model, feeds a dashboard, an expected-cost calculation or another model, compare its mean prediction with the true rate, check its log loss and the share of predictions above 0.99 or below 0.01, and count the errors among them.
- **Check calibration within the groups that matter.** Plot the calibration curve separately by region, product, age band or any other variable that bears on the decision. If a group is off while the overall curve is fine, recalibrating the score will not help; give the model the information that distinguishes the group.

### Class imbalance

- **Move the threshold before resampling.** If the goal is more recall, lower the plain model's threshold until it reaches the recall you need. In Section 11.1 that matched every correction's precision or beat it, without retraining.
- **Choose the threshold from costs.** A threshold near the prevalence corresponds to weighting a miss like many false alarms; make that weighting explicit and set the threshold from it (Chapter 12).
- **Undo the damage if the correction stays.** For a resampled or reweighted logistic model whose probabilities are used, apply the prior correction or retrain on the data as it comes.
- **For flexible models, measure rather than assume.** On boosted trees the corrections lowered the ranking but improved under-confident probabilities. Check calibration before deciding either way.

### Naive Bayes

- **Use it for what the defence covers.** Its sign and order survive correlated features; it is a reasonable fast baseline for classification and ranking.
- **Do not read its probabilities as probabilities.** Recalibrate before using its scores downstream: fit Platt scaling on out-of-fold log-odds, which repairs the scale without changing the ranking.
- **Look for families of redundant columns.** Compute within-class correlations and find groups that measure the same thing. Naive Bayes weights each group by its column count, so the feature set itself sets the weights.
- **Do not expect decorrelation to rescue it.** Whitening across all rows is not independence within classes.

### Recalibration

- **Use Platt scaling on small calibration sets.** In Section 11.3 it helped an overconfident model from 50 rows; isotonic regression hurt at 50 and needed 100. Reserve isotonic regression for calibration sets of thousands of rows.
- **Judge a recalibration by log loss on rows it was not fitted on.** Split the held-out data, fit on one part and score on the other; keep the correction only if log loss improves.
- **Expect isotonic regression to cost some ranking.** If the AUC or the ordering of rows matters, prefer a monotone parametric map, or check the AUC after isotonic regression.

## Limits of the evidence

### Imbalance corrections (Section 11.1)

- **Severe imbalance with tiny counts.** With about 100 positives the plain model is well estimated. With ten, every method is noisy, and some regularisation from reweighting can help a badly overfitted model; the fix there is regularisation or more positives, not balance.
- **Models that ignore the threshold.** Some pipelines take the argmax and have no threshold to move, such as a deep network's softmax inside a larger system. There reweighting is the only lever available, and it is the threshold in disguise; it should still be corrected before the probabilities are used.
- **Losses that are not proper.** A *proper* loss, such as log loss, is one minimised by reporting the true probabilities. The comparison assumes the model is trained on log loss. Trained to maximise accuracy directly, a model on imbalanced data can genuinely learn to ignore the minority class, and reweighting then changes what it learns.
- **One simulated task.** The tree result may depend on how closely the trees can fit individual rows; their regularisation was not varied. The direction, no gain in ranking, held in all three settings measured.

### Naive Bayes (Section 11.2)

- **One data set with heavy redundancy.** Breast cancer is close to the worst case for double-counting. With features that really are close to independent within class, naive Bayes' probabilities are much better, as the naive Bayes model on Section 11.3's simulated task, already close to calibrated, showed.
- **Gaussian naive Bayes only.** The multinomial and Bernoulli versions used for text double-count in the same way, through words that co-occur, and are calibrated no better.
- **Copies are extreme.** Exact duplicates make the scaling exact. Real correlated features double-count partially and unevenly, which is the reweighting case rather than the scaling one.
- **Five-fold cross-validation on 569 tumours.** Differences of a point of accuracy are within noise; the factor of eight in log loss and the share of confident errors are not.

### Recalibration (Section 11.3)

- **One task, two models.** The row counts at which recalibration starts to help depend on how badly calibrated the model is and how noisy the task is. What transfers is the order: Platt scaling before isotonic regression on small sets, and checking before correcting.
- **Calibration sets drawn fresh.** In practice the calibration rows are often a slice of the training data, or the validation set already used for tuning, which adds its own optimism.
- **Expected calibration error is binned.** It used ten equal-width bins; with other binning the numbers move a little. Log loss, which needs no bins, tells the same story.
- **Two groups, known.** Real subgroups are many, overlapping and often unrecorded. Calibration within the groups one can name is a lower bound on the problem.

## Key points

1. A classifier produces a ranking, probabilities and decisions, and they must be evaluated separately. Imbalance corrections change the last two and, at best, leave the first alone.
2. On logistic regression at 2% prevalence, oversampling, SMOTE and class weights changed AUC by about -0.0012 and undersampling by -0.0039. The recall they gain at 0.5 the plain model gets with a threshold near the prevalence (0.021), at the same precision or better. Imbalance is a cost question, and a threshold is where costs belong.
3. Resampling inflated logistic regression's predicted probabilities about 11-fold, and the prior correction undid it. On boosted trees the corrections made the ranking worse, about -0.01 AUC and 0.046 of average precision for SMOTE, while oversampling and class weights improved the plain trees' under-confident probabilities (mean 0.008); check calibration before assuming either way.
4. Naive Bayes counts correlated evidence twice. On breast cancer it was 0.041 less accurate than logistic regression and had 8 times its log loss; 67% of its errors came at above 0.99 confidence.
5. Copying every feature $k$ times multiplies naive Bayes' log-odds by $k$: the AUC of the log-odds was unchanged to four decimal places while log loss rose to 1.789 at $k$ = 10. Copying one family reweights the evidence and changes the answers: accuracy 0.914 with the size features copied ten times, 0.950 with texture copied five.
6. Platt scaling of naive Bayes' log-odds cut log loss from 0.611 to 0.149 with accuracy unchanged. Use naive Bayes for ranking and quick baselines, and remember that its weights are set by how many columns each kind of evidence occupies.
7. Recalibration is a fitted model, with variance. On 50 rows Platt scaling cut an overconfident model's log loss from 0.748 to 0.612; isotonic regression raised it to 0.761. Platt scaling leaves the ranking untouched; isotonic regression lowered the AUC at every size.
8. Recalibrating a model that is already calibrated makes it worse until the calibration set is large: 1000 rows for Platt scaling in Section 11.3, more than 5,000 for isotonic regression.
9. Calibration is an average. A model calibrated to 0.005 overall was off by 0.19 in a group holding 30% of the rows. Check calibration within the groups that matter, and fix a group's miscalibration with information, not with a correction of the score.
