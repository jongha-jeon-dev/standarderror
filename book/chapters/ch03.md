# Leakage

A score is only as good as the wall between the rows that build a model and the rows that grade it. *Leakage* is any route by which information from the grading rows gets through that wall, into the model or into decisions made about it, so that the score partly measures how well the model fits its own test. Practitioners meet it all the time, usually without noticing: a scaler or feature selector fitted before the data are split, a category encoded with the outcome it is meant to predict, the same record on both sides of a split, a holdout checked after every change. Each makes the reported score higher than what the model will deliver on new data. None of them shows in the score itself. A leak is usually found, if it is found at all, when the deployed model does worse than its evaluation promised.

The standard teaching treats leakage as a yes-or-no matter of hygiene: did evaluation rows touch anything fitted? This chapter treats it as a quantity. It measures how large each kind of leak is, explains what sets its size, and follows the leak into places where the usual rules say it cannot be. Each section tests one sentence that practitioners rely on to keep an evaluation clean:

- Section 3.1: *fit every preprocessing step inside the folds.*
- Section 3.2: *fit the target encoding on the training rows only, inside the folds, and it cannot leak.*
- Section 3.3: *a held-out test set gives an unbiased estimate, however often you look at it, as long as you never train on it.*

The sections build on one another. Section 3.1 accepts its rule as correct and asks a different question: when the rule is broken, how much of the reported number is leak? It measures seven violations, from feature selection on coin-flip labels, which turned chance into 0.880 accuracy, to standardising on all rows, which moved nothing. From these it draws a rule for sizing a leak before measuring it: a step leaks to the extent that it gives the model room to fit the test rows. Target encoding is one of the large leaks found there. Section 3.2 takes the standard repair for it, fitting the encoding inside the folds, and shows that the repair protects the test rows but not the model. The training rows leak into their own encoded values, and a model trained on them can end up worse than one that never saw the category. Section 3.3 then removes the fitted step altogether. Nothing is trained on the test set and nothing is fitted to it, yet a test set consulted to make decisions leaks through the decisions themselves. Its honesty is a budget, spent a little with each look.

Read together, the three sections replace the question "did the evaluation rows touch the training?" with a sharper one: how much room did anything, a preprocessing step, an encoder or an analyst, have to fit the noise in the rows used to report the score? Chapter 4 applies the same question to the search over models and settings that precedes almost every reported result.

## Not Every Leak Leaks, and the Ones That Do Can Be Measured

<!-- source: ml-3-leakage; hero: ml103-hero.png -->


Section 2.1 established what cross-validation estimates: the average error of a training procedure. This section is about the most common way to make it estimate something else. Every course teaches the rule: *fit every preprocessing step inside the folds.* Anything fitted to data, whether a scaler, an imputer, a feature selector or an encoder, must be fitted on each training fold and only applied to the held-out fold, never fitted on all the rows first. A violation is called *leakage*: information from the rows used for evaluation reaches the model through the preprocessing, and the evaluation becomes optimistic. Kapoor and Narayanan found leakage in hundreds of published papers across seventeen fields.

The rule is right. What the usual teaching leaves out is that violations differ in size by orders of magnitude, and that the size can be reasoned about from what the step does. That matters in practice, because the expensive question is rarely "did we leak?" but "how much of the reported number is leak?"

This section measures seven violations, each against its honest version on the same folds. It starts with the best-known case, feature selection on pure noise, and shows that the leak grows with the number of candidates. It then measures six more violations on real data, which range from nothing to several points. It ends with a rule for sizing a leak before measuring it.

### Feature selection on noise: the classic leak


The best-known leak needs no real data at all. Each simulated dataset has 100 rows, 5,000 features of standard-normal noise, and labels from a fair coin, so no model can do better than 0.5 accuracy on new data. A common pipeline picks the 20 features most associated with the label and then cross-validates logistic regression on them. The code runs that pipeline on 50 such datasets in two ways: selecting on all rows before cross-validating (leaky), and selecting inside each training fold (honest).

```python
from standarderror.ml import leakage as lk

# 100 rows, 5,000 noise features, coin-flip labels, 50 datasets.
r = lk.noise_selection(n=100, p=5000, k=20, reps=50)
print(f"select on all rows, then CV:  {r['leaky'].mean():.3f}"
      f"   (lowest {r['leaky'].min():.2f})")
print(f"select inside each fold:      {r['honest'].mean():.3f}"
      f"   (highest {r['honest'].max():.2f})")
```

```text
select on all rows, then CV:  0.880   (lowest 0.80)
select inside each fold:      0.525   (highest 0.63)
```

Selected on all rows, coin flips cross-validate at 0.880, and none of the fifty datasets came in below 0.80. Selected inside each fold, the estimate is 0.525, which is what labels from a coin deserve. This is the result Ambroise and McLachlan reported for microarray studies in 2002, reproduced in a few lines.

### The size of a selection leak is the room to choose


The mechanism is worth spelling out. The selection step computes, for each of the 5,000 features, how strongly it is associated with the label, and it does so using all 100 labels, including the ones each fold will later hold out. Each noise feature's association with the labels is a small random number, positive or negative. Among 5,000 such random numbers, the largest 20 are far from zero by chance alone. The selected columns therefore line up with the labels, including the held-out labels, by construction. Cross-validation then faithfully measures how well those columns predict labels they were chosen to predict. When selection happens inside each fold, the held-out labels play no part in the choice, and the selected columns are just noise to them.

That argument implies that the size of the leak is set by how much choice the step has: the more candidates, the more extreme the best chance alignments. The experiment confirms this by sweeping the number of noise candidates from which the 20 are chosen.

The leak is exactly zero with 20 candidates, because there is nothing to choose, and climbs steadily: 0.63 with 50 candidates, 0.74 with 200, 0.84 with 1,000 and 0.88 with 5,000. The honest line never moves off 0.5. The figure below shows both lines.

![With 20 candidates there is nothing to choose and nothing leaks. With 5,000 the leaky estimate is 0.88 for labels that are coin flips.](ml103-f0-sweep.png){alt="A rising line from 0.5 to near 0.9 as the number of noise candidates grows, and a flat line at 0.5."}

### Six violations on real data


On the Wisconsin diagnostic breast cancer data, 569 rows and 30 real features, six more violations are measured, each scored against its honest version on the same 20 fold assignments, so that the difference is attributable to the leak alone. The six are:

- *Standardising* on all rows: subtracting each feature's mean and dividing by its standard deviation, with both estimated from every row.
- *Mean imputation* on all rows: filling missing values with each feature's mean over every row.
- *Target encoding* a noise ID on all rows. Target encoding replaces each level of a categorical column by the mean label of the rows at that level. Here the column is 200 random categories with no relation to the label.
- Selecting 5 of 1,030 features on all rows: the 30 real features plus 1,000 columns of pure noise.
- Duplicating every row and splitting at random, for logistic regression and for 1-NN, the nearest-neighbour classifier that predicts each row's label from the single closest training row.

The code prints the honest and leaky accuracies and the gap in points.

```python
for row in lk.leak_table(reps=20):
    print(f"{row['leak']:40s} honest {row['honest']:.4f}  "
          f"leaky {row['leaky']:.4f}  {row['gap'] * 100:+.2f}")
```

```text
standardise on all rows                  honest 0.9790  leaky 0.9795  +0.05
impute means on all rows                 honest 0.9664  leaky 0.9662  -0.02
target-encode a noise ID on all rows     honest 0.9616  leaky 0.9859  +2.43
select 5 of 1,030 features on all rows   honest 0.9454  leaky 0.9454  +0.00
duplicate rows across folds (logistic)   honest 0.9768  leaky 0.9858  +0.90
duplicate rows across folds (1-NN)       honest 0.9522  leaky 0.9895  +3.74
```

#### Steps that estimate a mean or scale leak nothing measurable

Standardising and imputing on all rows leak +0.05 and -0.02 points: nothing measurable. Neither step looks at the label. Each estimates a handful of summary statistics per feature, and a mean or standard deviation estimated from 569 rows instead of 455 differs by too little to move a decision boundary. These are the violations easiest to spot by reading the code, and here they were the least consequential.

#### Label-informed selection leaks only where there is room to fit chance

Selecting features on all rows leaked nothing either, +0.00 points, even though the selection looked at every label and chose 5 of 1,030 columns, 1,000 of them pure noise. The real features are so much stronger than any noise column's chance alignment that the selection picks them whether or not it sees the held-out labels. The noise experiment leaked because there was nothing real to find, and every choice was a chance alignment. Using the label is therefore necessary for this kind of leak, but not sufficient. What leaks is the room to fit chance.

#### Target encoding hands the model the held-out labels

Target-encoding a noise ID leaks +2.4 points. The column carries no information about the label. But when the encoding is computed on all rows, each category's mean label includes the held-out rows' own labels. With few rows per category, a row's own label is a substantial part of the mean that replaces its category, so the encoded value partly *is* the label, and the model learns to read it back. Fitted inside the folds, the held-out rows' labels never enter the means, and the column is worthless, as it should be. Section 3.2 below shows that even the honest version can leak, into the training rows rather than the test rows.

#### Duplicates leak without looking at the label

Duplicated rows leak +0.9 points for logistic regression and +3.7 for 1-NN, although no step looked at the label at all. If every row is copied once and the rows are split into folds at random, most copies land in a different fold from their twin, so most held-out rows have an exact copy in the training data.

How much that helps depends on how local the model is. Logistic regression fits a smooth boundary from all training rows, and one extra row near a test point moves that boundary only a little: it gains slightly from having seen its test row. A nearest-neighbour model, by contrast, *is* a lookup: if the test row's twin is in training, its nearest neighbour is at distance zero and carries the right label. The leaky 1-NN score is almost exactly what that predicts. With about 80% of twins in another fold, and the rest scored as the honest model would score them,

$$
80\% \times 1 + 20\% \times 0.952 = 0.990,
$$

against 0.990 measured. The figure below sets the six real-data leaks side by side.

![Neither colour predicts the size. Looking at the label is necessary for a selection leak and not sufficient; duplicates leak without looking at it at all.](ml103-f1-leaks.png){alt="Horizontal bars: three at or near zero for standardising, imputing and feature selection, and three clearly positive for target encoding and the two duplicated-row cases."}

### A rule for sizing a leak


The measurements support one rule of thumb, sharper than "fit everything inside the folds": *a step leaks to the extent that it gives the model room to fit the test rows.* That room comes from two places.

The first is choice informed by the labels: feature selection, target encoding, hyperparameter tuning. Its size is set by how many options the step has relative to how much real signal there is to find. With plenty of real signal, the choice is made the same way with or without the held-out labels, and nothing leaks. With pure noise, every candidate is a chance to fit them, and the leak grows with the number of candidates.

The second is the test row itself, or a near-copy of it, being present in training. Its size is set by how local the model is: logistic regression barely notices a duplicate; 1-NN returns it.

Steps that do neither, such as estimating a mean, a scale or a missing-value fill from a few hundred rows, do not leak in any amount worth measuring. Fit them inside the folds anyway, because it costs nothing. But when auditing a result, start with the steps that can choose and with any way a row can appear on both sides of a split. The table below collects all seven measurements.

![The ranking is the lesson: a leak is the room a step has to fit the test rows, not whether it touched them.](ml103-f2-table.png){alt="A seven-row table of leaky and honest cross-validated accuracies; the first row's leak is about 35 points, the rest between zero and four."}

Target encoding, one of the large leaks in the table, has a standard repair: fit the encoding inside the folds, so that the held-out labels never enter it. Section 3.2 asks whether that repair is enough.

## Target Encoding Fitted on the Training Rows Still Leaks, Into Training

<!-- source: ml-40-target-encoding; hero: ml140-hero.png -->


Of the leaks measured in Section 3.1 above, target encoding is the one practitioners are least willing to give up, because the feature it builds from the outcome is often genuinely useful. A category with many levels, such as a postcode, a merchant, a product or a user, is called *high-cardinality*, and it is awkward for most models. *One-hot encoding*, one binary column per level, makes thousands of sparse columns; trees given integer level codes split on an arbitrary order. Target encoding, as Section 3.1 defined it, replaces each level with the mean outcome of the rows at that level, giving one numeric column that carries what the category says about the target. Section 3.1 measured the obvious mistake, computing those means on all rows including the test fold, and found that a 200-level noise column encoded that way inflated the score. The standard advice that follows is the sentence under test: *fit the target encoding on the training rows only, inside the folds, and it cannot leak.* If the sentence is false, a pipeline that follows the advice to the letter can still produce a worse model than one that ignored the category, and its honest validation score will say only that the model is bad, not why.

This section first shows that a per-level mean of a pure-noise category, fitted on training rows only, damages the model badly once levels are rare. It then locates the leak: not into the test set, but into the training rows' own encoded values. It compares two repairs, shrinkage and cross-fitting, on noise and on a category that carries real signal.

### A noise category, encoded honestly


The test is designed so that the encoding can never touch the evaluation data. Each simulated data set has five standard-normal features carrying the real signal, through log-odds $x_1 + 0.7\,x_2 - 0.5\,x_3$, and a category of $K$ equally likely levels, with $K$ from 10 to 4,000. The encoding is fitted on 4,000 training rows and the model is scored on 20,000 fresh rows. Results are averaged over five data sets. In the first experiment the category carries nothing: every level has the same effect, zero.

Four ways of handling the category are compared:

- **Dropped.** The category is left out; this is the baseline any encoding must beat.
- **Mean per level.** Each level is replaced by the mean label of its training rows.
- **Shrunk.** Each level's mean is pulled towards the overall mean $\bar y$ as though it had $m$ extra rows at the overall rate. For a level $\ell$ with $n_\ell$ training rows and mean $\bar y_\ell$,
$$
\tilde y_\ell = \frac{n_\ell\,\bar y_\ell + m\,\bar y}{n_\ell + m},
$$
here with $m = 20$. Common levels keep nearly their own mean; rare ones are pulled most of the way to $\bar y$.
- **Cross-fitted.** Described below; scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder` with five shuffled folds and its default shrinkage.

Models are compared by test *AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen positive row is ranked above a randomly chosen negative one, and by *log loss*, the average negative log probability assigned to the true label, which punishes confident errors heavily. The first block shows boosted trees.

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

t = rows.target_encoding()     # 4,000 training rows, 5 data sets
for r in t:
    if r["effect"] == 0 and r["model"] == "boosted trees":
        print(f"{r['levels']:5d} levels, {r['encoding']:15s} "
              f"AUC {r['auc']:.3f}, log loss {r['nll']:.3f}")
```

```text
   10 levels, dropped         AUC 0.764, log loss 0.593
   10 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
   10 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
   10 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.764, log loss 0.593
  100 levels, dropped         AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
  100 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.756, log loss 0.606
  100 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.756, log loss 0.607
  100 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
 1000 levels, dropped         AUC 0.763, log loss 0.594
 1000 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.640, log loss 1.306
 1000 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.689, log loss 0.779
 1000 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.763, log loss 0.594
 4000 levels, dropped         AUC 0.763, log loss 0.594
 4000 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.580, log loss 2.449
 4000 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.586, log loss 1.898
 4000 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.764, log loss 0.593
```

With 10 levels, 400 training rows each, encoding the category changes nothing. With 1,000 levels, four training rows per level, boosted trees given the per-level mean score a test AUC of 0.640, against 0.763 without the column, and their log loss doubles, to 1.306. With 4,000 levels they reach 0.580. Logistic regression falls too, from 0.791 to 0.690 at 1,000 levels. Nothing leaked into the test set; the encoding never saw a test label. The models are genuinely worse. The figure below shows the four encodings across the number of levels for both models.

![With 1,000 levels the per-level mean took boosted trees from 0.763 to 0.640; cross-fitting left them at 0.763.](ml140-f0-noise.png){alt="Lines for the dropped and cross-fitted encodings stay flat; lines for the per-level mean fall steeply beyond 100 levels; the shrunk encoding falls steeply for the trees and far less for logistic regression."}

### The leak is into the training rows


A level with four training rows has a mean label of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 or 1, and each of those four rows contributed its own label to that mean. For a training row, the encoded value is therefore partly its own answer: a quarter of it is that row's label. The next block measures how much this matters by scoring the encoded noise column on its own, with no model, against the labels of the training rows it was computed from and against fresh rows.

```python
c = rows.encoded_column_auc()  # the noise column alone, 1,000 levels
print(f"mean per level: AUC {c['mean_train']:.3f} on its own "
      f"training rows, {c['mean_test']:.3f} on fresh rows")
print(f"cross-fitted:   AUC {c['cross_train']:.3f} on its own "
      f"training rows, {c['cross_test']:.3f} on fresh rows")
```

```text
mean per level: AUC 0.774 on its own training rows, 0.498 on fresh rows
cross-fitted:   AUC 0.485 on its own training rows, 0.499 on fresh rows
```

On the rows it was computed from, the encoded noise column ranks the labels with an AUC of 0.774, better than many real features. On fresh rows it scores 0.498, the AUC of a coin. The model is trained on rows where this column is a strong predictor. It learns to rely on it at the expense of the five real features, and then meets test rows where the column is noise. That is overfitting, done by the preprocessing instead of the model. Cross-validation with the encoding fitted inside the folds measures the damage correctly, as a bad model, but does nothing to prevent it.

The mechanism is Section 3.1's leak moved one step. There, the encoding saw the test rows' labels; here, it sees the training rows' own labels, and the model trained on them cannot tell an honest feature from a recording of the answer. The table below collects all the results, with and without a real effect of the category.

![None of these is a leak in the usual sense: the test rows never touched the encoding. The damage is done to the training rows.](ml140-f2-table.png){alt="A twenty-four-row table. Without a category effect, the per-level mean is far below 'dropped' from 1,000 levels on; cross-fitted is within 0.002 of 'dropped'. With an effect, cross-fitted is best or close to best at every size."}

### Two repairs: shrinkage and cross-fitting


#### Shrinkage helps a linear model, not trees

Shrinkage pulls each level's mean towards the overall mean in proportion to how few rows it has. It helps logistic regression a great deal, to 0.767 at 4,000 levels. The likely reason is that shrinkage pulls the extreme values of rare levels, 0 and 1 for a level seen once, towards the middle, so the encoded column has a small spread, and logistic regression's default L2 penalty, which charges for large coefficients, then keeps the model from scaling the column back up.

Shrinkage does not help the trees (0.586 at 4,000 levels). Among levels with the same number of rows, the shrunk value is a fixed increasing function of the level's raw mean, so it keeps the levels in the same order, still ranked by the training rows' own labels. Trees split on the order of a feature's values, not on their scale, so a monotone squeeze leaves every split available to them.

#### Cross-fitting removes a row's own label from its value

*Cross-fitting*, also called out-of-fold encoding, is what scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder` and CatBoost's ordered target statistics both do, in different ways. The training rows are split into folds, and each training row is encoded by level means computed from the other folds only, so its own label never enters its own value. Test rows get means computed from all training rows. The encoded value of a training row is then an honest prediction for that row, made without its label, as the encoded value of a test row is.

On the noise category, cross-fitting matched dropping the column at every number of levels, to within 0.002 of AUC for both models. The encoded column's AUC on its own training rows fell to 0.485, slightly below a coin. A leave-out mean is pulled away from the row it leaves out: a positive row is excluded from its own level's mean, which is therefore a little lower than it would be for the level's negative rows.

### When the category carries signal


Dropping the column is safe only when the category is noise, which is not why anyone encodes it. In the second experiment each level shifts the log-odds by an amount drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 1. With 10 or 100 levels, every encoding works and gains four to six points of AUC for logistic regression over dropping the column. With 1,000 levels the per-level mean turns the signal into a loss, 0.724 against 0.760 for dropping it, while cross-fitting keeps a gain, 0.778. At 4,000 levels the gain is small (0.765 against 0.759), because one row per level says little about any level. Even so, cross-fitting is the only encoding that is not worse than dropping the column for either model. The figure below shows the results for logistic regression.

![At 1,000 levels cross-fitting kept part of the signal (logistic AUC 0.778 against 0.760 with the column dropped); the per-level mean turned it into a loss (0.724).](ml140-f1-signal.png){alt="All encodings beat dropping the column at 10 and 100 levels; at 1,000 and 4,000 levels the per-level mean falls below dropping it, and cross-fitting stays above."}

Pargent and colleagues compared encodings on real high-cardinality data sets and reached a compatible conclusion: regularised target encoding, fitted with an internal cross-validation, was the most reliable choice across the learners they tried.

Both leaks so far run through a fitted step: a selector or an encoder computed from labels it should not have seen. Section 3.3 removes the fitted step altogether. Nothing is trained on the evaluation rows and nothing is fitted to them, and the leak runs instead through the decisions an analyst makes after looking at their score.

## A Test Set Answers Honestly Only the First Time

<!-- source: ml-45-holdout; hero: ml145-hero.png -->


Sections 3.1 and 3.2 above traced leaks through fitted steps, and each had a repair that kept the evaluation rows out of the fitting. This section asks whether keeping them out is enough. Section 1.1 put an error bar on a test score, and Section 2.2 asked which tests can tell two models apart; Section 4.1, in the next chapter, finds that the best of many cross-validated scores reports the luck of the search as well as the quality of the model. Each assumes that the test set is used once. In practice it rarely is: a model is scored, adjusted and scored again, and a competition's public leaderboard is consulted thousands of times. The reassurance that makes this feel safe is the sentence under test: *a held-out test set gives an unbiased estimate, however often you look at it, as long as you never train on it.* If it is false, the score a team reports at the end of a project can be well above what the model will do on new data, even though no test row ever entered a training set.

This section wears out a holdout in three ways, none of which trains on it. The first is a deliberate attack on a leaderboard of coin-flip labels. The second is an analysis that looks careful: keeping only features that replicate on the holdout. The third is the most ordinary of all, an analyst choosing features by their holdout score. The section then states the common mechanism, adaptivity, and the two kinds of remedy.

### A leaderboard can be climbed without any signal


#### The attack

The cleanest refutation of the sentence is Blum and Hardt's, from 2015. A competition leaderboard holds 1,000 *public* labels, whose score is shown after each submission, and 1,000 *private* labels, used only for the final ranking. Every label is a coin flip, so no submission can genuinely score above 0.5.

The attacker submits random guesses of 0 or 1 for every row, notes which guesses scored above 0.5 on the public labels, and finally submits the *majority vote* of those guesses: for each row, the label that most of the kept guesses assigned to it. The code runs the attack on 50 simulated leaderboards for increasing numbers of submissions and prints the vote's average public and private scores.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

for r in combine.leaderboard_attack():   # 50 leaderboards
    print(f"{r['queries']:5d} submissions: public {r['public']:.3f}, "
          f"private {r['private']:.3f}")
```

```text
    1 submissions: public 0.503, private 0.499
   10 submissions: public 0.524, private 0.500
  100 submissions: public 0.568, private 0.500
  300 submissions: public 0.624, private 0.499
 1000 submissions: public 0.713, private 0.497
 3000 submissions: public 0.839, private 0.500
```

After 1,000 submissions the vote scores 0.713 on the public labels and 0.497 on the private ones. Nothing was trained on anything.

#### Why the vote climbs

Each submission asked the public labels one question, "is this guess better than chance?", and each answer leaked a little about them. A random guess that happens to score above 0.5 on the public labels agrees with them on slightly more than half the rows, so on each row it is slightly more likely than chance to carry the right public label. One such guess carries almost no information, but the majority vote of many of them adds these small tilts row by row, and the vote's agreement with the public labels grows as more guesses are collected. On the private labels the guesses were never selected, so their tilts point nowhere and the vote stays at chance. The figure below shows the two scores diverging.

![After 1,000 submissions the vote scored 0.713 on the public labels and 0.497 on the private ones.](ml145-f0-leaderboard.png){alt="The public score rises from 0.50 to 0.84 as submissions grow from 1 to 3,000; the private score stays at 0.50."}

### A careful-looking analysis leaks the same way


Nobody attacks their own holdout on purpose. Dwork and colleagues showed in 2015 that an ordinary-looking analysis does the same by accident. Their example, scaled down here, has 2,000 features of pure noise and coin-flip labels, with 1,000 rows each for training, holdout and fresh data. The analysis keeps the features whose correlation with the label is noticeably positive or negative on the training set *and* has the same sign on the holdout. That sounds like a careful way of keeping only features that replicate. It then classifies by a vote of the strongest kept features.

Asked for 100 such features (about 99 pass the filter on average), the classifier scores 0.687 on the holdout. On fresh data it scores 0.498, chance.

The holdout was never trained on. It was used to decide which features to keep, and the features it kept are the ones whose noise happens to agree with the holdout's particular noise. The holdout score is now a measurement of how well the model fits the holdout, which is exactly what the features were chosen for. The table below shows the holdout score rising as more selected features vote, while the fresh score stays at chance.

![The holdout was never trained on. It was asked which features to keep, and its answer is now part of the model.](ml145-f2-noise.png){alt="A four-row table: holdout accuracy rises from 0.56 to 0.69 as more selected features vote; fresh accuracy stays at 0.50."}

### An analyst who selects by holdout score


#### Two analysts, one data set

The third case is the most ordinary. The data set has 100 standard-normal features, ten of them weakly informative, with 300 rows to train on and 300 held out; a further 20,000 fresh rows measure the truth. The analyst builds a logistic regression by *forward selection*: starting from no features, add at each step the one feature that most improves the model's score, and repeat. This analyst measures improvement by accuracy on the holdout, a natural thing to do with a holdout one has been told only not to train on. A second analyst runs the same procedure but chooses each step by five-fold cross-validation on the training rows, and leaves the holdout alone until the end.

The code prints, after 1, 5, 10 and 20 features, each analyst's holdout accuracy and fresh accuracy, and the share of the 20 chosen features that are real.

```python
h = combine.greedy_holdout(by="holdout")
c = combine.greedy_holdout(by="cv")
print("features | holdout-guided: holdout / fresh | CV-guided: same")
for step in (0, 4, 9, 19):
    print(f"{step + 1:8d} | {h['holdout'][step]:.3f} / "
          f"{h['fresh'][step]:.3f}{'':18s} | {c['holdout'][step]:.3f}"
          f" / {c['fresh'][step]:.3f}")
print(f"real features among 20 chosen: holdout-guided "
      f"{h['real_share'][19]:.0%}, CV-guided {c['real_share'][19]:.0%}")
```

#### The holdout-guided score beats the true model

The first analyst's holdout score rises at almost every step, to 0.690 with 20 features. That is above 0.676, the fresh-data accuracy of a model given exactly the ten real features, which is the best this procedure could honestly reach. On fresh rows the same models peaked at 0.579 after 5 features and ended at 0.576, and only 14% of the 20 chosen features were real. The holdout overstated the final model by 0.114. Each step chose among a hundred candidates by their holdout score, so each step was a small search over the holdout's noise, and the reported score is the search's best result rather than a fresh measurement.

#### The cross-validation-guided score stays honest

The second analyst's holdout, consulted only to report the result, said 0.579 for a model that scored 0.580 on fresh rows, within 0.001. Cross-validation inside the training rows is subject to the luck of the search that Section 4.1 measures, and this selection picked noise too: only 16% of its 20 features were real. But its luck was spent on the training rows, not on the set used to report the score, so the report stayed honest about the model the analyst got. The figure below shows both analysts' curves.

### A test set's honesty is a budget spent by adaptivity


The common thread is that a test set's honesty is a budget, and it is spent by *adaptivity*: any decision made after looking at a test score, whether to keep a feature, a model, a hyperparameter or a submission, makes later scores on the same set depend on its particular noise. A score is unbiased only for a model fixed before the score was seen. Training on the test set spends the budget at once. Looking at it spends a little per look, and the amount depends on how much each look changes what happens next.

Two kinds of remedy follow.

**The procedural remedy** is the oldest: keep a final test set that is used once, after all decisions are made, and accept that its score, with the error bar of Section 1.1, is the report. Everything consulted before it, including any "validation" or "public" set, is part of training in the sense that matters.

**The algorithmic remedy**, from the same authors, limits what each look reveals. Blum and Hardt's *Ladder* reports a new leaderboard score only when a submission beats the previous best by a margin, so most submissions learn nothing new. Dwork and colleagues' *Thresholdout* answers each query with the training score unless the holdout score disagrees with it by more than a noisy threshold, so the holdout is consulted only when it has something to correct. Both slow the leak, at the price of less precise answers.

Benchmarks raise the same question at the scale of a field. Recht and colleagues rebuilt ImageNet's test set following the original procedure and found that published models lost 11 to 14 points of accuracy on it while keeping their order. They attributed the loss mainly to the new images being harder, rather than to years of adaptive reuse of the old test set. With large test sets and many genuinely different models, the leak can be slow; the three cases in this section show where it is fast.

The analyst in the third case searched over features using the holdout. Chapter 4 takes up the search itself, starting with the most common one, a hyperparameter search, where the luck is spent on cross-validation folds rather than on a test set.

## In practice

### Measuring and sizing a leak

- **Measure a suspected leak instead of debating it.** Run the cross-validation with the step fitted on all rows and again fitted inside the folds, on the same fold assignment, and subtract. Compare the difference with the error bar from Section 1.1.
- **Report the leak-free number.** If a measured leak is larger than the error bar, the reported score was partly the leak, and the honest version is the one to report.
- **Audit label-informed choices first.** Feature selection, target encoding and tuning on the evaluation folds are where leaks of many points live, and they are largest when there are many candidates and little real signal.
- **Look for rows on both sides of a split.** Exact and near-duplicates leak without touching the label, and they matter most for local models such as nearest neighbours.
- **Fix the cheap violations, but do not panic about them.** Standardising and mean imputation on all rows are worth moving inside the folds as a matter of hygiene; on a few hundred rows they did not move the score.

### Encoding high-cardinality categories

- **Use out-of-fold target encoding**, such as scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder`, which cross-fits the training rows when they pass through `fit_transform` (as in a pipeline); calling `fit` and then `transform` on the same rows does not. Do not write a plain per-level mean.
- **Measure the encoded column's leak directly.** Compute its AUC, or its correlation with the target, on the training rows and on held-out rows. A much higher training value means the column is partly a recording of the training labels.
- **Always compare with dropping the column.** That is the baseline any encoding has to beat, and on rare levels the per-level mean did not beat it.
- **Count rows per level, especially for the rarest levels.** The damage depends on rows per level, not on the total, and it concentrates in the long tail.
- **Do not rely on shrinkage alone with tree models.** It rescued logistic regression in Section 3.2 but left trees almost as badly hurt as the raw mean.

### Using a test set

- **Keep one test set for one final report.** Decide everything first, score once, and report that score with its error bar.
- **Treat every set that informed a decision as training data.** A validation set, a public leaderboard or a holdout used to choose features, models, hyperparameters, thresholds or early-stopping points no longer gives an unbiased score.
- **Make selection decisions inside the training rows,** with cross-validation, so that the search's luck is not spent on the set used for reporting.
- **Count the looks.** Record each decision made after seeing a score on a held-out set. If the count is large and the set is small, expect optimism.
- **If a set must be consulted repeatedly,** limit what each look reveals, for instance by reporting only improvements beyond a margin, as the Ladder does.

## Limits of the evidence

### Sizing leaks (Section 3.1)

- **Small data makes the harmless steps less harmless.** At 569 rows a scaler fitted on 455 or 569 rows is the same scaler. With 30 rows and a heavy-tailed feature it would not be, and standardising could leak a little through one extreme row.
- **Real duplicates are rarely exact.** Near-duplicates, such as augmented images, resampled time series or the same patient twice, leak by the same mechanism with less force and are much harder to find. The 1-NN number is the upper end of the range. Section 2.4 treats the related case of many rows from the same people.
- **Tuning is the leak not measured here.** Choosing hyperparameters on the same cross-validation you report is label-informed choice, so by the rule of Section 3.1 its size depends on how many configurations were tried and how much they differ. The best of many configurations looks good on noise for the same reason the selected features do; Section 4.1 measures that optimism.
- **One dataset.** The breast cancer features are strong, which is why selection leaked nothing on them. On a dataset with weak signal and many features, the setting where selection is used most, it will look much more like the noise experiment.

### Target encoding (Section 3.2)

- **Equally likely levels.** Real categories have a few common levels and a long tail of rare ones. The common levels encode safely and the tail does the damage, so the harm is concentrated where there is least data, which also makes it harder to see in an overall score.
- **One sample size.** There were 4,000 training rows. What matters is rows per level, so the same damage should appear at 40,000 levels with 40,000 rows.
- **Default settings.** `TargetEncoder`'s shrinkage and the boosted trees' regularisation were left at their defaults. Stronger regularisation in the trees reduces their reliance on any single column, including this one.
- **Binary outcome.** The same mechanism applies to regression targets and to multi-class targets, which encode one column per class.

### Reusing a test set (Section 3.3)

- **Adversarial by construction.** The leaderboard attack and the noise selection are the worst cases, with nothing real to find. With strong real signal, the honest part of each score dominates and the adaptive part matters less.
- **Small holdouts.** The holdouts here have 300 to 1,000 rows. In Blum and Hardt's analysis the gain from such attacks shrinks roughly as one over the square root of the holdout's size, so large test sets tolerate more looks; they do not tolerate unlimited ones.
- **One analyst, one strategy.** Real model development mixes holdout-guided decisions with others, and counting how many decisions a test set has informed is rarely possible after the fact, which is the practical argument for a set used once.
- **Greedy selection is crude.** A more careful analyst would regularise and stop earlier; the mechanism, deciding by a score and then reporting that score, is the same.

## Key points

1. Fitting a step on all rows before cross-validating can leak anything from nothing to a third of the accuracy scale. Measure it: run the honest version on the same folds and subtract.
2. Standardising and mean imputation on all rows leaked +0.05 and -0.02 points. Not worth an alarm; still worth fixing.
3. Feature selection on all rows turned coin flips into 0.88 accuracy, growing with the number of candidates, and leaked +0.00 on real data where the real features dominate.
4. Target encoding on all rows leaked +2.4 points from a column of pure noise, and duplicates leaked without touching the label: +0.9 points for logistic regression and +3.7 for 1-NN.
5. A step leaks to the extent it gives the model room to fit the test rows: label-informed choice, or the test row itself in training.
6. Fitting a target encoding on the training rows only protects the test set and nothing else. With 1,000 noise levels, boosted trees fell from AUC 0.763 to 0.640, because the noise column ranked its own training labels at AUC 0.774 and fresh labels at 0.498.
7. Shrinkage largely rescued logistic regression but not trees, which use the order of the encoded values. Cross-fitting matched dropping the column on noise and kept the signal when there was one; encode with out-of-fold means and check how many rows the rarest levels have.
8. A test set can be worn out without training on it: a majority vote of random guesses on coin-flip labels scored 0.713 on the public labels after 1,000 submissions and 0.497 on the private ones, and features kept because they replicated on a holdout gave 0.687 there and 0.498 on fresh data.
9. Forward selection guided by the holdout reported 0.690 after 20 steps, above the true model's 0.676, for a fresh accuracy of 0.576. Guided by cross-validation instead, the holdout matched fresh rows to within 0.001.
10. A test set's honesty is spent by adaptivity. Treat every score that informed a decision as part of training, keep one test set for one final report, and count the decisions made with anything else.

## Exercises

1. Take a pipeline you have shipped and list every step that is fitted to data. For each, record whether it looks at the label and how many options it chooses between. Then check whether any row, or a near-copy of a row, appears on both sides of your splits.
2. Measure the step you are least sure of: run the cross-validation with that step fitted on all rows and again fitted inside the folds, on the same fold assignment, and subtract. If the difference is within the error bar from Section 1.1, you have a style problem. If it is not, the number you reported was partly the leak.
3. Using the rule in Section 3.1, predict without running anything whether selecting 5 of 1,030 features on all rows would leak more on a dataset whose real features were weak, and explain why.
4. Explain why duplicating rows helps a 1-NN classifier's cross-validated score far more than a logistic regression's.
5. Take a model that uses a target-encoded category. Compute the encoded column's AUC (or correlation with the target) on the training rows and on held-out rows; in Section 3.2 the gap was 0.28 of AUC for a column with no signal at all. Then replace the encoding with a cross-fitted one, refit, and compare held-out scores, including against dropping the column.
6. Explain why a level seen once has an encoded value equal to its own row's label under the per-level mean, and what a tree can do with such a column. Then explain why the cross-fitted column scored slightly below an AUC of one half on its own training rows.
7. List every decision made in your last modelling project after looking at a score on the validation or test set: features, models, hyperparameters, thresholds, early stopping, which runs to keep. Mark the ones made on the set you finally reported. If any are marked, hold out a fresh sample, even a small one, score the final model on it once, and compare; the difference is a measurement of how much of the test set's honesty was spent.
8. Explain why a random guess that scored above 0.5 on the public labels carries information about them but none about the private labels, and why a majority vote of many such guesses amplifies it.
