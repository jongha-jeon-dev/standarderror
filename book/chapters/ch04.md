# A Learning Curve Fitted Early Promises More Than More Data Delivers

Chapters 1 to 3 were about reading a score you already have. This chapter is about a decision made before there is much of one. A team has run a pilot study on a few hundred labelled rows and must decide whether labelling ten or a hundred times as many is worth the cost. The standard tool is the learning curve, and the sentence under test is the one that justifies it: *a learning curve tells you whether more data will help.* If the usual way of reading a learning curve forward is biased, the team will buy labels that do not deliver the promised improvement, and may pick the wrong model on the pilot data as well.

The chapter builds a simulated task where the curve can be followed far beyond any pilot study and where the best achievable error is known. It fits the standard two-parameter power law to the early part of the curve and compares its forecasts with what the models actually reach. It explains the failure through the local slope of the curve, then tests the textbook fix, a power law with a floor, and shows where that fix helps and where it misleads. It ends with the same check on the digits, where a second problem appears: the model ranking at pilot size turns over.

## Learning curves and the power-law forecast

A *learning curve* plots a model's test error against the size of its training set. The standard way to extrapolate it is a power law,

$$
\mathrm{err}(n) \approx a\,n^{-b},
$$

where $n$ is the training-set size and $a$ and $b$ are fitted constants. Taking logarithms gives $\log \mathrm{err} = \log a - b \log n$, a straight line on log-log axes with slope $-b$. The procedure is to fit the line to the pilot sizes, extend it, and read off the error at the size you could afford. It is the same move as the scaling laws used to plan large neural-network training runs, made at the scale of a spreadsheet; Chapter 30 examines those laws directly.

Whether this works can be checked only where the curve can be followed much further than the fit. The task here is built for that. It has two Gaussian classes in ten dimensions with different covariance matrices, so the best possible decision boundary is quadratic. Training sets grow from 50 rows to 25,600. Every model is scored on the same 20,000-point test set, on which the *Bayes error* is also computed. The Bayes error is the error of the true decision rule, the rule that assigns each point to the class with the higher true posterior probability; no classifier, with any amount of data, can beat it. Because the densities are known, that rule's error is computed from them on the same test points.

Three models are compared: logistic regression, which draws a linear boundary and so cannot represent the quadratic one; 15-nearest-neighbours (15-NN), which can represent a quadratic boundary but learns it slowly; and gradient-boosted trees, which can represent it too and learn it faster. The code prints the Bayes error and each model's test error at three training sizes.

```python
from standarderror.ml import curves as cu

task = cu.GaussianTask()          # quadratic Bayes boundary, d = 10
curve = cu.task_curve(task)
print(f"Bayes error {task.bayes:.4f}")
for n in (200, 1600, 25600):
    row = "  ".join(f"{k} {curve['error'][k][n]:.4f}"
                    for k in curve["error"])
    print(f"n = {n:>6,}  {row}")
```

```text
Bayes error 0.2684
n =    200  logistic 0.3896  15-NN 0.3830  boosted trees 0.3806
n =  1,600  logistic 0.3734  15-NN 0.3357  boosted trees 0.3230
n = 25,600  logistic 0.3672  15-NN 0.3128  boosted trees 0.2885
```

Logistic regression stalls, as a linear model on a quadratic problem must. 15-NN improves slowly. At 25,600 rows the boosted trees reach 0.289, still 0.020 above the Bayes error and still improving.

## The two-parameter power law runs through the floor

Now pretend the pilot study stopped at 1,600 rows. The next block fits $a\,n^{-b}$ to each flexible model's errors up to that size and extends it to 25,600 rows.

```python
for model in ("15-NN", "boosted trees"):
    f = cu.fit(curve, model, until=1600, floor=False)
    print(f"{model:13s} a n^-b from n <= 1,600 predicts "
          f"{f['predict'](25600):.4f} at 25,600;  "
          f"actual {curve['error'][model][25600]:.4f}")
```

```text
15-NN         a n^-b from n <= 1,600 predicts 0.2698 at 25,600;  actual 0.3128
boosted trees a n^-b from n <= 1,600 predicts 0.2524 at 25,600;  actual 0.2885
```

The boosted trees' prediction, 0.252, is below the Bayes error of 0.268: it forecasts an error that no classifier can achieve. The 15-NN prediction, 0.270, is 0.001 above the Bayes error, a forecast that the slowest of the flexible models will very nearly reach the best possible error, when it is in fact still 0.044 away.

These are not isolated cases. Across all nine fits, three models with pilot studies stopping at 400, 1,600 and 6,400 rows, the two-parameter power law predicted a lower error than the model actually reached at 25,600 every time, and 4 of the nine predictions fell below the Bayes error.

### Why: the log-log slope is not constant

The reason is visible in the *local slope* of the curve on log-log axes: the change in log error divided by the change in log training size between two neighbouring sizes. A power law has a constant local slope, $-b$, at every size. The next block prints every third local slope for each model, from the smallest sizes to the largest.

```python
for model in curve["error"]:
    sl = cu.log_slope(curve, model)
    print(f"{model:13s} local log-log slope  "
          + "  ".join(f"{x:+.3f}" for x in sl[::3]))
```

```text
logistic      local log-log slope  -0.048  -0.045  -0.002
15-NN         local log-log slope  -0.113  -0.060  -0.018
boosted trees local log-log slope  -0.119  -0.096  -0.051
```

The slopes are not constant. In full they are noisy and do not shrink at every step: the boosted trees' slope stays between -0.096 and -0.063 from 100 to 1,600 rows, so over the whole pilot range their curve looks as straight as a power law. But every curve ends far flatter than it starts, the boosted trees at -0.015 between 12,800 and 25,600 rows, as it approaches a floor it cannot go through. No model's error can fall below the Bayes error, and each model has its own limit at or above it, so the curve must bend.

For the boosted trees that bend comes after the pilot stops. A straight line fitted to the early, steep part of the curve carries that steepness forward indefinitely and overshoots. The fit has no way to know that the curve will bend, because nothing in the pilot data shows it. The figure below shows the real curves flattening and the fitted ones continuing to fall.

## Adding a floor, and where that fails

The textbook fix, going back to Cortes and colleagues, is a third parameter:

$$
\mathrm{err}(n) \approx a\,n^{-b} + c,
$$

where $c$, the *floor* or asymptote, is the error the model would reach with unlimited data. The power-law part then describes only the excess over the floor, so the fitted curve can flatten.

### It works where the curve has already bent

For logistic regression the floor works. Its curve has flattened by about a thousand rows: its local slope is -0.045 between 400 and 800 rows and within ±0.011 from 800 on. Because the flattening lies inside the fitted range, the fit can see the floor, and from 6,400 rows it predicts 0.3672 against 0.3672 actual. Its fitted floor, 0.365, is far above the Bayes error, and correctly so: a linear boundary on a quadratic problem has its own limit, set by the model class rather than by the data.

### It guesses where the curve is still falling

For the boosted trees, which are still improving at every size measured, the floor is not something the fit can see, so it guesses. Fitted to 400 rows, the floor is 0.330, above the 0.289 the trees actually reach. The fit concludes that more data will barely help and predicts 0.333 at 25,600 rows, when the trees in fact get to 0.289. Fitted to 1,600 or 6,400 rows, the floor is 0.219 and 0.214, below the Bayes error. The predictions at 25,600 rows are closer, 0.278 and 0.277 against 0.289, but the parameter that is supposed to say how far the model can go has no meaning: no model can reach an error below the Bayes error.

The underlying problem is that, on a stretch of curve with no visible bend, many combinations of $b$ and $c$ fit almost equally well. A steeper decay towards a higher floor and a shallower decay towards a lower floor trace nearly the same path over the pilot range and diverge only beyond it. The data cannot choose between them, so the fitted floor moves a great deal when the fit range changes.

15-NN sits in between. It is still falling at every size measured, yet its floored fits from 1,600 and 6,400 rows miss by only -1.4% and -0.4%, with floors of 0.288 and 0.295, above the Bayes error. A floor can work on a curve that is still falling; what the fit cannot tell you is whether it has.

So the floor is least reliable where it is most needed. A curve that has visibly bent already tells you that more data will not help much, with or without a fit. A curve still falling fast is the case where the labelling decision matters, and there the third parameter is poorly determined: for the boosted trees it moved from 0.330 to 0.219 when the fit range grew from 400 to 1,600 rows. The first figure below traces the fitted floors; the table after it collects all nine predictions.

## On the digits, and the ranking that turns over

The digits allow only 1,200 training images after a fixed test set is set aside, so the check there is shorter: fit up to 300 images, predict 1,200. The models are those of Chapter 1: logistic regression, an RBF SVM and 3-NN. The code prints each model's test error at four training sizes.

```python
dig = cu.digits_curve()
for n in (50, 100, 300, 1200):
    print(f"{n:>5,} images  " + "  ".join(
        f"{k} {dig['error'][k][n]:.3f}" for k in dig["error"]))
```

```text
   50 images  logistic 0.230  RBF SVM 0.355  3-NN 0.256
  100 images  logistic 0.133  RBF SVM 0.163  3-NN 0.132
  300 images  logistic 0.067  RBF SVM 0.060  3-NN 0.052
1,200 images  logistic 0.040  RBF SVM 0.022  3-NN 0.020
```

All nine two-parameter fits, three models and three pilot sizes, were optimistic again. From 300 images the predictions were 0.022 against an actual 0.040 for logistic regression (-46%), 0.019 against 0.022 for the SVM, and 0.015 against 0.020 for 3-NN. The three-parameter fit put the floor at zero for 2 of the three models: on curves this short and steep there is no floor to see.

The table also shows the second way a pilot study misleads. Logistic regression has the lowest error at 50 images and the highest at 1,200, double the error of the other two. It is the most constrained of the three models, and constraint is what small data rewards: a model with fewer ways to fit the data has less room to fit noise when there is little data, and less room to fit the signal when there is a lot. A model chosen on the pilot would be the wrong one. The simulated task shows the same turnover at a smaller scale: logistic regression leads at 50 rows, 15-NN at 100, and the boosted trees from 200 on.

The tasks so far have had classes of similar size. Chapter 5 turns to a common way tasks are not balanced, rare positive classes, and to the resampling methods used to handle them.

## In practice

- **Do not trust a two-parameter power-law forecast from a pilot study.** In every fit here, simulated and real, it promised a lower error than the model reached. Expect it to be optimistic, and do not report it as an estimate.
- **Plot the local log-log slope.** Compute the slope between neighbouring training sizes. If it is shrinking, there is a floor ahead and a straight-line fit will overshoot it.
- **Refit with the largest pilot size left out.** If the forecast at your target size moves by more than the improvement you are hoping for, the curve cannot tell you what the extra data will buy.
- **Distrust a fitted floor on a curve that is still falling.** It can land above what the model will reach or below the Bayes error, and it moves with the fit range. It is reliable mainly where the curve has already bent, and there you hardly need it.
- **Choose the model at the size you will train on.** The best model at pilot size need not be the best at full size. Check the ranking at the largest size you can afford to test.

## Limits of the evidence

- **These are small models on small data.** The scaling laws used to plan large neural-network runs are fitted over many orders of magnitude, far into the straight part of the curve, and have predicted well there. The failure here is specific to fitting early, which is exactly where a pilot study fits.
- **Least squares on the error scale weights the early points.** The large errors at small $n$ dominate an unweighted fit. Fitting on log error, or weighting by each point's variance, changes the numbers; it does not remove the curvature, so it does not change the direction of the bias.
- **The task was chosen to have a known floor.** Real problems have one too, but nobody computes it, which is the point: the only check on a fitted floor is the Bayes error, and in practice it is unavailable.
- **Few training sets at the largest sizes.** The 25,600-row errors average three training sets, and the digits' 1,200-image errors come from one. Their noise is small next to the gaps quoted, but not zero.

## Key points

1. A power law $a\,n^{-b}$ fitted to a pilot study predicted too low an error at the target size in all 18 fits here, simulated and real.
2. On a task with a known Bayes error of 0.268, 4 of 9 such predictions were below it: errors no classifier can reach.
3. Learning curves flatten towards a floor; the local log-log slope shrinks overall, if not at every step, so a straight line fitted early overshoots.
4. The floored form $a\,n^{-b} + c$ worked for logistic regression and, from 1,600 rows, for 15-NN, and failed for the boosted trees, still falling fastest: their fitted floor went from 0.330 (above what they reach) to 0.219 (below Bayes) with the fit range.
5. The best model at pilot size need not be the best at full size. On the digits, logistic regression went from best at 50 images to worst at 1,200.
