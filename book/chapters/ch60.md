# The Most Stable k Is Not the Number of Clusters

Choosing the number of clusters is the question every clustering analysis has to answer, and earlier chapters have shown two popular answers failing. Chapter 32 found the silhouette choosing four clusters in uniform noise, and Chapter 57 found the BIC choosing as many Gaussian components as a cluster's shape could pay for. A third idea sounds more like science than either: a real structure should reproduce. Cluster two random subsamples of the data, compare the two clusterings on the points they share, and repeat; the right k is the one whose answer comes back most consistently. Resampling stability is now a widely used check, and the sentence under test is the rule itself: *the right number of clusters is the one whose clustering is most stable under resampling*. An analyst who reports "k = 4 was the most stable solution" as evidence that four groups exist is relying on it. If it is false, stability certifies groups in data that have none.

The chapter first defines the stability score and the comparison method, the gap statistic. It then applies both to two shapes with no clusters at all, explains with a theoretical result why stability can be high there, and turns to shapes where clusters do exist, including a two-level hierarchy. It then tries both on the handwritten digits. A final section draws together what the five chapters of Part XII measured.

## Two ways to choose k

The stability score used here works as follows. Draw two random subsamples, each containing 80% of the points; run k-means with $k$ clusters on each; restrict both clusterings to the points the two subsamples share; and measure their agreement with the *adjusted Rand index* (ARI), which counts the pairs of points the two partitions treat alike, corrects for chance, and equals 1 for identical partitions. Repeat for 20 pairs of subsamples (10 for the digits) and average. The ARI is invariant to how clusters are numbered, so two runs that find the same groups under different labels agree perfectly. The k with the highest mean agreement is chosen.

Ben-Hur, Elisseeff and Guyon proposed a version of this in 2002. Their own rule looks at the whole distribution of agreements across pairs and picks the largest k whose agreements are still concentrated near 1; the version tested here takes the k with the highest mean agreement.

The comparison is the *gap statistic* of Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie. Let $W_k$ be the total within-cluster dispersion of a k-means clustering with $k$ clusters, the sum over clusters of squared distances of points to their cluster centre. $W_k$ always falls as $k$ grows, so its value alone cannot choose $k$. The gap statistic compares it with what the same procedure gives on reference data drawn uniformly over the same region, which by construction has no clusters:

$$
\mathrm{Gap}(k) = \mathbb{E}^{*}\!\left[\log W_k^{*}\right] - \log W_k,
$$

where the expectation is estimated by averaging over several reference data sets (10 here, 5 for the digits). The rule chooses the smallest $k$ such that $\mathrm{Gap}(k) \ge \mathrm{Gap}(k+1) - s_{k+1}$, where $s_{k+1}$ is the standard deviation of $\log W_{k+1}^{*}$ across the $B$ reference sets, multiplied by $\sqrt{1 + 1/B}$ to allow for the error in the simulated average: stop when one more cluster no longer improves the gap by more than its own noise. Because $\mathrm{Gap}(1)$ is defined, the gap statistic, unlike stability, can answer $k = 1$.

The block below applies both to one elongated Gaussian, a single cloud of 1,000 points with standard deviations 3 and 1, which has no clusters.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import unsup

X = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(1000, 2)) * [3, 1]
for k in (2, 3, 5):                 # one elongated Gaussian, no clusters
    print(f"k={k}  stability {unsup.stability(X, k):.3f}")
g = unsup.gap(X)
print("gap statistic chooses", g["rule"])
```

```text
k=2  stability 0.920
k=3  stability 0.899
k=5  stability 0.636
gap statistic chooses 1
```

## Stability is high where there are no clusters

On the elongated Gaussian, a single cloud three times longer than it is wide, $k = 2$ had a stability of 0.92: two random subsamples split the cloud at almost the same place nearly every time. Stability was 0.90 at $k = 3$ and 0.87 at 4, then dropped, so the highest-mean rule chose 2. Ben-Hur and colleagues' own rule would probably have accepted 3 or 4, which is no better. On a uniform square of 1,000 points the most stable $k$ was 4, at 0.94, the same four that the silhouette chose in Chapter 32. Neither shape has any clusters.

### Why: stability measures the uniqueness of the optimum

Ben-David, von Luxburg and Pál explained why in 2006. Stability measures whether the clustering objective has a unique best answer on the distribution the data come from. If it does, then as the sample grows every subsample's clustering converges to that same answer, and two subsamples agree almost perfectly, whether or not the answer corresponds to real groups. An elongated cloud has a unique best two-way cut for the k-means objective, across its long axis at the centre, so every subsample cuts there. A square has a unique best four-way cut, into quadrants. Both are therefore highly reproducible.

Conversely, where the objective has several equally good answers, as it would for a perfectly round cloud, which can be cut in two along any diameter, different subsamples settle on different answers and stability is low, whether or not clusters exist. Reproducibility is a property of the method on the data, telling us whether its optimum is unique; it is not evidence that the groups are real. And since stability needs at least two clusters to compare, it can never answer that there is only one: at $k = 1$ every point is in the same cluster in both subsamples, so agreement is trivially perfect and carries no information. ## Where clusters exist

On three well-separated unit Gaussian blobs, centred at (0,0), (6,0) and (3,5), stability did its job: $k = 3$ reproduced perfectly (1.00), and every other $k$ less so. Here the unique optimum coincides with the real groups.

The fourth shape is a hierarchy: four unit blobs on a line at $x$ = 0, 4, 20 and 24, forming two pairs that are far apart, with the two blobs within each pair close. Here $k = 2$ reproduced perfectly (1.00) and $k = 4$ almost as well (0.99). Both answers are right, at different scales, and the rule picked the coarser one because merging the close blobs left nothing to disagree about. A hierarchy is not a mistake, but a single "most stable k" hides it.

The gap statistic chose one cluster for the elongated Gaussian and for the square, and three for the blobs. On the two pairs its stopping rule chose 2, and its largest gap was at 4, so it too registered both levels. Of the two methods, only the gap could say "no clusters", because only it compares the data with data that have none. The table below collects both methods' choices.

## The digits

On the 1,797 digits, k-means stability rose from 0.29 at $k = 2$ to a peak of 0.869 at $k = 10$, the number of classes, with $k = 9$ and 11 within 0.013 of it. It is a fragile success. With two other resampling seeds the peak moved to $k = 9$ (0.883 and 0.930). Three neighbouring answers are indistinguishable on this evidence, and Chapter 32 found k-means at $k = 10$ agreeing with the digit classes only partly, so even the right number of clusters does not imply the right clusters.

The gap statistic, computed from 1 to 15, rose at every step by more than the next step's standard error, so its rule found no stopping point and fell back to 15, the largest $k$ tried. At the last step the margin was about 0.001, with five reference sets. It would presumably have gone on. In 64 dimensions, against uniform reference data in a box, every extra centre plausibly explains structure that uniform data lack, whether that structure is the ten digit classes or the many ways each digit is written. The gap statistic was built to detect clustering against a null of none, and on data this far from uniform it detects only that.

## What Part XII measured

Part XII took the labels away and tested five unsupervised methods on data with a known answer. A t-SNE picture kept each point's neighbours and drew cluster sizes and gaps that were not in the data (Chapter 56). The BIC counted the Gaussians a cluster's shape required, more of them the more data it saw (Chapter 57). DBSCAN found any shape at one density and none when the densities differed sixfold or more, and its standard radius heuristic missed on the digits and in one of six draws of the half-moons (Chapter 58). An anomaly detector ranked well and flagged whatever share it was told to; local scores failed for grouped anomalies, and the isolation forest for anomalies hidden in a few of many features (Chapter 59). And the most reproducible clustering was reproducible because its answer was unique, not because the clusters existed (this chapter).

Each method answered a narrower question than the one it is used for: who is near whom, how many Gaussians, how dense, how unusual in these features, how unique the optimum. The common repair was the one this book has relied on since Chapter 1, data with a known answer: simulate structure whose truth you know, including the case of no structure at all, and see whether the method can tell. Part XIII turns from fitting to what happens after a model ships.

## In practice

- **Test any rule for choosing k on data with no clusters first.** A single Gaussian cloud and a uniform square of the same scale as your data are a minimum. If the rule picks k greater than one there, its choice on your data is not evidence of clusters.
- **Report stability as a property of the method's answer.** "k-means at k = 4 is reproducible" is what stability shows; "the data contain four groups" is not.
- **Compare with a null that has no structure.** Use a method that can answer $k = 1$, such as the gap statistic, or rerun the whole procedure on data with the structure destroyed, for example by shuffling each column independently.
- **Look for more than one stable k.** When two values of k are both nearly perfectly stable, the data may have a hierarchy; report both levels instead of the coarser one alone.
- **Check the choice across resampling seeds.** On the digits the most stable k moved with the seed; report the range of k that are indistinguishable, not a single winner.

## Limits of the evidence

- **k-means only.** Stability was measured for k-means, whose objective has unique optima on these shapes. Other algorithms have other objectives and other kinds of stability; the argument of Ben-David and colleagues applies to any algorithm whose answer converges to a unique optimum.
- **One version of each method.** Stability has variants that compare to the full-data clustering, use bootstrap samples or normalise by a null; the gap statistic has variants in its reference distribution. Some of them can answer $k = 1$; this one cannot.
- **Two-dimensional shapes.** Four shapes in two dimensions isolate the mechanism. Whether higher-dimensional data tend toward unique or ambiguous optima depends on the data, and the digits are one example.
- **k from 2 to 8.** On the uniform square stability rose again from $k = 7$ to $k = 8$ while staying below its value at 4. A wider range was not tried, and could in principle find a more stable k for any shape.

## Key points

1. Stability chose $k = 2$ for one elongated Gaussian (0.92) and $k = 4$ for a uniform square (0.94); neither has clusters, and stability cannot answer one.
2. Stability measures whether the clustering objective has a unique optimum, which a clusterless shape can have; reproducibility is not evidence that groups exist.
3. It chose correctly for three separated blobs and chose the coarser of two right answers for two pairs of blobs.
4. The gap statistic said one cluster where there were none and three where there were three, and on the digits found no stopping point up to 15; stability chose 10 or 9 there, depending on the resampling seed.
5. Test any rule for choosing k on data with no clusters first. Report reproducibility as evidence about the method's answer, and compare against a null with no structure before claiming the structure exists.
