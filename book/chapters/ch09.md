# Training a Network

Training a neural network means making a run of choices that most training scripts make by default: how the weights start, how large a step to take and on how many examples, which optimiser takes the step, and which layers sit between the weighted sums and the activations. Each default comes with a one-line justification that is repeated in textbooks and documentation, and a practitioner usually meets these justifications not as claims but as settings already filled in. This chapter treats five of them as claims and measures them, on the UCI handwritten digits and, where a task with room for improvement is needed, on the simulated task of Section 5.1, whose best possible error is known. The theme throughout is that a default is right for a reason, and that the reason is narrower than the sentence that usually states it. When a run fails, or a model behaves differently in production than in testing, the narrower reason is the one that explains it.

The sections test one sentence each:

- Section 9.1: *initialisation only needs to break symmetry*.
- Section 9.2: *learning rate and batch size are separate knobs*.
- Section 9.3: *Adam converges faster and generalises as well as SGD*.
- Section 9.4: *batch normalisation normalises each example*.
- Section 9.5: *dropout at test time is approximated by scaling the weights*.

The sections follow a training run from before its first step to the moment its predictions are served. Section 9.1 starts with the weights, and shows that in a deep network breaking symmetry is necessary but not sufficient: the scale of the random weights decides, through a factor applied once per layer, whether the network can train at all. Once the network starts in a trainable state, the learning rate and the batch size set how it moves. Section 9.2 shows that with plain stochastic gradient descent the two act through their ratio, until the learning rate reaches a limit that does not move with the batch. Section 9.3 replaces plain SGD with the two optimisers most networks are trained with, Adam and SGD with momentum, and finds that the familiar verdict on them depends on how each was tuned, which connects the learning-rate grid of Section 9.2 to the question of what a comparison of methods measures.

The last two sections turn to layers that compute one function during training and another at inference. Section 9.4 shows that batch normalisation normalises an example with the statistics of the other examples in its batch, so that a prediction in training mode depends on its companions, and that small or unrepresentative batches make the layer harmful. Section 9.5 takes dropout, the other common layer with two modes, and shows that the standard way of switching it off reproduces the labels of a principled average over random masks but not its probabilities. Both sections end with the same practical warning, from two directions: a network left in training mode at serving time gives answers that depend on things other than its input.

The chapter's closing sections gather the practical guidance, the limits of the evidence and the exercises from all five sections.

## Initialisation Must Break Symmetry, at the Right Scale

<!-- source: ml-21-init-scale; hero: ml121-hero.png -->


Before a network takes its first gradient step, every weight already has a value, and the textbook explains why those values are random with an argument about symmetry. The conclusion usually drawn from it is the sentence under test: *initialisation only needs to break symmetry*. A practitioner relies on it whenever a layer is created with a default initialiser and never revisited. If it is false, a correctly coded network with a sensible learning rate can fail to train at all, for a reason settled before training begins.

Section 1.2 showed networks that differed only in their random seed and still disagreed on a fifth of their answers; here the random starting point itself is the subject. This section first confirms the symmetry argument with a network whose weights all start equal. It then draws random weights at several scales, follows the size of the activations through thirty untrained layers, derives the per-layer factor that governs that size, and shows what it does to training. A three-layer control shows that depth is what turns a mis-scaled initialisation into a failure.

### Why the weights start random


Consider one fully connected layer of 128 units. If every weight in the layer starts at the same value, every unit computes the same weighted sum of the same inputs and produces the same output. In the backward pass each unit then receives the same gradient, because its outgoing weights are equal too, and so every unit makes the same update. The weights stay equal, and the layer behaves as a single unit copied 128 times however long it trains. This is the *symmetry problem*: gradient descent preserves any symmetry between units present at the start. Random weights break the tie, because each unit begins as a slightly different function and receives a slightly different gradient.

The first half of the argument is easy to confirm. A network with 30 hidden layers of 128 units, every weight set to 0.01, is trained on the UCI handwritten digits with the best of four learning rates. It scores 0.197 on the test half. After training, the 128 units of its first layer are still exactly identical: the count of distinct units is 1. Training does not break symmetry on its own.

### Random weights at different scales


The second half of the sentence, the word *only*, is the claim to test. Every standard initialiser draws each weight independently with a standard deviation proportional to $1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}$, where the *fan-in* of a layer is the number of inputs each of its units receives (here 128 for every hidden layer after the first). The experiment draws weights from a normal distribution with standard deviation

$$
\sigma_w = \frac{g}{\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}},
$$

and varies the *gain* $g$. Real initialisers differ in detail: some draw from a uniform distribution rather than a normal one, and Glorot's averages the fan-in and fan-out, but all share this scaling. Every value of $g$ breaks symmetry, since every weight is drawn independently. The biases start at zero.

The code below builds the 30-layer network with tanh and with ReLU activations, at gains of 0.5, 1, $\sqrt{2}$ (printed as 1.41) and 2, and passes a batch of digits through it without any training. It prints the standard deviation of the activations at layers 1, 10 and 30.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

r = nets.init_depth()          # 30 hidden layers of 128 units
for row in r["rows"]:
    sd = row["stds"]
    print(f"{row['act']:4s} g = {row['gain']:.2f}: activation sd at "
          f"layers 1, 10, 30 = {sd[0]:.2g}, {sd[9]:.2g}, {sd[29]:.2g}")
```

```text
tanh g = 0.50: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.38, 0.00071, 6.7e-10
tanh g = 1.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.58, 0.22, 0.13
tanh g = 1.41: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.68, 0.56, 0.54
tanh g = 2.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.76, 0.73, 0.72
relu g = 0.50: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.29, 2.5e-05, 3.8e-14
relu g = 1.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.58, 0.026, 4e-05
relu g = 1.41: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 0.82, 0.83, 1.3
relu g = 2.00: activation sd at layers 1, 10, 30 = 1.2, 27, 4.3e+04
```

By the thirtieth layer the eight initialisations differ by many orders of magnitude: three stay roughly level, tanh at $g = 1$ fades slowly, ReLU at $g = 1$ shrinks to 4e-05, two collapse to $10^{-9}$ and below, and ReLU at $g = 2$ grows to about $4 \times 10^4$. The figure below plots the full profile on a log scale.

At g = √2 the thirtieth layer's activations have standard deviation 1.33; at g = 1, 4e-05.](ml121-f0-stds.png){alt="Eight lines on a log scale over 30 layers: three stay roughly level, one tanh line falls slowly, the ReLU line for g = 1 falls to about 1e-5, two fall to 1e-9 and below, and one ReLU line rises to about 4e4."}

### Each layer multiplies the scale by a fixed factor


The ReLU lines are close to straight on the log scale, which says that each layer multiplies the scale of its input by a roughly constant factor, and thirty layers apply that factor thirty times. For a ReLU layer the factor can be derived.

Let a unit's pre-activation be $z = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j x_j$, where $n$ is the fan-in, the weights $w_j$ are independent with mean zero and variance $g^2/n$, and they are independent of the inputs $x_j$. Then

$$
\mathrm{Var}(z) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathrm{Var}(w_j)\, \mathbb{E}[x_j^2] = g^2 \cdot \overline{\mathbb{E}[x^2]},
$$

where the bar denotes the average over the $n$ inputs. The weight variance multiplies the *second moment* of the input, its mean square, by $g^2$; dividing by the fan-in in the weight variance is exactly what cancels the sum over $n$ inputs. Because $z$ is symmetric about zero, the ReLU sets half of its values to zero and leaves the other half unchanged, so

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{ReLU}(z)^2\big] = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(z).
$$

Each ReLU layer therefore multiplies the second moment of the signal by $g^2/2$. Its standard deviation, which is what the code reports, is a fixed fraction of the square root of that second moment as long as the shape of the distribution does not change from layer to layer, so it too is multiplied by about $g/\sqrt{2}$.

Measured over the 29 steps from layer 1 to layer 30, the factor is **0.719** at $g = 1$ (theory 0.707), **1.017** at $g = \sqrt{2}$ (theory 1) and **1.438** at $g = 2$ (theory 1.414). Small per-layer factors compound into enormous overall ones. At $g = 1$ the thirtieth layer's activations are 7e-05 times the first layer's; at $g = 2$, $4 \times 10^4$ times. Only $g = \sqrt{2}$, which makes the factor exactly 1, keeps the scale level. That is the value He and colleagues derived for ReLU networks in 2015, by the same argument; the resulting rule, weights with variance $2/\text{fan-in}$, is known as *He initialisation*.

#### Tanh saturates, and the backward pass behaves differently

Tanh is close to the identity near zero and flattens towards ±1 for large inputs, a behaviour called *saturation*. At small scales it therefore behaves almost linearly, and at $g = 0.5$ its activations shrink geometrically, to 7e-10 by layer 30. The gradient reaching the first layer has norm 1e-09: a *vanishing gradient*, the backward-pass counterpart of the vanishing signal.

At $g = 2$ the activations cannot grow past ±1, so the forward pass stays bounded, at a standard deviation of 0.72 by layer 30. The gradient flowing back grows instead: the first layer's gradient norm is 25.4 and the last layer's 0.62. A tanh network can look well behaved in its activations while its gradients are *exploding*, growing as they pass backwards. At $g = 1$, the value to which Glorot and Bengio's 2010 analysis points for tanh (the basis of *Glorot* or *Xavier initialisation*), the activations fade slowly, to 0.13 by layer 30.

### What the scale does to training


The next block trains each of the eight networks. Each gets the best of four learning rates, 0.001, 0.003, 0.01 and 0.03, so that no initialisation loses merely for want of the right step size. The training uses stochastic gradient descent with momentum for 30 epochs. The block also prints the constant initialisation's result and its count of distinct first-layer units.

```python
for row in r["rows"]:
    print(f"{row['act']:4s} g = {row['gain']:.2f}: best test accuracy "
          f"{row['best']:.3f} at learning rate {row['best_lr']}")
print(f"all weights 0.01: {r['const_best']:.3f}; distinct units in "
      f"layer 1 after training: {r['const_distinct_units']}")
```

```text
tanh g = 1.00: best test accuracy 0.949 at learning rate 0.003
tanh g = 1.41: best test accuracy 0.919 at learning rate 0.01
tanh g = 2.00: best test accuracy 0.726 at learning rate 0.001
relu g = 0.50: best test accuracy 0.101 at learning rate 0.001
relu g = 1.00: best test accuracy 0.132 at learning rate 0.001
relu g = 1.41: best test accuracy 0.919 at learning rate 0.003

```

Four of the eight random initialisations do not train at all in thirty epochs; with ten balanced classes, chance accuracy is about 0.10. ReLU at $g = 1$ scores **0.132**. Its signal reaches the last hidden layer at less than a ten-thousandth of its size, and the gradients reaching the early layers are of order 1e-05, so the early layers barely move. ReLU at $g = 2$ scores 0.099; its activations and gradients are tens of thousands of times too large, and none of the learning rates tried can take a useful step through them. Tanh at $g = 0.5$ and ReLU at $g = 0.5$ also score chance.

The two failing ReLU networks at $g = 1$ and $g = 2$ sit either side of the one that succeeds by a factor of only $\sqrt{2}$ in the weights' standard deviation; $g = \sqrt{2}$ scores **0.919**.

Tanh at $g = 1$ and $g = \sqrt{2}$ both train, to 0.949 and 0.919. Tanh at $g = 2$ reaches 0.726, at the smallest learning rate tried, which is where an exploding gradient does least harm. With one seed and a coarse grid of four learning rates, the ordering among the networks that do train is not the point. The point is the gap between the networks that train and those that cannot.

### Depth is what makes the scale matter


The control repeats the whole experiment with three hidden layers instead of thirty.

```python
r3 = nets.init_depth(depth=3)  # the same, with 3 hidden layers
print("  ".join(f"{row['act']} {row['gain']:.2f}: {row['best']:.3f}"
                for row in r3["rows"][:4]))
print("  ".join(f"{row['act']} {row['gain']:.2f}: {row['best']:.3f}"
                for row in r3["rows"][4:]))
print(f"constant: {r3['const_best']:.3f}")
```

```text
tanh 0.50: 0.954  tanh 1.00: 0.960  tanh 1.41: 0.959  tanh 2.00: 0.947
relu 0.50: 0.969  relu 1.00: 0.972  relu 1.41: 0.962  relu 2.00: 0.950
constant: 0.385
```

Every random initialisation trains above 0.9. The per-layer factors are unchanged; only the number of times they are applied differs. Two multiplications by 0.707 halve the signal, which the first few updates can absorb by adjusting the weights. Twenty-nine multiplications shrink it to less than a ten-thousandth, which they cannot. The constant initialisation still fails at three layers, scoring 0.385: the symmetry problem does not depend on depth. The figure below sets the two depths side by side.

![The same gains that fail at thirty layers all train at three. The constant initialisation fails at both.](ml121-f1-bars.png){alt="Paired bars: with 3 layers every random initialisation reaches above 0.9; with 30 layers four of the eight fall to near chance, and the constant initialisation is low at both depths."}

So the sentence holds for these shallow networks and fails for deep ones. Breaking symmetry is necessary at any depth. In a deep network it is not sufficient: the scale must be chosen so that each layer passes on a signal of about the size it received, in both directions. That is the whole content of the initialisers that deep-learning libraries now apply by default, and the reason they differ by activation. Glorot's rule targets tanh and sigmoid, He's targets ReLU, and using either with the other's activation is risky, as the ReLU network at $g = 1$ shows. The table below collects the activation scale, gradient size and accuracy for every configuration at both depths.

![Every row but the last breaks symmetry. A scale per layer far from 1 stops training at thirty layers; one near 1 is not enough on its own: tanh at g = 2 has 0.998 and scores 0.726.](ml121-f2-table.png){alt="A nine-row table of activation scale, gradient size and accuracy for each activation and gain at 30 and 3 layers."}

The table also carries a qualification: tanh at $g = 2$ has a per-layer scale of 0.998, almost level, yet scores only 0.726. A level forward signal is not enough on its own; this is the network whose gradients grow on the way back. Once a network starts in a trainable state, the learning rate decides how fast it moves, and Section 9.2 examines that setting together with the batch size.

## Learning Rate and Batch Size Are One Knob, Until the Rate Hits a Wall

<!-- source: ml-22-lr-batch; hero: ml122-hero.png -->


Section 9.1 showed that a network's initial scale decides whether it trains at all. Once it starts in a trainable state, the next two settings decide how it moves: the learning rate and the batch size, which every training script exposes. They are usually treated as separate decisions: the batch size is set by the memory available, and the learning rate is tuned afterwards for that batch. The sentence under test is that division of labour: *learning rate and batch size are separate knobs*. A practitioner relies on it whenever a script moves to a machine with more memory, the batch size goes up, and the tuned learning rate is carried over unchanged. If the two settings in fact act together, that move silently changes the training run, and a learning rate tuned at one batch size says little about another.

This section trains one small network over a grid of learning rates and batch sizes with the number of training examples held fixed, and shows first that the best learning rate grows in proportion to the batch size, at least from batches of 8 to 32. It then shows that this is a consequence of something more exact, that the run is governed by the ratio of the two settings, and explains why. Finally it locates the point where the proportionality ends, a learning rate beyond which training fails whatever the batch size, and draws out what that wall means for large batches.

### The experiment: a grid at a fixed number of examples


*Stochastic gradient descent* (SGD) with *minibatches* of size $B$ updates the weights $w$ using the average gradient over $B$ randomly chosen training examples:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i \in \text{batch}} \nabla \ell_i(w),
$$

where $\eta$ is the *learning rate* and $\ell_i$ is the loss on example $i$. One *epoch* is one pass through the training set. The variant used here is plain SGD: no momentum, no weight decay, nothing adaptive, so that the update is exactly the formula above.

The network has two hidden layers of 128 units with ReLU activations and is trained on half the UCI digits, 898 images. Batch sizes are 8, 32, 128 and 512, and learning rates double from 0.003 to 6.144. Every run sees the same number of examples, 20 epochs' worth, so a batch of 8 takes 2,245 steps and a batch of 512 takes 35. Holding the examples fixed rather than the steps is the comparison that matters in practice, because the number of examples processed is what a training budget pays for. The score is the training loss (cross-entropy) at the end, averaged over two seeds, because the question is about optimisation rather than generalisation.

The first block prints, for each batch size, the learning rate with the lowest final training loss, that loss, and the test accuracy of the run.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

r = nets.lr_batch()     # plain SGD, 20 epochs of examples, 2 seeds
for B, lr, loss, acc in zip(r["batches"], r["best_lr"], r["best_loss"],
                            r["best_acc"]):
    print(f"batch {B:3d}: best rate {lr:.3f}  training loss {loss:.4f}  "
          f"test {acc:.3f}")
```

```text
batch   8: best rate 0.192  training loss 0.0005  test 0.972
batch  32: best rate 0.768  training loss 0.0005  test 0.974
batch 128: best rate 1.536  training loss 0.0016  test 0.968
batch 512: best rate 1.536  training loss 0.0264  test 0.964
```

The best learning rate for batches of 32 is **four times** the best for batches of 8, and the two reach the same training loss and almost the same test accuracy. This is the *linear scaling rule*: when the batch size is multiplied by $k$, multiply the learning rate by $k$. Continuing the pattern, batches of 128 and 512 would want rates of 3.072 and 12.288. Instead, both stop at 1.536, and their losses are higher. The figure below plots the final training loss against the learning rate for each batch size.

![Each fourfold increase in batch size moves the curve two grid points to the right, until the curve runs into the same wall.](ml122-f0-lr.png){alt="Four falling curves on log axes, each shifted to the right of the last, all cut off where training fails, the two right-hand ones at the same learning rate near 3."}

Because the grid doubles the rate, a fourfold change in rate is two grid points, so each curve is the previous one shifted two points to the right. The point where a curve is cut off shifts with it for the two smallest batches, but not for the two largest, whose curves are cut off at the same rate.

### The ratio of rate to batch size sets the run


"Best rate" is a coarse summary of a curve. The rule holds far more exactly when the ratio $\eta/B$ is fixed and the loss is read across batch sizes. The next block does that for three ratios, picking out, for each batch size, the grid point whose rate gives that ratio.

```python
import math

for ratio in (0.00075, 0.003, 0.012):     # learning rate / batch size
    row = []
    for i, B in enumerate(r["batches"]):
        j = round(math.log2(ratio * B / 0.003))   # grid is 0.003 * 2**j
        row.append(f"B={B}: {r['loss'][i][j]:.4f}")
    print(f"rate/batch {ratio:.5f}   " + "  ".join(row))
```

```text
rate/batch 0.00075   B=8: 0.1832  B=32: 0.1835  B=128: 0.1849  B=512: 0.1892
rate/batch 0.00300   B=8: 0.0183  B=32: 0.0183  B=128: 0.0184  B=512: 0.0264
rate/batch 0.01200   B=8: 0.0016  B=32: 0.0016  B=128: 0.0016  B=512: 6.1513
```

At a ratio of 0.00075 the four batch sizes reach losses of 0.183 to 0.189, although the batch of 8 takes 2,245 steps and the batch of 512 only 35. At 0.003 the first three agree to within 1%. Taking every pair of runs on the grid in which both the batch and the rate are four times larger, and in which both rates are at least four times below the rate at which their batch size fails (defined below), the losses differ by at most **2.3%**. Closer to failure the pairs part, by up to 44%. Plotted against the ratio, as in the figure below, the four curves are one curve until each in turn leaves it.

![Away from the wall, runs with the same ratio reach the same loss to within 2.3%. The batch of 512 leaves the shared curve first.](ml122-f1-ratio.png){alt="The four curves collapse onto one falling line; the batches of 8 and 32 leave it together, the batch of 128 earlier and the batch of 512 first."}

#### Why the ratio, and not each setting

Two arguments lead to the same ratio. The first is arithmetic. With a fixed number of examples, a batch four times larger means four times fewer steps. Compare four consecutive steps at rate $\eta$ on batches $b_1, \dots, b_4$ of size $B$ with one step at rate $4\eta$ on their union, a batch of $4B$. If the gradient changes little over those four steps, the four small steps move the weights by approximately

$$
\eta \sum_{k=1}^{4} \bar g_{b_k},
$$

where $\bar g_{b_k}$ is the average gradient on batch $b_k$, and the single large step moves them by

$$
4\eta \cdot \frac{1}{4} \sum_{k=1}^{4} \bar g_{b_k},
$$

which is the same quantity. The approximation is good when the learning rate is small, because then each step changes the weights, and so the gradient, very little. It degrades as the rate grows, which is consistent with the pairs parting near failure.

The second argument concerns noise. A minibatch gradient is an average of $B$ per-example gradients, so its variance about the full-data gradient is proportional to $1/B$. Smith and Le showed that the size of the random kicks SGD gives the weights, which they call the noise scale, grows in proportion to the learning rate divided by the batch size. In the continuous-time approximation behind that result, two runs with the same $\eta/B$ and the same number of examples follow the same expected path with the same amount of noise around it. Either way, the run is set by $\eta/B$, and the two "separate knobs" are one.

### The wall: a learning rate that does not move with the batch


The last line of the previous output is where the rule ends. At a ratio of 0.012 the batch of 512 needs a learning rate of 6.144, and the run ends with a mean training loss of 6.15. For this section a run has *failed* when its mean training loss ends above 1. For reference, a network that has learned nothing, and assigns probability one tenth to each of the ten classes, has cross-entropy $\ln 10 = 2.30$.

Every batch size fails somewhere on the grid: the batch of 8 at a rate of 0.384 (mean loss 1.92), the batch of 32 at 1.536 (2.32), and the batches of 128 and 512 both at **3.072**. At that rate the batch of 128 diverges outright, ending at a loss of 6.01. The batch of 512 is degraded rather than diverged, ending at 1.27, worse than at any smaller rate but still below a network that has learned nothing.

For the two small batches the failure point follows the ratio, as the best rate did: both fail at 0.048 per example. For the two large batches it does not; they fail at the same learning rate. That is what a limit unrelated to the batch size would look like, though with only two batch sizes and a grid of doublings the pattern is suggestive rather than shown.

There is a standard candidate for such a limit. Near a minimum, a loss surface is approximately quadratic, and gradient descent on a quadratic whose sharpest curvature (largest second derivative) is $\lambda$ is stable only for

$$
\eta < \frac{2}{\lambda}.
$$

Above that rate each step overshoots along the sharpest direction by more than it corrects, and the error grows. This limit is a property of the loss surface, not of the batch, so it binds a full-batch step and a large-batch step alike. The small batches hit a limit that scales with the batch, as a limit set by gradient noise would; the large batches hit one that does not, as a limit set by curvature would. Curvature itself was not measured here, so the attribution is an interpretation consistent with the pattern rather than a demonstration.

#### What happens beyond the wall

Once a batch size needs a learning rate above the wall, the linear scaling rule has nothing left to offer: the rate cannot be raised to keep the ratio. Here the batch of 128, at its best rate, reached a training loss 3.3 times the batch of 32's, and the batch of 512 a loss 55 times as high. Test accuracy fell from 0.974 to 0.968 and 0.964. With 35 steps in total, the likeliest reading is that the batch of 512 did not travel far enough, and the only way to travel further is more steps.

This is the regime McCandlish and colleagues describe with the *critical batch size*: beyond it, a larger batch buys little reduction in the number of steps needed to reach a given loss, so it mostly spends more computation for the same progress. The table below summarises the four batch sizes.

![Batches of 128 and 512 would need rates of 3.072 and 12.288 to keep the ratio. Both rates fail.](ml122-f2-table.png){alt="A four-row table: the best rate grows fourfold from batch 8 to 32 and stops at 1.536; training loss rises sharply for the batch of 512."}

### Reading a training script in terms of the ratio


The practical rule is not "tune both". It is to treat $\eta/B$ as the setting, and to change the batch size only together with the learning rate, in proportion, while the rate stays well below the point where training fails. Below the wall the change is free: the batches of 32 and 8 reached the same loss, 0.0005 and 0.0005, and the batch of 32 did it with a quarter of the steps. Above the wall, a larger batch is a decision to train less, and has to be paid for with more epochs.

The ratio also says how to read a tuning result. A learning rate tuned at one batch size is a ratio tuned at that batch size. Copying the rate to a run with four times the batch, as happens when a script moves to a larger machine, divides the effective setting by four. On this grid, the batch of 32's best rate, 0.768, gives the batch of 128 a training loss of 0.0050 instead of 0.0005.

Goyal and colleagues trained an ImageNet model in an hour on this principle, with batches of 8,192 and the learning rate scaled up to match. They added a *warm-up*: a few epochs in which the learning rate rises gradually to its target. Its purpose is to keep the large rate from diverging in the first steps, when the network changes fastest. In the terms of this section, warm-up is a way of approaching the same wall at the start of training without crossing it. Section 9.3 turns from plain SGD to the optimiser most networks are actually trained with, Adam.

## Adam Is a Little Faster and No Better

<!-- source: ml-23-adam-sgd; hero: ml123-hero.png -->


Section 9.2 found that for plain SGD the learning rate and the batch size act as one setting. Most networks today are not trained with plain SGD. They are trained with Adam, and its reputation is the sentence under test: *Adam converges faster and generalises as well as SGD*. A practitioner relies on it every time Adam is chosen by default, and every time a comparison of optimisers is read as a verdict on the optimisers themselves. Both halves have been challenged. If the first half is false, the time saved by choosing Adam is illusory; if the second is false, the choice costs accuracy on new data. And if comparisons of optimisers depend mainly on how carefully each one was tuned, many published rankings measure the tuning rather than the method.

This section compares Adam with SGD with momentum on the UCI digits and on a simulated task where the best possible error is known. It first describes how each optimiser updates the weights. It then tunes both by the same procedure and compares their test accuracy over twenty splits, which addresses generalisation. Next it measures speed at every learning rate on each optimiser's grid, and shows that which optimiser is faster depends on the rate. Finally it explains why the tuning procedure chose the rates it did, and what that implies for comparing optimisers.

### Two optimisers


*SGD with momentum* keeps a running *velocity* $v$, an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients, and moves the weights along it:

$$
v \leftarrow \mu v + g, \qquad w \leftarrow w - \eta v,
$$

where $g$ is the current minibatch gradient, $\eta$ the learning rate and $\mu$ the momentum, here 0.9 as is usual in practice. Momentum smooths the gradient across steps and lengthens the effective step along directions in which successive gradients agree. The size of each step is proportional to the size of the gradient.

*Adam* (Kingma and Ba) keeps two running averages for every weight: $m$, of the gradient, and $s$, of the squared gradient. After correcting both for their initialisation at zero, it updates each weight by

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s} + \epsilon},
$$

with $\epsilon$ a small constant for numerical safety. Dividing by the root of the average squared gradient normalises each weight's step by an estimate of that weight's gradient scale. A weight with large gradients and a weight with small ones therefore move at similar rates, and the size of a step is set mainly by $\eta$ rather than by the magnitude of the gradient. This normalisation is one reason the ratio rule of Section 9.2 does not carry over to Adam unchanged.

The challenges to Adam's reputation come from two directions. Wilson and colleagues reported adaptive methods such as Adam generalising worse than SGD. Choi and colleagues found that rankings of optimisers change when every optimiser's settings are tuned with the same care. This section follows the second point and tunes both by one procedure.

### Tuned the same way, the two generalise equally well


The tuning uses a separate split of the digits, kept apart from the evaluation splits. On it, each optimiser's learning rate is chosen from a grid of five rates spanning a factor of 100, by accuracy on a held-out fifth of the training data, a *validation set*. Both then train from the same initial weights on twenty fresh splits. The network has two hidden layers of 256 units with ReLU activations and is trained for 30 epochs in minibatches of 32.

The same procedure is repeated on the simulated task from Section 5.1: two overlapping Gaussian classes in 10 dimensions, with 2,000 training rows per split and a network with two hidden layers of 64 units. #### Sensitivity to the learning rate

What does differ between the optimisers is how much the rate matters. Across Adam's grid of five rates, spanning a factor of 100, test accuracy stayed between 0.961 and 0.977; across SGD's, between 0.928 and 0.975. The mechanism was not isolated here, and a range measured across a grid depends on where the grid sits relative to each optimiser's good rates. Lower sensitivity is a practical virtue, but a different claim from converging faster. ### A narrower sentence


On these two problems, measured over twenty splits with both optimisers tuned, the sentence becomes: *at a learning rate chosen for speed, Adam reaches a low training loss a little sooner than SGD with momentum; tuned for validation accuracy, the two generalise equally well; Adam's accuracy depends less on the learning rate*. Each part is narrower than the original, and each is measured.

The generalisation gap Wilson and colleagues reported does not appear here. Their networks were far larger and their tasks harder, so this is not a refutation of their result; it is a reminder that "Adam generalises worse" is also a claim about particular settings. The honest summary is that the choice of optimiser matters less than tuning whichever one is chosen, and that a comparison which tunes one optimiser and not the other measures the tuning. ## Batch Normalisation Normalises the Batch, Not the Example

<!-- source: ml-24-batchnorm; hero: ml124-hero.png -->


The networks of Sections 9.2 and 9.3 had nothing between their layers but activations. Most modern networks also have a normalisation layer there, most often batch normalisation, and it is usually described in one sentence, which is the sentence under test: *batch normalisation normalises each example*, rescaling its activations to a standard range. A practitioner meets it in almost every convolutional network, and relies on it whenever a normalised model is trained in small batches, fed batches sorted by label or source, or accidentally served in training mode. If the sentence is false, and the statistics used to normalise an example come from the other examples around it, then an example's prediction depends on its companions in the batch, and the network computes different functions during training and at inference.

This section first defines the layer and its two modes. It then takes a trained network and measures how often one test digit's predicted label changes when the digit is placed in different random batches, at several batch sizes and with companions drawn from a single class. It explains the extreme case of a batch of two exactly. It then trains networks at each batch size, with and without normalisation, and shows that small batches make the normalised network worse than an unnormalised one. Finally it describes what the common alternatives change.

### A layer with two modes


*Batch normalisation* (Ioffe and Szegedy), the first *normalisation layer* to be widely used, operates on each unit separately. For a unit whose values on the $B$ examples of a minibatch are $x_1, \dots, x_B$, it computes the batch mean and variance,

$$
\mu_B = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} x_i, \qquad \sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} (x_i - \mu_B)^2,
$$

normalises each value, and then applies a learned scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$:

$$
\hat x_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y_i = \gamma \hat x_i + \beta,
$$

where $\epsilon$ is a small constant that prevents division by zero. Training with the layer is faster and less sensitive to the initial scale of the weights, the problem Section 9.1 above measured.

The mean and standard deviation in these formulas are not the example's. They are the batch's. During training, the layer is in *training mode*: an example's output is computed from statistics of the other examples it happens to be batched with. The layer also keeps *running averages* of $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$, exponentially weighted averages updated at every training step. At inference it switches to *inference mode* (also called evaluation mode) and uses those running averages instead, so that a prediction depends on the example alone. The two modes compute two different functions, and the question is how different they are.

### One digit, many batches


The network is fully connected, 64-128-128-10 (64 pixel inputs, two hidden layers, ten outputs), with batch normalisation before each ReLU activation. It is trained on half the digits with SGD (momentum 0.9, learning rate 0.05) in batches of 64 for 20 epochs. Then 100 test digits are taken, and each digit's prediction is computed in training mode inside 200 different random batches of other test digits. The running averages are frozen during this evaluation, so that evaluating does not change the network.

The block prints the network's accuracy on the 100 digits in inference mode and, for each batch size, how often a digit's training-mode label differs from its inference-mode label, together with the training-mode accuracy. Its last line repeats the batch-of-16 case with all 15 companions drawn from one class.

### The companions are an input


The dependence falls with batch size because the batch mean and variance are estimates of the population mean and variance, and like any sample estimates they settle towards the population values as the sample grows. In batches of 64 the batch statistics should be close to the running averages, and training mode computes nearly the same function as inference mode. With 16 examples the estimates are noisier: in a batch of 16 the label still changes in 3.2% of batches, and 46% of the digits change label in at least one of their 200.

#### A larger batch is not enough; it must be a random sample

The dependence does not fall merely because the batch is large. It falls because a random batch is a *random sample* of the population, so its statistics estimate the population's. The last line of the output keeps the batch at 16 but draws the 15 companions from a single class, as happens when data arrive sorted by label, by date or by source. The digit's label now differs from its inference-mode label in **22.6%** of batches, 7 times as often as with random companions. The batch statistics describe the companions; the companions are all 3s, or all 7s; and the network normalises the digit against them, as if it were being compared with a population made entirely of one digit. A batch statistic is only a good stand-in for the population statistic when the batch is a representative sample, and no batch size repairs a biased sample.

#### A batch of two keeps only an ordering

Batches of 2 show the limiting case, and it can be worked out exactly. For two values $a$ and $b$ of a unit, the batch mean is their midpoint and the standard deviation (with the $1/B$ divisor above) is half their distance:

$$
\mu_B = \frac{a + b}{2}, \qquad \sigma_B = \frac{|a - b|}{2}.
$$

Normalising gives

$$
\hat a = \frac{a - (a+b)/2}{|a-b|/2} = \frac{a - b}{|a - b|} = \pm 1,
$$

and $\hat b = -\hat a$. Before the learned scale and shift, then, every unit is $+1$ for one example and $-1$ for the other, whatever the two values were, unless they were almost equal, when $\epsilon$ in the denominator dominates. The layer passes on only which of the two examples was larger on each unit; all information about the magnitudes is gone. A network that has to classify from that is classifying a pair, not a digit.

### Training in small batches


That last observation matters most during training, because training always runs in training mode. A network trained in batches of 2 learns to classify from the ±1 patterns of pairs. At inference the layer uses running averages instead, a normalisation that no example ever received during training, so the network is asked to compute a function it was never trained to compute.

The second experiment trains the same architecture with and without batch normalisation at each batch size, with Adam at a learning rate of 1e-3 for 20 epochs, over seeds 0 to 2, and scores it on the full test half. For the normalised network it reports accuracy in inference mode and, in parentheses, in training mode on test batches of the same size.


### What the alternatives change


Three alternatives address the dependence in different ways. *Batch renormalisation* (Ioffe) corrects the batch statistics towards the running averages during training, so that the function trained is closer to the one used at inference; it was designed for small and non-independent batches. *Layer normalisation* and *group normalisation* (Wu and He) compute the mean and variance over the units of one example, or over groups of them, instead of over the examples of one batch. The output then never depends on the companions, and the two modes are the same function. Transformers use layer normalisation, and are free of the problem.

The practical failures of batch normalisation follow directly from the measurements:

- A model left in training mode at serving time gives answers that depend on what else is being served with them.
- A batch assembled from one class, one customer or one day normalises every member against an unrepresentative sample.
- Gradient accumulation over many tiny batches, used to fit a large model in memory, still normalises over each tiny batch, not over the accumulated one.
- A fine-tuning run in small batches can shift the running statistics away from the population the network was trained on.

Batch normalisation is one layer whose behaviour changes between training and inference; dropout, the subject of Section 9.5, is another.

## Weight Scaling Keeps Dropout's Answers, Not Its Doubts

<!-- source: ml-25-dropout; hero: ml125-hero.png -->


In Section 9.4 a prediction from a batch-normalised network depended on whether the network was in training or inference mode. Dropout is the other common layer with two modes: it is trained with random masks and evaluated without them, and the textbook explains the switch with the sentence under test: *dropout at test time is approximated by scaling the weights*. A practitioner relies on it every time a dropout network is put into evaluation mode and its outputs are used, as labels or as probabilities. The sentence does not say what is approximated, or how well. If the approximation holds for labels but not for probabilities, then a network whose accuracy is unaffected can still report confidence it has not earned, and any decision that uses the probabilities, a threshold, a ranking or an expected cost, inherits the error.

This section first defines dropout's two modes and the average over masks that weight scaling stands in for. It then compares the two rules on the UCI digits, on labels, log loss and confidence, and explains why they agree exactly for a linear unit and drift apart through nonlinearities. It repeats the comparison on the hard simulated task of Section 9.3, where the balance between labels and probabilities reverses. Finally it measures the cheapest rule of all, a single random mask, which is what a model left in training mode computes.

### Two ways to switch dropout off


During training, dropout sets each hidden unit's output to zero with probability $p$, the *dropout rate*, making a fresh random choice for every example at every step. The pattern of kept and dropped units is the *mask*. Because any unit may be missing, the network learns not to rely on any one of them.

At test time dropout is switched off, and all units are kept. Each unit then receives more input than it did on average during training, so the weights are scaled to compensate: multiplying the outgoing weights by $1 - p$ makes each unit's expected input the same in both modes. PyTorch uses the equivalent convention of dividing the kept activations by $1 - p$ during training and doing nothing at test time. Either way, this is *weight scaling*.

The original paper (Srivastava and colleagues) presents weight scaling as a cheap stand-in for something more principled. Each mask defines a *thinned network*, the original with some units removed, and a network with $n$ droppable units has $2^n$ of them. The principled prediction is the average of all their predicted probabilities. That average cannot be computed exactly, but it can be estimated by *Monte Carlo averaging*: leave dropout on at test time, run $K$ forward passes with independent random masks, and average the $K$ predicted probability vectors.

### Same labels, different probabilities


The digits network has two hidden layers of 256 units with ReLU activations and dropout at $p = 0.5$ after each. It is trained with SGD (momentum 0.9, learning rate 0.01) in minibatches of 32 for 60 epochs, over three seeds. The block evaluates it by weight scaling and by averaging $K$ masks for $K$ from 1 to 300. For each it prints accuracy, *log loss* (the mean over test rows of $-\log$ of the probability assigned to the true class) and, for the averages, the share of test digits whose label matches the weight-scaled label.

```python

d = nets.dropout_mc()          # digits, dropout 0.5, three seeds
print(f"weight scaling  accuracy {d['ws_acc']:.3f}  "
      f"log loss {d['ws_nll']:.3f}")
for k, acc, nll, same in zip(d["Ks"], d["mc_acc"], d["mc_nll"],
                             d["mc_agree"]):
    print(f"{k:3d} masks       accuracy {acc:.3f}  log loss {nll:.3f}  "
          f"same label {same:.1%}")
```

On the label, the approximation is excellent. The average of 300 masks and the weight-scaled network agree on **99.7%** of test digits, and their accuracies differ by 0.000. On log loss it is not: weight scaling's is 0.131 and the average's 0.103, a gap of 28%. Averaging just 5 masks already gives a lower log loss than weight scaling. Log loss measures the quality of the probabilities, not only of the label, so the two rules make the same decisions with differently good probabilities. ### Why scaling is only an approximation


For a single linear unit the two rules are the same thing. Write the unit's input under dropout as $\sum_j w_j m_j x_j$, where each mask entry $m_j$ is 1 with probability $1 - p$ and 0 otherwise. Its expectation over masks is

$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_j w_j m_j x_j\Big] = (1 - p) \sum_j w_j x_j,
$$

which is exactly what scaling the weights by $1 - p$ computes. For a linear function, the function of the average equals the average of the function.

The approximation enters through everything that is not linear. A ReLU of an average input is not the average of the ReLU's outputs, and a softmax of averaged logits is not the average of the softmaxes. Weight scaling in effect passes an average forward through each nonlinearity; Monte Carlo averaging passes each mask through and averages at the end. With two hidden layers the differences compound. The difference shows in the confidence, which the next block prints: the mean probability each rule assigns to its top class, and the averaged top probability on the digits where the two rules give different labels.

```python
print(f"mean top probability: weight scaling {d['ws_conf']:.3f}, "
      f"300 masks {d['mc_conf']:.3f}")
print(f"averaged top probability where the labels differ: "
      f"{d['dis_conf']:.2f}")
```

```text
mean top probability: weight scaling 0.985, 300 masks 0.954
averaged top probability where the labels differ: 0.42
```

The weight-scaled network puts a mean probability of **0.985** on its top class, the average of masks 0.954. Each thinned network is a slightly different classifier, and on hard digits they vote differently. The average carries that disagreement into its probabilities; the single weight-scaled network does not show it. The few digits on which the two rules give different labels are those on which the masks split: the average's top probability on them is 0.42, against 0.96 elsewhere.

#### Calibration points the other way

A coarser check gives a different picture. The *expected calibration error* (ECE) groups predictions into bins by their top probability, here ten bins, and averages the gap between mean confidence and accuracy within each bin, weighted by the bin's size. It is 0.014 for weight scaling and 0.021 for the average, and on average it is the average of masks that is further off: its mean top probability is 0.019 below its accuracy of 0.973, while weight scaling's is 0.012 above its accuracy of 0.973.

So the log-loss gap is not a matter of average confidence. Log loss punishes a confident mistake far more than an unsure one, since $-\log$ of a small probability is large. The likeliest source of the gap is therefore the few digits that weight scaling gets wrong with near-certainty; that was not isolated here. The table below collects the measures for both tasks.

![Accuracy barely separates the rules; log loss and confidence do, on the digits. One sampled mask is the worst of all.](ml125-f2-table.png){alt="An eight-row table: on the digits, accuracy equal and log loss lower for 300 masks; on the hard task, both nearly equal; one mask is worse than weight scaling on every measure."}

### On a hard task the balance reverses


The digits are nearly separable: most test images lie far from any decision boundary. The simulated task of Section 9.3, taken from Section 5.1, is the opposite. Its two overlapping Gaussian classes in 10 dimensions are hard enough that the Bayes rule errs on 0.276 of the 5,000 test rows used here. A large share of rows therefore sit near the boundary whatever the model. The network has two hidden layers of 128 units and is trained on 2,000 rows.

```python
g = nets.dropout_mc(task="gauss")   # episode 4's task, a hard one
print(f"weight scaling {g['ws_acc']:.3f} / {g['ws_nll']:.4f}, "
      f"300 masks {g['mc_acc'][-1]:.3f} / {g['mc_nll'][-1]:.4f}, "
      f"same label {g['mc_agree'][-1]:.1%}")
print(f"averaged top probability where the labels differ: "
      f"{g['dis_conf']:.2f}")
```

```text
weight scaling 0.708 / 0.5587, 300 masks 0.710 / 0.5554, same label 94.3%
averaged top probability where the labels differ: 0.53
```

Here the labels agree less, on 94.3% of rows, and the probabilities agree more: the log losses differ by 0.6%, and it takes 30 masks before the average beats weight scaling at all. Both observations have the same cause. The rows near the boundary are many, and on them both rules give probabilities near 0.5. A small difference in probability then moves a row across the threshold, changing its label, without changing its log loss much. The averaged network's top probability on the disputed rows is 0.53. Those rows are coin flips under either rule, and which side of 0.5 they fall on is noise. The figure below plots one rule's probability against the other's for each test row.

![The median gap between the two probabilities is 0.019 and the largest 0.19. The 4.7% of rows with different labels (seed 0) sit where both rules are close to 0.5.](ml125-f1-scatter.png){alt="A tight cloud along the diagonal; the orange points where the labels differ sit near the centre, around 0.5 on both axes."}

So the approximation is good in the sense that matters for decisions and loose in the sense that matters for probabilities, and how loose depends on the task. A sharpened probability costs most where the model is usually sure, presumably because that is where an overconfident mistake is most heavily penalised.

### A single mask is worse than both


Monte Carlo averaging needs $K$ forward passes per prediction. The tempting shortcut is one pass: leave dropout on and take one sample. It is worse than either rule on every measure here. On the digits a single mask scores 0.953 against weight scaling's 0.973, with log loss 0.200, and agrees with the weight-scaled label on only 96.8% of digits. On the hard task one mask scores 0.685 against 0.708.

That is the cost of a random network. One draw of the mask removes half the units, and the prediction inherits that draw's luck. It matters in practice because forgetting to switch a model to evaluation mode is a common bug, and a model served in training mode makes exactly these single-mask predictions, a different one each time the same input arrives.

#### Dropout as a source of uncertainty

The more principled use of sampling is Gal and Ghahramani's reading of dropout as approximate Bayesian inference, in which the spread of the $K$ sampled predictions is taken as a measure of the model's uncertainty. The measurements in this section support the narrow part of that reading: the masks do disagree, the averaged probability drops on the rows whose labels are in doubt, and the weight-scaled network does not show that drop. The spread of the $K$ samples itself was not computed here. Whether it is a *calibrated* measure of uncertainty is a separate question that this section does not answer. Chapter 10 turns from the settings of training to what a trained network has learned, beginning in Section 10.1 with a network that can fit random labels.

## In practice

### Initial weights (Section 9.1)

- **Never initialise a layer's weights to a constant.** The units stay identical through training, and the layer is one unit wide in effect.
- **Match the initialiser to the activation.** Use He initialisation (variance $2/\text{fan-in}$) for ReLU layers and Glorot initialisation for tanh and sigmoid layers. Check which initialiser your layers actually use rather than assuming.
- **Measure the activations before training.** Pass one batch through the untrained network, record the standard deviation of each layer's output and plot it against depth on a log scale. A line that rises or falls steadily means the initial scale is wrong.
- **Compute the per-layer factor.** It should be close to 1; for ReLU it should be close to $g/\sqrt{2}$, and if it is far from that, suspect that the activation or the gain is not what you think.
- **Check the gradients as well.** A level forward signal can hide exploding gradients. Record the gradient norm at the first and last layers after one backward pass.
- **Treat depth as the multiplier of any mis-scaling.** A choice that is harmless at three layers can stop a thirty-layer plain network from training.

### Learning rate and batch size (Section 9.2)

- **Treat the ratio of learning rate to batch size as the setting.** When you change the batch size, scale the learning rate by the same factor, and record the ratio alongside the rate.
- **Do not copy a tuned learning rate to a different batch size.** A rate tuned at one batch size is a ratio; moving to four times the batch at the same rate divides the effective setting by four.
- **Locate the wall before scaling up.** Run a short sweep at doubling rates and note where training fails. If the batch size you want needs a rate near or above it, the linear rule will not hold.
- **Beyond the wall, pay with epochs.** A larger batch at a capped rate takes fewer, shorter-travelling steps; budget more epochs or accept a higher loss.
- **Consider warm-up for large rates.** Goyal and colleagues raised the rate gradually over the first epochs to keep large-batch runs from diverging at the start; warm-up was not tested in this chapter.
- **Remember the rule was measured with plain SGD.** With momentum the ratio applies to the effective rate; with Adam the relation changes, as the limits below explain.

### Choosing and comparing optimisers (Section 9.3)

- **Tune every optimiser you compare by the same procedure.** Same grid width, same validation data, same budget. A comparison that tunes one and uses defaults for the other measures the tuning.
- **Say what the rate was tuned for.** A rate chosen for validation accuracy and a rate chosen for speed can differ, and the speed comparison reverses between them.
- **Compare paired runs over several splits.** Start both optimisers from the same weights on each split, report the mean difference with its standard error, and treat a difference within two standard errors of zero as not distinguishable from no difference.
- **Check the validation set's resolution.** If one validation example is worth more accuracy than the differences you are ranking, expect ties, and decide deliberately how ties are broken.
- **Value Adam for robustness, not for a better model.** Its smaller sensitivity to the learning rate is useful when tuning is expensive; it is not evidence that it finds better solutions.

### Layers with two modes (Sections 9.4 and 9.5)

- **Check the mode at serving time.** Confirm that every normalisation and dropout layer is in inference (evaluation) mode when the model serves predictions, and test that the mode is set. A batch-normalised model in training mode gives answers that depend on the rest of the batch; a dropout model in training mode makes single-mask predictions, worse than both evaluation rules and different for the same input on each call.
- **Shuffle before batching.** Make sure batches at training time are random samples. Data sorted by label, date or source give batch statistics that describe the sort, not the population.
- **Avoid batch normalisation with very small batches.** Below a batch size at which training-mode and inference-mode accuracy agree, consider layer or group normalisation, or batch renormalisation (none was tested here).
- **Compare the two modes on held-out data.** Evaluate a trained batch-normalised network in both modes, inside random batches of the training size. Close agreement is evidence that the running averages are trustworthy.
- **Do not rely on gradient accumulation to enlarge the normalisation batch.** It enlarges the gradient's batch, not the statistics'.
- **Watch the running statistics when fine-tuning.** Freeze them, or check them against the original data, when fine-tuning in small or unrepresentative batches.
- **Use weight scaling for labels.** If only the predicted class matters, evaluation mode with weight scaling gives almost exactly the labels of averaging many masks, at the cost of one forward pass.
- **Do not trust weight-scaled probabilities without checking.** Measure log loss and mean top-class probability against a Monte Carlo average on held-out data; on the digits, five masks already improved log loss.
- **Average masks when probabilities drive decisions.** If thresholds, rankings or expected costs use the probabilities, consider averaging a modest number of masks, or recalibrating on held-out data (Section 11.3 measures recalibration).
- **Look at where the masks disagree.** Here the rows on which the two rules disputed the label were rows where the averaged probability was close to a coin flip; that drop in averaged probability is information weight scaling discards. Whether the spread of the masks is a calibrated uncertainty was not tested.

## Limits of the evidence

### Initialisation (Section 9.1)

- **Plain stacks of layers.** Residual connections, which add each block's input to its output, and normalisation layers, which rescale activations at every layer (Section 9.4), both make a network far less sensitive to its initial scale. Most modern deep networks have both, which is why this failure is rarer in practice than the arithmetic suggests.
- **One seed and four learning rates.** The verdicts are large enough to survive both limits: the four failing networks score at most 0.132 at any rate tried, against chance at 0.10. The differences among the networks that do train are not established here.
- **Thirty epochs.** A network whose gradients are of order 1e-5 might eventually begin to learn with a much larger learning rate or many more epochs. Neither was tried, and a much larger rate would risk overflow in the layers whose activations are not small.
- **The theory describes the start.** The $g/\sqrt{2}$ factor describes the network at initialisation. Training changes the weights, and the scales with them, which is why the ReLU network at $g = \sqrt{2}$ can train at all: the initialisation only has to give it a place to start.

### Learning rate and batch size (Section 9.2)

- **Plain SGD only.** With momentum, the effective step is the learning rate divided by one minus the momentum, and the same ratio argument applies to that effective rate. With Adam, whose step size does not scale with the gradient's magnitude, the relation is closer to a square root and depends on the noise.
- **Training loss, not test accuracy.** The question was optimisation. Test accuracy followed the training loss closely here; on larger problems it can prefer the noisier, smaller-batch runs at equal training loss, a separate question with a contested answer.
- **One network and one budget.** The position of the wall depends on the network's curvature, and the critical batch size depends on the task's gradient noise, so neither number transfers to other problems. What transfers is the shape: a ratio that sets the run, and a wall that does not move with the batch.
- **A coarse grid.** Rates double, so "best" and "fails" are each known only to within a factor of two.

### Optimisers (Section 9.3)

- **Small networks, small data.** Two hidden layers on 900 digits or 2,000 simulated rows. On large language models Adam (or AdamW) is the near-universal choice, and it is common in vision, partly because SGD is hard to tune at that scale and partly because adaptive scaling helps with very different gradient scales across layers, such as embeddings.
- **A coarse grid.** Five rates per optimiser, spaced by about a factor of three. Both "fastest" and "tuned" are known only to that resolution, and validation accuracy on a fifth of one split (180 digits) has ties: Adam's 3e-4 and 1e-3 scored the same, and the smaller was taken.
- **One schedule.** Constant learning rates, no warm-up, no decay. Schedules change both speed and final accuracy, for both optimisers.
- **No weight decay.** Adam's interaction with weight decay is its own subject (AdamW exists because of it), and was left out by giving neither optimiser any.

### Batch normalisation (Section 9.4)

- **Fully connected layers on small images.** In convolutional networks the statistics are pooled over all spatial positions as well as over the batch, so a batch of 2 images contributes many values per channel, and the dependence is likely weaker than here; that was not measured.
- **Frozen running statistics in the first study.** Their momentum was set to zero so that the evaluation did not change the network. In a real training-mode deployment the running averages would also drift with every batch.
- **One architecture, one data set.** The batch size at which normalisation turns from hurting to helping, between 4 and 16 here, depends on the network and on how much the batch statistics vary, and is not a general constant.
- **Adam at one learning rate.** The batch-size study holds the learning rate at 1e-3 for every batch size. Section 9.2 showed, for plain SGD, that the best rate moves with the batch, and Adam's rate is unlikely to be immune, so the plain network's flat line across batch sizes is partly a matter of where 1e-3 happens to sit.

### Dropout (Section 9.5)

- **One dropout rate.** At $p = 0.5$ each mask removes half the units, the largest perturbation in common use. At $p = 0.1$ the thinned networks are much closer to each other and to the scaled one, and both gaps should shrink; that was not measured here.
- **Small fully connected networks.** Two hidden layers; the gap compounds with depth, so deeper networks could differ more. Convolutional networks usually apply dropout only near the output, which limits how much nonlinearity sits between the masks and the prediction.
- **300 masks is an estimate.** The average of 300 masks is not the exact average over all of them. The curves are flat by 100 masks, which suggests the remaining error is small.
- **Calibration was not fixed.** A temperature fitted on held-out data could bring the weight-scaled network's log loss down without any sampling. The comparison here is between the two rules as they come, which is how they are used.

## Key points

1. Symmetry must be broken: with every weight equal, the 128 units of a layer were still identical after training. But in a deep network breaking symmetry is not enough. Each ReLU layer multiplies the signal's scale by about $g/\sqrt{2}$, and over thirty layers that factor decided whether training happened at all: 0.132 at $g = 1$, 0.919 at $g = \sqrt{2}$ and 0.099 at $g = 2$.
2. Tanh saturates, so its forward signal can stay level while its gradients explode; the scale must be right in both directions. With three layers every random scale trained above 0.9: depth turns a modest mis-scaling into a failure.
3. With plain SGD and a fixed number of examples, learning rate and batch size act through their ratio. Away from divergence, runs with the same ratio reached the same training loss to within 2.3%, from batches of 8 to 512, and the best rate grew fourfold from a batch of 8 to a batch of 32.
4. For large batches, training failed at a learning rate that did not move with the batch, 3.072 for both 128 and 512. Beyond that wall a larger batch means less training, not faster training.
5. Tuned on validation data and run over twenty splits, Adam and SGD with momentum reached the same test accuracy: +0.0009 ± 0.0007 on the digits and +0.0005 ± 0.0007 on a hard simulated task.
6. Speed belongs to the learning rate. At the rates validation chose, SGD's loss fell faster; at each optimiser's fastest rate, Adam reached a loss of 0.01 in 6 epochs and SGD in 8. Validation on 180 digits could not separate fast rates from slow ones, and its tie-break chose the slower. Adam's accuracy varied less across its grid than SGD's.
7. Batch normalisation normalises with the batch's statistics. In training mode one digit's label changed with its random companions in 45% of batches of 2 and 0.6% of batches of 64, and in 23% of batches of 16 whose companions came from one class, against 3.2% with random ones.
8. In a batch of 2 every normalised unit is ±1 before the learned scale and shift. Trained that way, the network scored 0.863 in inference mode, against 0.972 without normalisation. From batches of 16 up, normalisation scored slightly higher, by margins three seeds do not establish.
9. Weight scaling reproduces the labels of dropout's mask average closely (99.7% agreement on the digits, 94.3% on the hard task) but not its probabilities: on the digits it was sharper and its log loss 28% higher, because nonlinearities do not commute with averaging.
10. A model left in training mode serves predictions that depend on something other than its input: its batch companions under batch normalisation, a single random mask under dropout. One mask was worse than both evaluation rules on accuracy and log loss.

2. Explain why a constant initialisation fails at three layers as well as thirty, while a mis-scaled random initialisation fails only at thirty.
3. Take a training script and find its best learning rate at your usual batch size. Halve the batch size and run it at the old rate and at half the old rate, for the same number of epochs. If the halved rate matches the original run and the old rate does not, your setting was the ratio. Then double the batch size instead, at twice the rate, and see whether it trains or diverges. If it diverges, you have located your wall.
4. Explain why a limit set by curvature would be the same learning rate for every batch size, while a limit set by gradient noise would scale with the batch. Using the arithmetic argument of Section 9.2, explain why runs with the same ratio part company as the learning rate approaches the point of failure.
5. Take a model you train with Adam at its default learning rate. Train it with SGD with momentum 0.9 at five rates spaced by a factor of three, and with Adam at five rates around the default, all for the same number of epochs from the same initial weights. Plot validation accuracy against rate for both. If the best SGD point is within noise of the best Adam point, your choice of optimiser is a choice about tuning effort, not about the model you end up with.
6. Work out how much accuracy one example is worth on your own validation set, and compare it with the differences between your candidate learning rates. Explain how ties among those rates would be broken by your tuning code.
7. Take a trained network with batch normalisation and pick 50 test examples. 8. Show that in a batch of 2 the normalised values are $+1$ and $-1$ whatever the two inputs. What happens if the two inputs are equal, and what role does $\epsilon$ play? Then explain why layer normalisation computes the same function in training and inference.
9. Take a network you trained with dropout and evaluate it on a held-out set three ways: in evaluation mode, with dropout on and one pass, and with dropout on averaged over 30 passes.
