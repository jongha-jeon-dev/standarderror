# A Prediction Interval Is Right on Average and Wrong Where It Matters

A regression's prediction is a number, and anyone acting on it needs to know how far off it might be: a delivery estimate, a price, a dose. The standard answer is a band of fixed width around every prediction, plus or minus 1.96 times the standard deviation of the residuals, read as the range that will contain the outcome 95% of the time. The sentence under test is that reading: *a prediction plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covers 95% of new outcomes*. If it fails, the band misleads, and it misleads most where the outcomes are largest and least predictable.

Most of this book has been about classifiers; this chapter is about regression. It checks whether the textbook band and its distribution-free replacement, split conformal prediction, deliver 95% overall, then slices the bands by input to show where a fixed width fails, tests a repair whose width varies, and repeats the comparison on a small real data set.

## The test: a regression whose noise grows

The *coverage* of an interval method is the proportion of new outcomes that fall inside their intervals. It is compared with the *nominal level*, the 95% claimed.

The test uses a regression where the truth is known: $y = \sin x + x/2 + \varepsilon$, with $x$ spread evenly from 0 to 10 and the noise $\varepsilon$ having a standard deviation that rises from 0.2 at the left to 3.2 at the right. Noise whose spread changes with the inputs is called *heteroscedastic*. It is common, since larger quantities usually vary more: a large claim more than a small one.

A gradient-boosted model fits the curve on 2,000 rows. Each band is checked on 20,000 fresh rows, overall and in each tenth of the input's range, and everything is averaged over 20 data sets. The code below builds the split conformal band, defined in the next section, on one data set and prints its coverage overall and at the two ends of the range.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from standarderror.ml import deploy

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X, y = deploy._hetero(2000, rng)        # noise sd 0.2 + 0.3 x, x in [0, 10]
Xc, yc = deploy._hetero(1000, rng)      # calibration rows
Xt, yt = deploy._hetero(20000, rng)     # fresh rows
model = GradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(X, y)
q = np.quantile(np.abs(yc - model.predict(Xc)), 0.95 * (1 + 1 / 1000))
inside = np.abs(yt - model.predict(Xt)) <= q   # split conformal
print(f"overall {inside.mean():.3f}; x < 1: {inside[Xt[:, 0] < 1].mean():.3f}"
      f"; x > 9: {inside[Xt[:, 0] > 9].mean():.3f}")
```

```text
overall 0.969; x < 1: 1.000; x > 9: 0.864
```

On this one data set the band covered every outcome with $x$ below 1 and 86.4% of those above 9. The figures below are averages over all 20 data sets.

## Overall coverage: the conformal band keeps its promise

### Why the textbook band falls short

Built from the training residuals, the band covered 90.8% of new outcomes. The model had fitted some of the training noise, so its residuals on the training rows were smaller than its errors on new rows, and a band sized from them was too narrow.

Built from residuals on 1,000 held-out rows, it covered 93.3%. The remaining gap has a different cause. The rule "1.96 standard deviations covers 95%" is a property of the normal distribution. The residuals here are a mixture of narrow noise from the left of the range and wide noise from the right, and the 95% point of such a mixture is not 1.96 of its standard deviations. The standard deviation is the wrong summary of a distribution whose spread varies.

### Split conformal prediction

*Split conformal prediction*, set out by Vovk and colleagues and developed for regression by Lei and colleagues, makes one change. Fit the model on one part of the data. On a separate *calibration set* of $n$ rows, compute the absolute residuals $|y_i - \hat f(x_i)|$. Instead of their standard deviation, take their empirical quantile at level

$$
(1 - \alpha)\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right),
$$

call it $\hat q$, and report $\hat f(x) \pm \hat q$ for every new $x$. The factor $1 + 1/n$ is a small finite-sample correction; it appears in the code as `0.95 * (1 + 1 / 1000)`.

The method has a guarantee that needs no model to be right. Suppose the new row and the calibration rows are *exchangeable*: their joint distribution is unchanged by reordering them, as holds when all are independent draws from one distribution. Then the new row's residual is equally likely to fall at any rank among the calibration residuals, so it exceeds the quantile chosen above with probability at most $\alpha$. The band covers at least $1 - \alpha$ of new outcomes on average, whatever the model and whatever the noise.

It delivered: 95.1%, with a standard deviation of 0.008 across the 20 data sets, about what a 1,000-row calibration set allows. On the question the sentence asks, the conformal band is the honest version of the textbook one. The table below collects the overall and worst-tenth coverage and the widths of all the bands.

## Coverage by input: a fixed width fails where the noise is

### Marginal and conditional coverage

The conformal guarantee is about *marginal coverage*, the average over all inputs. What a user acting on one prediction usually wants is *conditional coverage*: the probability of covering the outcome given this particular $x$. A band of one width around noise of varying width must be too wide where the noise is small and too narrow where it is large, and the average hides both.

In the quietest tenth of inputs every fixed-width band covered more than 99% of outcomes, wider than it needed to be. In the noisiest tenth the training-residual band covered 70.5%, the held-out band 76.3%, a straight line with a held-out band 80.2%, and split conformal **80.9%**. One outcome in five fell outside a "95%" interval exactly where the outcomes were largest and least predictable, which in many applications is where the stakes are.

The straight line, which cannot follow the curve, had larger residuals everywhere and so a wider band, 7.91 against 7.72, and with the same recipe covered the noisiest tenth better than boosting (80.2% against 76.3%). The worse model looked safer there only because it was less sure of itself everywhere.

This is a limit of the problem. Barber, Candès, Ramdas and Tibshirani proved in 2021 that no method can guarantee conditional coverage at every input, with bands of useful width, without assumptions about the distribution. Any repair has to bring an assumption.

### Conformalised quantile regression

*Conformalised quantile regression* (CQR), from Romano, Patterson and Candès in 2019, brings the assumption that the noise's spread can be learned. *Quantile regression* fits a model for a chosen quantile of $y$ given $x$ instead of its mean, by minimising the *pinball loss*, which penalises under-predictions with weight $\tau$ and over-predictions with weight $1 - \tau$ for quantile level $\tau$. Two boosted models estimate the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles, $\hat q_{\text{lo}}(x)$ and $\hat q_{\text{hi}}(x)$, so the band widens where the data are noisier.

A conformal step then supplies the guarantee. On the calibration rows, compute how far each outcome falls outside its band,

$$
E_i = \max\{\hat q_{\text{lo}}(x_i) - y_i,\; y_i - \hat q_{\text{hi}}(x_i)\},
$$

which is negative when the outcome is inside. Take the conformal quantile $\hat Q$ of these scores and report $[\hat q_{\text{lo}}(x) - \hat Q,\; \hat q_{\text{hi}}(x) + \hat Q]$. The band is widened or narrowed by however much it misses.

CQR covered 95.0% overall and between 93.2% and 96.0% in every tenth, with an average width of 6.76 against split conformal's 8.54: 21% narrower and right almost everywhere, because the width went where the noise was. The figure below shows coverage by tenth for the fixed-width bands and for CQR.

## The repair needs data: diabetes progression

The replication uses the diabetes data bundled with scikit-learn: 442 patients, ten baseline variables, and disease progression after a year as the outcome. The level is 90%, and the patients are split into 250 to fit, 100 to calibrate and 92 to check, over 100 random splits. Without a single input to slice by, the code slices by the predicted value, reporting coverage in the thirds of patients with the lowest, middle and highest predictions.

```python
d = deploy.intervals_diabetes(reps=100)
for k in ("split conformal", "CQR"):
    v = d[k]
    print(f"{k:15s} {v['coverage']:.3f} overall; thirds by prediction "
          f"{v['low']:.3f} {v['middle']:.3f} {v['high']:.3f}")
```

```text
split conformal 0.909 overall; thirds by prediction 0.972 0.892 0.863
CQR             0.904 overall; thirds by prediction 0.944 0.916 0.852
```

Split conformal around a linear model covered 90.9% overall, on target. By thirds it covered 97.2% of the patients predicted to progress least and 86.3% of those predicted to progress most: the same pattern as the simulation, presumably because progression varies more among the patients with more of it.

CQR, with shallow boosted trees on 250 patients, covered 94.4% and 85.2% in the outer thirds, no better, with a band 11% wider. Learning how the spread varies takes data, and 250 patients with ten variables were not enough to learn it better than a constant. The repair that worked on 2,000 rows did not transfer to 250.

The next chapter slices by group instead, asking what calibration in every group implies about a model's errors.

## In practice

- **Replace $\pm 1.96$ training-residual standard deviations with split conformal on held-out rows.** It costs one quantile and delivers nominal marginal coverage under exchangeability.
- **Report coverage in slices, not only overall.** Compute it by bins of the predicted value and of the inputs you suspect drive the noise. A band that meets its level overall can miss badly in the slice where outcomes are largest.
- **Let the width vary only where there are data to learn how.** CQR fixed the slices on 2,000 rows and did not on 250. Check coverage by slice on held-out data before trusting it.
- **Treat over-coverage as a cost**, and do not prefer a worse model because its wider band looks safer in one slice.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One shape of noise.** The simulation's noise grows smoothly with one input. Real heteroscedasticity can depend on several inputs or on unmeasured ones, which no model of the spread can follow.
- **Exchangeability.** Every conformal guarantee assumes the new rows are like the calibration rows. Under the drift of Chapter 61 the guarantee lapses, even on average.
- **Thirds of the predictions.** Coverage by predicted value is one way to slice. Slices by any variable can show different gaps, and with enough slices some will miss by chance.
- **One quantile model.** Other ways to let the band vary, such as dividing residuals by a fitted spread, behave similarly in kind. They all need the spread to be learnable.

## Key points

1. A band of plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covered 90.8% with training residuals and 93.3% with held-out ones; split conformal delivered its promised 95.1%.
2. Conformal coverage is marginal. In the noisiest tenth of inputs every fixed-width band covered 70% to 81%, and in the quietest more than 99%.
3. No method can guarantee coverage at every input without assumptions; conformalised quantile regression assumes the spread is learnable.
4. CQR covered 93.2% to 96.0% in every tenth with a 21% narrower band on 2,000 rows; on 250 diabetes patients it did no better than a fixed band.
5. Check an interval's coverage in slices that matter, not only overall. Use held-out or conformal calibration, and let the width vary only where there are enough data to learn how.
