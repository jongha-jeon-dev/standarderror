# Causes and Feedback

Chapter 12 followed predictions into decisions that act on a list: which cases to flag, which to clear, where to set the threshold. This chapter follows them one step further, into decisions that act on the world. Someone reads a model's output and changes something: a habit is encouraged, a treatment prescribed, an offer made, an inspector sent. A practitioner meets this whenever a model built to predict is handed to people who set policy, run campaigns or allocate scarce effort, and are asked to use it to decide what to do. The model may score well on every predictive metric and still give the wrong answer, because the question being asked of it is no longer the question it was trained to answer.

A predictive model learns how the outcome varies with the features in the data as they were collected. A decision asks something else: what would happen to the outcome if something were changed, for whom the change would make the most difference, and what the record will look like after the decisions have been made. Each section of this chapter takes one of these questions, sets up a small simulated system in which the true answer is known, and compares it with what the predictive model says. Each tests one sentence:

- Section 13.1: *the features a model relies on most are the levers that move the outcome*;
- Section 13.2: *give the intervention to the people the model says are most at risk*;
- Section 13.3: *a model retrained on the data it collects corrects itself over time*.

The sections build on one another. Section 13.1 asks what to change. It finds that the features a model leans on hardest can be consequences of the outcome or fellow travellers of it, that the real lever can be nearly hidden, and that the lever's apparent effect depends on which other features are in the model. Recovering the true effect needs knowledge of the causal structure, or an experiment. Section 13.2 takes the lever as known and tested, and asks whom to apply it to under a budget. Ranking by predicted risk turns out to target people the intervention barely helps, and the better ranking, by the predicted difference the intervention makes, is available only because a randomised trial supplied both arms. Section 13.3 lets the decisions repeat. When the next model is trained on outcomes recorded where the last one chose to look, the record can lock in to an answer fixed by early luck, and the repairs are to estimate rates per unit of effort and to keep a share of decisions independent of the model.

A common thread runs through the three. In each case the data available to the model were shaped by the world or by earlier decisions in a way the model cannot see from the inside, and the repair needs information from outside the model: an assumed causal structure, a randomised comparison, or decisions deliberately made without reference to the record. The last section ends by drawing together what Chapter 12 and this chapter measured. Part V returns to the theme from the side of the data, in Chapter 15, where outcomes exist only for the cases an earlier decision selected.

## What a Model Relies On Is Not What to Change

<!-- source: ml-48-intervention; hero: ml148-hero.png -->


Once a model predicts well, the next request is usually to explain which features matter, and the explanation is then read as advice about what to change. The model is queried through permutation importance, partial dependence or a what-if tool that moves one input and watches the prediction, and the features at the top of the list are treated as levers. The sentence under test is: *the features a model relies on most are the levers that move the outcome*. A practitioner meets it whenever a feature-importance chart is presented to people who set policy, design interventions or allocate budgets. If it is false, resources go into changing variables that have no effect, and the variable that does have one can go unnoticed.

Sections 12.1 and 12.2 acted on a model's ranking: flag these cases, clear those. This chapter turns to decisions that act on the world, and this section begins with what to change. Sections 14.2, 14.3 and 14.4, in Part IV, show that importance, partial dependence and SHAP values describe the model rather than the data; this section asks whether they describe the world. It sets up a small simulated system whose causal structure is known, compares what two models say about each feature with what changing that feature actually does, identifies three distinct ways the models' answers go wrong, and shows what recovers the true effect from the same data.

### A system whose causes are known


Answering the question requires a world in which the effect of changing each variable is known, so this section builds one. An unmeasured trait $U$ raises the risk of an outcome. $U$ also lowers a habit, $x_1$, which has no effect of its own on the outcome. $U$ raises the level of a treatment, $x_2$, and the treatment genuinely raises the risk. A test result, $x_3$, is produced by the outcome's own underlying state, in the way a symptom or a laboratory value follows a condition. $x_4$ is pure noise. With $e$ denoting independent standard-normal noise terms and $\varepsilon$ logistic noise, the system is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1 &= -0.8\,U + 0.6\,e, \qquad x_2 = 0.6\,U + 0.8\,e,\\
\text{latent} &= 1.2\,U + 0.5\,x_2 + \varepsilon, \qquad \text{outcome} = [\text{latent} > 0],\\
x_3 &= \text{latent} + 0.8\,e.
\end{aligned}
$$

In causal language, $U$ is a *confounder* or *common cause*: a variable that influences both a feature and the outcome, and so creates an association between them that is not an effect of one on the other. The test result is *downstream* of the outcome: it is caused by it, not a cause of it.

A boosted-tree model and a logistic regression are trained on 20,000 rows of the four observed features; $U$ is not among them. Three numbers are reported for each feature. The first is *permutation importance* (Section 14.2): how much the model's AUC falls when that feature's values are shuffled across rows, a measure of how much the model relies on it. The second is a *what-if*: the change in the model's mean predicted risk when the feature is raised by one standard deviation in the model's input, everything else in the input held fixed. The third is the *true effect*: the change in the outcome's probability when the feature is raised by one standard deviation in the simulated system itself, with everything that depends on it allowed to respond.

```python
from standarderror.ml import decide

r = decide.importance_vs_effect()   # 20,000 simulated rows
m = r["models"]["boosted trees"]
for n in r["names"]:
    print(f"{n:15s} importance {m['importance'][n]:.4f}, "
          f"what-if {m['whatif'][n]:+.3f}, true effect {r['true'][n]:+.3f}")
```

```text
x1 habit        importance 0.0022, what-if -0.029, true effect +0.000
x2 treatment    importance 0.0021, what-if +0.024, true effect +0.085
x3 test result  importance 0.4489, what-if +0.314, true effect +0.000
x4 noise        importance -0.0001, what-if -0.003, true effect +0.000
```

The test result carries almost all of the model's reliance: shuffling it costs 0.45 of AUC, and raising it in the input raises the predicted risk by 0.31. In the system, raising the test result changes nothing, because it is a consequence of the outcome, not a cause. The figure below sets the models' what-ifs beside the true effects.

![The test result moves the model's prediction by 0.31 and the outcome by nothing; the treatment moves the outcome by 0.085 and the model by 0.024.](ml148-f0-effects.png){alt="Grouped bars. For the test result the models predict a rise of about 0.31 and the system shows none. For the habit the models predict a small fall and the system none. For the treatment the models predict about 0.025 and the system 0.085."}

### Three ways a predictive answer misleads


Each of the three informative features misleads in a different way, and each is one of the standard patterns of causal reasoning.

#### A consequence of the outcome

The test result is the best predictor because it is partly a measurement of the outcome. Intervening on it is like treating the thermometer to cure a fever: the reading changes and the condition does not. A model that predicts well will typically lean on such variables when they are available, and as a predictor it should. The error lies only in reading its reliance as a recommendation.

#### A fellow traveller

The habit is associated with lower risk only because the unmeasured trait drives both: people with high $U$ have less of the habit and more of the outcome. Given the habit, the model can infer something about $U$, and so, correctly for prediction, it treats the habit as evidence of lower risk: $-0.029$ per standard deviation. Encouraging the habit would not move the outcome at all, because it would not change anyone's $U$. Such a variable is sometimes called a *fellow traveller* of the outcome.

#### The real lever, nearly hidden

The treatment is the one variable whose change would move the outcome, and the model nearly hides it. Its importance is 0.0021, indistinguishable from the habit's, and the model's what-if puts its effect at $+0.024$, under a third of the true $+0.085$. A likely reason is that the test result already records most of what the treatment did to the outcome's underlying state. Once the model knows the test result, the treatment adds little predictive information, so the model has little reason to use it. Conditioning on a variable downstream of the outcome absorbs part of the effect one is trying to see. The table below gives the full comparison, including two logistic regressions built with the causal structure in mind.

![Importance, what-if and true effect for each feature. The last two rows are logistic regressions chosen with the causal structure in mind: one given the common cause, one simply without the downstream test.](ml148-f1-table.png){alt="A six-row table: the test result has by far the largest importance and what-if and no true effect; the treatment has small importance, a small what-if and the only true effect."}

### What recovers the lever


The treatment's effect is recoverable from the same kind of data, by a model built for that question rather than for prediction. The next block prints the treatment's what-if from five specifications: the treatment alone, a logistic regression on all four observed features, a logistic regression given the treatment and the common cause $U$, a logistic regression on the observed features without the test result, and the true value.

```python
print(f"treatment alone, no other features:   {r['alone_x2']:+.3f}")
print(f"treatment, logistic on all four:      "
      f"{r['models']['logistic']['whatif']['x2 treatment']:+.3f}")
print(f"treatment, given the common cause:    {r['adjusted_x2']:+.3f}")
print(f"treatment, without the test result:   {r['without_x3_x2']:+.3f}")
print(f"treatment, true:                      "
      f"{r['true']['x2 treatment']:+.3f}")
```

```text
treatment alone, no other features:   +0.202
treatment, logistic on all four:      +0.026
treatment, given the common cause:    +0.089
treatment, without the test result:   +0.144
treatment, true:                      +0.085
```

The logistic regression on all four observed features, $+0.026$, agrees with the boosted trees: the problem is not the flexibility of the model but the choice of features. Given the common cause and the treatment only, a logistic regression's what-if is $+0.089$, within 0.005 of the truth. Adjusting for the confounder and leaving out the variable downstream of the outcome is the textbook recipe, and here it works.

The naive alternative of looking at the treatment on its own is far worse. A logistic regression on the treatment alone puts its effect at $+0.202$, 2.4 times the truth, because people with more of the treatment also carry more of the unmeasured trait that raises the risk; the regression credits the treatment with the trait's effect. This is *confounding bias*. Simply dropping the test result is not enough either. The habit is then the only stand-in for $U$, and only a partial one, so some confounding remains, and the treatment's apparent effect, $+0.144$, overstates the truth by 70%. The same data gave four answers for one lever, from under a third of the truth to more than twice it, depending only on which other features were in the model.

#### What the recipe needed

The recipe needed two things no predictive pipeline supplies: a measurement of the common cause, and the knowledge of which variables come before the outcome and which after. In the language of causal graphs, a *back-door path* is a route of association from the treatment to the outcome that starts with an arrow into the treatment, here through $U$. Zhao and Hastie made the conditions precise for black-box models: a partial dependence plot can be read causally when the features held fixed block every back-door path and include nothing downstream of the feature being varied, as the test result is downstream of the treatment, through the outcome. Those conditions are about the world, and no inspection of the model can check them.

#### The experiment behind the true effect

The column labelled true effect is itself what an experiment would report. It was computed by raising each feature by one standard deviation for 200,000 simulated people, everything else left to follow from the change, and comparing their outcomes with an untouched population: a randomised trial with perfect compliance, run in software. When the causal structure is unknown, running such a trial for real, on the variable someone actually proposes to change, is the one measurement that needs no assumption about which features to include. That is why the uplift models of Section 13.2 below, which ask not whether to intervene but on whom, start from a randomised trial.

## Target the Effect, Not the Risk

<!-- source: ml-49-uplift; hero: ml149-hero.png -->


Section 13.1 ended with the randomised trial as the one measurement of a lever's effect that needs no assumption about which features to include. Suppose, then, that the lever is known and has been tested. An intervention is available but cannot be given to everyone, someone has to decide who receives it, and the model asked to decide is usually a risk model: who is most likely to churn, default or be readmitted. The policy that follows looks obvious, and it is the sentence this section tests: *give the intervention to the people the model says are most at risk*. Practitioners meet it in retention campaigns, collections, care management and fraud review, wherever a budget limits how many people can be contacted. If it is wrong, the budget is spent on people the intervention does not help, and possibly on people it harms, while the risk model itself scores well on every standard metric.

Section 13.1 found that the features a model leans on need not be levers. Here the lever is given, a retention offer, and the only question is whom to apply it to. This section sets up a simulated customer base with a randomised trial of the offer, compares targeting by predicted risk with targeting at random and with targeting by the predicted effect of the offer, explains why risk is the wrong ranking here, and measures how the answer changes with the size of the budget.

### The value of an intervention is a difference


For a customer with features $x$, the offer's value is the reduction it makes in that customer's probability of churning:

$$
\tau(x) = P(\text{churn} \mid x, \text{no offer}) - P(\text{churn} \mid x, \text{offer}).
$$

This quantity is called the *uplift*, or in causal-inference terms the *conditional average treatment effect*. A risk model estimates only the first term. Ranking by risk targets the people with the largest uplift only if the offer works best for the people most likely to leave, which is a fact about the intervention, not about the model.

Radcliffe and Surry named four kinds of customer by how the offer acts on them. *Persuadables* stay if they receive the offer and leave otherwise. *Sure things* stay either way. *Lost causes* leave either way. *Do-not-disturbs*, also called *sleeping dogs*, are made more likely to leave by being contacted. Only the persuadables are worth the budget, and the do-not-disturbs cost more than nothing.

### A simulated retention offer


The simulation has five standard-normal features. Without the offer, 34.5% of customers churn, and the log-odds of churning are $-1 + 1.5\,x_1 + x_2$, so risk rises with the first two features. The offer adds

$$
-1.6\,\exp(-x_1^2)
$$

to the log-odds, plus $0.8$ for customers with $x_3 > 1$. The first term is largest in magnitude when $x_1$ is near zero, that is, roughly for customers of middling risk: the offer does little for those almost certain to stay, who will stay anyway, and little for those almost certain to leave, whom one offer will not keep. For customers with a high value of the third feature whose risk is far from the middle, the positive term dominates and the offer backfires; they are about one customer in fifteen.

A randomised trial offers the deal to half of either 2,000 or 20,000 customers, chosen at random. From the trial, models choose which 20% of a new population of 100,000 to offer it to, and the churn prevented by each choice is computed from the simulation's true probabilities. Four kinds of targeting rule are compared:

- **random**: a random 20%;
- **true effect**: the 20% with the largest true uplift, the best possible;
- **highest risk**: the 20% with the highest predicted churn, from a model fitted to the trial's untreated arm;
- **largest effect**: the 20% with the largest predicted uplift, described below.

The last two rules are each run with boosted trees and with logistic regression.

```python
from standarderror.ml import decide

u = decide.uplift_targeting()   # budget: 20% of customers
for row in u["rows"]:
    print(f"trial of {row['n']:,} customers:")
    for k, v in row.items():
        if k not in ("n", "sd"):
            print(f"   {k:26s} {v:5.1f} churns prevented per 1,000")
```

```text
trial of 2,000 customers:
   random                      21.9 churns prevented per 1,000
   true effect                 55.5 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (boosted)      22.0 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (boosted)    39.0 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (logistic)     20.3 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (logistic)   46.5 churns prevented per 1,000
trial of 20,000 customers:
   random                      21.9 churns prevented per 1,000
   true effect                 55.5 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (boosted)      21.4 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (boosted)    47.9 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (logistic)     20.1 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (logistic)   50.4 churns prevented per 1,000
```

Offering the deal to the fifth with the highest predicted churn prevented between 20.1 and 22.0 churns per 1,000 customers. Choosing the fifth at random prevented 21.9. With the logistic risk model and the large trial, the risk-targeted offer did slightly worse than random. Boosted and logistic risk models did about equally badly, which points away from the quality of the risk model: it estimates the first term of the uplift, and that term is the wrong thing to rank by here. The figure below compares the rules for the large trial.

![Targeting the highest predicted risk prevented 21.4 per 1,000; random targeting 21.9; the predicted effect 50.4.](ml149-f0-targeting.png){alt="Bars: random and highest-risk targeting both prevent about 21 churns per 1,000; targeting by predicted effect prevents 39 to 50; the true best is 55."}

### Why risk is the wrong ranking here


The next block measures who is in the highest-risk fifth: the share of customers the offer makes more likely to churn, overall and in that fifth, and the mean reduction in churn probability in each group.

```python
print("made more likely to churn by the offer:")
print(f"   {u['harmed_share']:.1%} of all customers, "
      f"{u['top_risk_harmed']:.1%} of the highest-risk fifth")
print(f"mean reduction in churn probability: all customers "
      f"{u['mean_gain']:.3f}, highest-risk fifth {u['top_risk_mean_gain']:.3f}")
```

```text
made more likely to churn by the offer:
   6.4% of all customers, 11.2% of the highest-risk fifth
mean reduction in churn probability: all customers 0.109, highest-risk fifth 0.100
```

The highest-risk fifth holds more of the customers the offer harms. On average the offer reduced their churn probability by 0.100, less than its average over all customers, 0.109, and 11.2% of them were made more likely to leave, against 6.4% overall. Ranking by risk spends the budget on customers who are moved slightly less than average and harmed more often. The highest-risk customers are disproportionately lost causes and do-not-disturbs; the persuadables sit further down the risk scale. The table below gives the results with their standard deviations across trials.

'Effect' ranks by the difference between models of the untreated and treated arms.](ml149-f1-table.png){alt="A six-row table of churns prevented per 1,000 customers, for two trial sizes, with standard deviations."}

### Ranking by the estimated effect


The randomised trial makes a better ranking possible. Fit one model to the customers who were offered the deal and another to those who were not, and rank new customers by the difference between their two predictions. Künzel and colleagues call this the *T-learner* ("T" for two models), the simplest of several *meta-learners*, general recipes that turn any supervised learner into an estimator of heterogeneous treatment effects. Because assignment in the trial was random, the two arms differ systematically only in the offer, so the difference between the arms' models estimates the offer's effect rather than the reasons people were offered it.

The T-learner prevented 50.4 churns per 1,000 with logistic models and the large trial, more than twice the risk ranking, against 55.5 for ranking by the true effect.

#### Estimating a difference is harder than estimating a level

Each arm's model has its own estimation error, and the errors of two separate fits add up in their difference. Since the uplift is often much smaller than the churn probabilities themselves, the noise can be large relative to the signal. With the smaller trial, the effect ranking fell to 46.5 with logistic models, varying from trial to trial with a standard deviation of 3.7, and to 39.0 with boosted trees.

The logistic T-learner beat the boosted one at both trial sizes (50.4 against 47.9 with the large trial), even though the true effect is not linear in the features. A plausible reason, not isolated here, is that two simpler models leave less noise in their difference than two flexible ones. Neither came closer than 5.1 churns per 1,000 to ranking by the true effect. Methods that model the difference directly rather than as the gap between two fits, such as Athey and Imbens's causal trees, exist for this reason.

The whole approach rests on the trial. Without random assignment, the difference between offered and not-offered customers would mix the offer's effect with the reasons they were offered it. That combines two problems: the confounding of a lever with the causes of its use, met in Section 13.1, and outcomes that exist only for those an earlier decision selected, which Section 15.2 studies in a simulated lender.

### How the budget changes the answer


All of this was at one budget, a fifth of the customers. The ranking matters most when the budget is small and stops mattering when the budget covers everyone. The same comparison was run at five budgets, with logistic models and the large trial.

```python
for b, v in decide.uplift_budgets().items():   # trial of 20,000
    print(f"offer to {b:4.0%}: by risk {v['risk']:5.1f}, by effect "
          f"{v['effect']:5.1f}, at random {v['random']:5.1f}, "
          f"best {v['true']:5.1f}")
```

```text
offer to   5%: by risk   0.9, by effect  14.7, at random   5.5, best  17.1
offer to  10%: by risk   4.7, by effect  27.9, at random  10.9, best  31.7
offer to  20%: by risk  20.1, by effect  50.4, at random  21.9, best  55.5
offer to  50%: by risk  79.5, by effect  92.4, at random  54.7, best  98.1
offer to 100%: by risk 109.4, by effect 109.4, at random 109.4, best 109.4
```

With only 5% of customers reachable, offering the deal to the highest predicted risk prevented 0.9 churns per 1,000, against 5.5 for a random 5% and 14.7 for the effect ranking. The riskiest twentieth gained on average only 0.018 in churn probability from the offer, against 0.109 across all customers, and the risk ranking did several times worse than chance. At half the customers the risk ranking finally beat random targeting (79.5 against 54.7), because a list that long reaches far enough down the risk scale to include the middling customers the offer helps. Offering it to everyone prevents 109.4 whatever the ranking.

So the cost of ranking by risk is largest exactly when targeting matters most, under a tight budget, and it is a cost relative to doing nothing clever at all.

Every decision in Chapter 12 and in this chapter so far has been made once, on data collected independently of it. Section 13.3 asks what happens when the decisions themselves choose the data the next model is trained on.

## A Rule That Chooses Its Own Data Learns Its Own Choices

<!-- source: ml-50-feedback; hero: ml150-hero.png -->


The decisions of Chapter 12 and of the first two sections of this chapter all produce data. The cases flagged are the cases whose outcomes get checked; the customers offered a deal are the ones whose response is recorded; the places inspected are the places where violations are found. When the next model is trained on that record, its training data were chosen by the previous model. The comfortable assumption is that this loop corrects itself, and it is the sentence this section tests: *a model retrained on the data it collects corrects itself over time*. Practitioners meet it in inspection and audit targeting, fraud review, content recommendation and predictive policing, anywhere a model decides where to look. If it is false, the record converges not on the truth but on the model's own earlier choices, and the stability of that record is mistaken for confirmation.

Section 13.2 depended on a trial run independently of any model. Section 15.2, in Part V, studies a model trained on outcomes that exist only because an earlier decision allowed them, a single round of selection. This section studies the repeated version. It sets up a deliberately small inspection programme, compares rules for allocating inspectors that learn from their own record, shows that the natural rules lock in to an answer fixed by early luck, and identifies the two repairs that work: estimating rates per unit of effort, and keeping a share of decisions independent of the record. It ends by drawing together what Chapter 12 and this chapter measured.

### An inspection programme that learns from its own record


An inspection programme has ten inspectors a day and two districts, A and B. Each inspection finds a violation with a probability equal to the district's true rate, and a violation is recorded only where an inspector goes. Each morning a rule splits the day's ten visits between the districts using everything recorded so far; the day's visits are allocated binomially according to the rule's share. Every rule starts from one violation found and one visit made in each district, so that no ratio is undefined on the first day. Each rule runs for a year, 365 days, and the year is repeated 500 times.

There are two worlds. In the first the districts are identical, both at a rate of 0.10 per visit, so the right answer is an even split. In the second, district B's rate is 0.12, 20% higher than A's. If visits are allocated in proportion to the true rates, A's share is 0.45; this is the "truth" against which the rules are judged in the second world.

Three rules are compared first:

- **greedy**: send all of the day's inspectors to the district with more violations on record;
- **proportional**: split the visits in proportion to the violations on record;
- **per visit**: split the visits in proportion to each district's violations found per visit made.

The code runs the identical world and prints, for each rule, the range of A's share of visits at the end of the year over the middle 80% of the 500 runs, and the fraction of runs in which every visit was going to one district.

```python
from standarderror.ml import decide

eq = decide.feedback_loop()                   # equal rates, 0.10
for rule in ("greedy", "proportional", "per visit"):
    v = eq[rule]
    print(f"{rule:12s} A's final share: 10th-90th percentile "
          f"{v['q10']:.2f}-{v['q90']:.2f}, all in one district "
          f"{v['locked']:.0%}")
```

```text
greedy       A's final share: 10th-90th percentile 0.00-1.00, all in one district 100%
proportional A's final share: 10th-90th percentile 0.14-0.88, all in one district 6%
per visit    A's final share: 10th-90th percentile 0.47-0.53, all in one district 0%
```

### The greedy rule locks in


The greedy rule is the most natural: go where the problems have been found. In every one of 500 years, by the last day every inspector was going to one district. Which district was decided by the luck of the first day or two; in the identical world it was A in 56% of years.

The mechanism is simple. A district that falls behind in recorded violations is not visited, and a district that is not visited records no new violations, so it cannot catch up. The rule's information about the neglected district freezes at whatever it was when the district fell behind, while the favoured district's count keeps growing. The record then says, truthfully, that nearly all violations were found in one place, and that is because nearly all inspections were made there. Lum and Isaac showed in 2016, by applying a predictive-policing algorithm to Oakland's drug-arrest records, how such a system would keep returning to the same neighbourhoods. The figure below shows the spread of A's final share under each rule in the second world, where B's rate is higher and the true share for A is 0.45; the greedy rules span the whole range there too.

![With B's rate 20% higher, splitting visits by violations found left A's share between 0.07 and 0.75 in the middle 80% of runs; dividing by visits made, between 0.43 and 0.49.](ml150-f0-spread.png){alt="Horizontal bars. The two rules that send every visit to the leader span 0 to 1; splitting by violations found spans 0.07 to 0.75; splitting by violations per visit is a narrow bar at the true share of 0.45; with exploration, a point at 0.05."}

### Proportional allocation is not neutral


The proportional rule looks fairer. It rarely sends everyone to one place, only 6% of runs in the identical world, but it still does not learn the truth. Every violation found earns its district more visits, which find more violations. This is a *Pólya urn*: an urn holding balls of two colours, from which a ball is drawn at random and returned together with another of the same colour. The proportion of each colour in a Pólya urn does settle down, but at a random value fixed largely by the early draws, not at any value determined by the process itself. Ensign and colleagues used exactly this urn in 2018 to describe a runaway feedback loop in predictive policing.

In the identical world, A's final share under the proportional rule ranged from 0.14 to 0.88 across the middle 80% of years, and it was within 0.1 of an even split in only 21% of them. The next block runs the world in which B's rate is higher, and adds two more rules, described below. For each rule it prints the fraction of runs that ended with most visits in A, the district with the lower rate, and the fraction that ended within 0.1 of the true share.

```python
un = decide.feedback_loop(rates=(0.10, 0.12))  # B's rate 20% higher
for rule, v in un.items():
    if rule != "true_share":
```

### Dividing by the effort


The repair for the counting rules is one division. A count of violations found mixes two things: how common violations are in a district, and how often anyone looked. Dividing by the number of visits made turns the count into an estimate of the district's rate per visit, which does not grow simply because a district is visited more. Under the per-visit rule, more visits to a district produce more evidence about its rate, not a larger rate, and the loop stops feeding itself.

With this rule A's final share was within 0.1 of its true share in every run, in both worlds, averaging 0.456 against a true 0.455 in the second world. In the identical world the same rule ended at an average share of 0.499, against an even split of 0.5. Only 3% of runs in the second world ended with most visits in the worse district.

#### When the rule can stop looking

The division is enough here because the per-visit rule never stops visiting either district, so both estimates keep improving. A rule that sends all visits to the district with the higher rate per visit, labelled "greedy per visit" above, is what an organisation trying to find as many violations as possible would do, and it can stop looking. Once a district falls behind it is no longer visited, and its estimated rate freezes, perhaps at an unluckily low value. That rule ended the year with most visits in the worse district in 30% of runs.

Keeping 10% of visits split evenly, so that every district is always being measured, cut that to 8%. This is *exploration*: deliberately spending some decisions on options that the current estimates do not favour, in order to keep those estimates honest. Reinforcement learning has long treated it as a price of learning from one's own actions, a trade-off between exploiting what is believed and exploring to test the belief. Neither greedy rule came within 0.1 of the true share in any run, because the true share is a split and a greedy rule, with or without a small exploration share, sends nearly all visits to one place.

### What Chapters 12 and 13 measured


Chapter 12 and this chapter followed predictions into decisions. A threshold of 0.5 assumed costs nobody had, and the cost-derived threshold assumed calibration (Section 12.1). The model with the best AUC was the wrong choice when misses were expensive (Section 12.2). The features a model relied on were consequences and fellow travellers of the outcome, not levers (Section 13.1). The people most at risk were helped no more than people chosen at random (Section 13.2). And a rule that learned from its own decisions learned its own decisions back (this section).

In each case the model did what it was built to do, and the decision assumed it had been built for something else. The repairs were small and specific: a cost ratio and a calibration check; an evaluation at the operating point; a causal adjustment or an experiment; a model of the difference a treatment makes; a division by effort and a share of decisions kept independent of the record. None of them is available from the model alone. Chapter 14 turns back to the model itself, and to what the importance and explanation methods that Section 13.1 read as advice actually say about it.

## In practice

### Asking what to change

- **Read importance and what-if analyses as statements about the model.** They say what the model uses to predict, not what would happen if a feature were changed.
- **Classify each important feature by timing.** For each, record whether it is measured before or after the outcome could have influenced it. Anything recorded after the outcome began, or produced by the process that records it, is a consequence, not a lever.
- **List the plausible common causes.** For any proposed lever, write down what might drive both it and the outcome. If those causes cannot be measured, adjustment is not available.
- **Build a separate model for the causal question.** Include the treatment and the measured confounders, exclude anything downstream, and state the assumed structure explicitly.
- **Prefer an experiment when one is possible.** Where a change has been made deliberately, in a trial or a policy change, compare the observed effect with the model's what-if before trusting either.

### Choosing whom to treat

- **Ask what the intervention changes, not who is at risk.** The quantity to rank by is the difference the intervention makes for each person.
- **Keep a randomised holdout.** Even a small group of eligible people who are not treated, chosen at random, is what makes the effect estimable and the targeting rule checkable.
- **Check whether risk tracks effect.** Split the scored population into fifths by risk and compare treated and untreated outcomes within each. If the effect is not largest in the top fifth, the risk score is choosing the wrong people.
- **Try simple effect models alongside flexible ones, especially when trials are small.** The noise of two fits adds up in their difference; in Section 13.2 the logistic T-learner beat the boosted one at both trial sizes.
- **Report every targeting rule against random targeting of the same size**, in outcomes prevented per thousand, so the comparison that matters is always in view. This matters most when the budget is tight.

### When decisions choose the data

- **Audit where the training data came from.** For a model that decides where to look, whom to review or what to show, count how much of its training data came from units it selected and how much from anywhere else.
- **Estimate rates per unit of effort.** Replace counts of findings with findings per inspection, per review or per impression, so that looking more does not by itself make a unit look worse.
- **Keep a share of decisions independent of the model.** A fixed fraction allocated at random or evenly keeps every unit measured; in Section 13.3, 10% cut wrong lock-ins from 30% to 8%.
- **Check the units the model rarely selects.** Compare its estimates for them with an independent measurement such as an audit, a survey or a random sample.
- **Treat a stable record as evidence about the rule first.** A loop that has locked in shows up as units the model is confident about and nobody has looked at.

## Limits of the evidence

All three sections use simulated systems written down in advance, which is what makes the true answers available; each has its own further limits.

**What a model relies on (Section 13.1).**

- **A system written down in advance.** The causal structure is known because it was chosen. In practice it is a hypothesis, and the adjusted estimate is only as good as the assumption that $U$ is the only common cause.
- **The common cause was measurable.** $U$ was handed to the adjusted model. When it cannot be measured, adjustment is impossible, and the options are randomised experiments, instrumental variables or bounds.
- **Linear effects.** The treatment's effect is linear in the log-odds. Effects that differ between people, the subject of Section 13.2, need more than one number.
- **Four features.** Real models have hundreds, and the downstream ones are often hard to recognise: a variable recorded after the outcome began, or produced by the same process that records it.

**Targeting by effect (Section 13.2).**

- **A simulated effect with a clear shape.** The offer's effect depends on risk in a way that makes risk targeting fail. Where the effect is largest for the highest-risk people, risk targeting is close to right, and a trial is the only way to know which case holds.
- **Known true probabilities.** Churn prevented was computed from the simulation's probabilities. In practice the value of a targeting rule has to be estimated from another trial, with its own noise.
- **Budgets measured on one trial size.** The budget sweep used the large trial and logistic models; with a smaller trial the effect ranking is noisier at every budget.
- **One-shot.** The offer is made once. Repeated offers, and customers who learn to expect them, are a different problem.

**Feedback loops (Section 13.3).**

- **Two districts, fixed rates.** Real programmes have many units whose rates change, partly in response to the inspections. That adds a second loop: deterrence lowers the rate where inspectors go, and the record then says those places improved.
- **Violations found perfectly when present.** Inspections that miss some violations, or find more where inspectors expect them, bias even the per-visit estimate.
- **Simple rules.** A real system retrains a model on features, not a ratio on counts. The mechanism is the same whenever the model's training data come from where its own predictions sent people.
- **One year.** Lock-in under the greedy rules is decided early, but under the proportional rule with unequal rates the year-end share depends on the horizon, and the effect of exploration over much longer horizons was not measured.

## Key points

1. The feature the models relied on most, a consequence of the outcome, carried 0.45 of AUC importance and has no effect when changed.
2. A fellow traveller of the outcome lowered the predicted risk ($-0.029$) and has no effect; the real lever ranked no higher than it in importance, with a what-if under a third of its true effect.
3. A regression on the treatment and the common cause recovered $+0.089$ against a true $+0.085$; the treatment alone gave $+0.202$, and dropping the downstream feature alone gave $+0.144$. Which features are in the model decides the apparent effect of a lever, and the right set depends on a causal structure that no inspection of the model can reveal. Questions about what to change need that structure and a model built for the question, or an experiment.
4. Offering a retention deal to the highest predicted churners prevented 20.1 to 22.0 churns per 1,000 customers, about the same as random targeting (21.9). The highest-risk fifth held more customers the offer could not help or would harm: 11% of them were made more likely to leave, against 6% overall.
5. Ranking by the predicted difference the offer makes, from models of a randomised trial's two arms, prevented up to 50.4, against a best possible 55.5; it needed the larger trial to get there.
6. Under a tight budget the risk ranking did worst: at 5% it prevented 0.9 per 1,000, against 5.5 at random. Target an intervention by its estimated effect, which needs a randomised comparison, and measure the targeting rule against random targeting before trusting it.
7. Sending inspectors where the most violations had been found put every visit in one district by year's end in every run, the district with the lower true rate in 46% of runs. Splitting visits in proportion to violations found settled at random, as a Pólya urn does: within 0.1 of the truth in 21% of runs.
8. Dividing by visits made put the split within 0.1 of the truth in every run, in both worlds. When the rule could starve a district of visits, keeping 10% of visits for exploration cut wrong lock-ins from 30% to 8%.
9. When a model's decisions decide its data, estimate rates per unit of effort, keep a share of decisions independent of the model, and treat a stable record as evidence about the rule before treating it as evidence about the world.
