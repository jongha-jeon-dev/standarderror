# Target Encoding Fitted on the Training Rows Still Leaks, Into Training

Part VIII has followed the process behind the rows: time in Chapter 36, people in Chapter 37, gaps in Chapter 38 and decisions in Chapter 39. It ends with a feature built from the outcome itself. A category with many levels, such as a postcode, a merchant, a product or a user, is called *high-cardinality*, and it is awkward for most models. *One-hot encoding*, one binary column per level, makes thousands of sparse columns; trees given integer level codes split on an arbitrary order. *Target encoding* replaces each level with the mean outcome of the training rows at that level, giving one numeric column that carries what the category says about the target. Chapter 3 measured the obvious mistake, computing those means on all rows including the test fold, and found that a 200-level noise column encoded that way inflated the score. The standard advice that follows is the sentence under test: *fit the target encoding on the training rows only, inside the folds, and it cannot leak.* If the sentence is false, a pipeline that follows the advice to the letter can still produce a worse model than one that ignored the category, and its honest validation score will say only that the model is bad, not why.

The chapter first shows that a per-level mean of a pure-noise category, fitted on training rows only, damages the model badly once levels are rare. It then locates the leak: not into the test set, but into the training rows' own encoded values. It compares two repairs, shrinkage and cross-fitting, on noise and on a category that carries real signal.

## A noise category, encoded honestly

The test is designed so that the encoding can never touch the evaluation data. Each simulated data set has five standard-normal features carrying the real signal, through log-odds $x_1 + 0.7\,x_2 - 0.5\,x_3$, and a category of $K$ equally likely levels, with $K$ from 10 to 4,000. The encoding is fitted on 4,000 training rows and the model is scored on 20,000 fresh rows. Results are averaged over five data sets. In the first experiment the category carries nothing: every level has the same effect, zero.

Four ways of handling the category are compared:

- **Dropped.** The category is left out; this is the baseline any encoding must beat.
- **Mean per level.** Each level is replaced by the mean label of its training rows.
- **Shrunk.** Each level's mean is pulled towards the overall mean $\bar y$ as though it had $m$ extra rows at the overall rate. For a level $\ell$ with $n_\ell$ training rows and mean $\bar y_\ell$,
$$
\tilde y_\ell = \frac{n_\ell\,\bar y_\ell + m\,\bar y}{n_\ell + m},
$$
here with $m = 20$. Common levels keep nearly their own mean; rare ones are pulled most of the way to $\bar y$.
- **Cross-fitted.** Described below; scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder` with five shuffled folds and its default shrinkage.

Models are compared by test *AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen positive row is ranked above a randomly chosen negative one, and by *log loss*, the average negative log probability assigned to the true label, which punishes confident errors heavily. The first block shows boosted trees.

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

t = rows.target_encoding()     # 4,000 training rows, 5 data sets
for r in t:
    if r["effect"] == 0 and r["model"] == "boosted trees":
        print(f"{r['levels']:5d} levels, {r['encoding']:15s} "
              f"AUC {r['auc']:.3f}, log loss {r['nll']:.3f}")
```

```text
   10 levels, dropped         AUC 0.764, log loss 0.593
   10 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
   10 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
   10 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.764, log loss 0.593
  100 levels, dropped         AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
  100 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.756, log loss 0.606
  100 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.756, log loss 0.607
  100 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.763, log loss 0.595
 1000 levels, dropped         AUC 0.763, log loss 0.594
 1000 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.640, log loss 1.306
 1000 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.689, log loss 0.779
 1000 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.763, log loss 0.594
 4000 levels, dropped         AUC 0.763, log loss 0.594
 4000 levels, mean per level  AUC 0.580, log loss 2.449
 4000 levels, shrunk, m = 20  AUC 0.586, log loss 1.898
 4000 levels, cross-fitted    AUC 0.764, log loss 0.593
```

With 10 levels, 400 training rows each, encoding the category changes nothing. With 1,000 levels, four training rows per level, boosted trees given the per-level mean score a test AUC of 0.640, against 0.763 without the column, and their log loss doubles, to 1.306. With 4,000 levels they reach 0.580. Logistic regression falls too, from 0.791 to 0.690 at 1,000 levels. Nothing leaked into the test set; the encoding never saw a test label. The models are genuinely worse. The figure below shows the four encodings across the number of levels for both models.

## The leak is into the training rows

A level with four training rows has a mean label of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 or 1, and each of those four rows contributed its own label to that mean. For a training row, the encoded value is therefore partly its own answer: a quarter of it is that row's label. The next block measures how much this matters by scoring the encoded noise column on its own, with no model, against the labels of the training rows it was computed from and against fresh rows.

```python
c = rows.encoded_column_auc()  # the noise column alone, 1,000 levels
print(f"mean per level: AUC {c['mean_train']:.3f} on its own "
      f"training rows, {c['mean_test']:.3f} on fresh rows")
print(f"cross-fitted:   AUC {c['cross_train']:.3f} on its own "
      f"training rows, {c['cross_test']:.3f} on fresh rows")
```

```text
mean per level: AUC 0.774 on its own training rows, 0.498 on fresh rows
cross-fitted:   AUC 0.485 on its own training rows, 0.499 on fresh rows
```

On the rows it was computed from, the encoded noise column ranks the labels with an AUC of 0.774, better than many real features. On fresh rows it scores 0.498, the AUC of a coin. The model is trained on rows where this column is a strong predictor. It learns to rely on it at the expense of the five real features, and then meets test rows where the column is noise. That is overfitting, done by the preprocessing instead of the model. Cross-validation with the encoding fitted inside the folds measures the damage correctly, as a bad model, but does nothing to prevent it.

The mechanism is Chapter 3's leak moved one step. There, the encoding saw the test rows' labels; here, it sees the training rows' own labels, and the model trained on them cannot tell an honest feature from a recording of the answer. ## Two repairs: shrinkage and cross-fitting

### Shrinkage helps a linear model, not trees

Shrinkage pulls each level's mean towards the overall mean in proportion to how few rows it has. It helps logistic regression a great deal, to 0.767 at 4,000 levels. The likely reason is that shrinkage pulls the extreme values of rare levels, 0 and 1 for a level seen once, towards the middle, so the encoded column has a small spread, and logistic regression's default L2 penalty, which charges for large coefficients, then keeps the model from scaling the column back up.

Shrinkage does not help the trees (0.586 at 4,000 levels). Among levels with the same number of rows, the shrunk value is a fixed increasing function of the level's raw mean, so it keeps the levels in the same order, still ranked by the training rows' own labels. Trees split on the order of a feature's values, not on their scale, so a monotone squeeze leaves every split available to them.

### Cross-fitting removes a row's own label from its value

*Cross-fitting*, also called out-of-fold encoding, is what scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder` and CatBoost's ordered target statistics both do, in different ways. The training rows are split into folds, and each training row is encoded by level means computed from the other folds only, so its own label never enters its own value. Test rows get means computed from all training rows. The encoded value of a training row is then an honest prediction for that row, made without its label, as the encoded value of a test row is.

On the noise category, cross-fitting matched dropping the column at every number of levels, to within 0.002 of AUC for both models. The encoded column's AUC on its own training rows fell to 0.485, slightly below a coin. A leave-out mean is pulled away from the row it leaves out: a positive row is excluded from its own level's mean, which is therefore a little lower than it would be for the level's negative rows.

## When the category carries signal

Dropping the column is safe only when the category is noise, which is not why anyone encodes it. In the second experiment each level shifts the log-odds by an amount drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 1. With 10 or 100 levels, every encoding works and gains four to six points of AUC for logistic regression over dropping the column. With 1,000 levels the per-level mean turns the signal into a loss, 0.724 against 0.760 for dropping it, while cross-fitting keeps a gain, 0.778. At 4,000 levels the gain is small (0.765 against 0.759), because one row per level says little about any level. Even so, cross-fitting is the only encoding that is not worse than dropping the column for either model. The figure below shows the results for logistic regression.

![At 1,000 levels cross-fitting kept part of the signal (logistic AUC 0.778 against 0.760 with the column dropped); the per-level mean turned it into a loss (0.724).](ml140-f1-signal.png){alt="All encodings beat dropping the column at 10 and 100 levels; at 1,000 and 4,000 levels the per-level mean falls below dropping it, and cross-fitting stays above."}

Pargent and colleagues compared encodings on real high-cardinality data sets and reached a compatible conclusion: regularised target encoding, fitted with an internal cross-validation, was the most reliable choice across the learners they tried. ## In practice

- **Use out-of-fold target encoding** for high-cardinality categories, such as scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder`, which cross-fits the training rows when they pass through `fit_transform` (as in a pipeline); calling `fit` and then `transform` on the same rows does not. Do not write a plain per-level mean.
- **Measure the encoded column's leak directly.** Compute its AUC, or its correlation with the target, on the training rows and on held-out rows. A much higher training value means the column is partly a recording of the training labels.
- **Always compare with dropping the column.** That is the baseline any encoding has to beat, and on rare levels the per-level mean did not beat it.
- **Count rows per level, especially for the rarest levels.** The damage depends on rows per level, not on the total, and it concentrates in the long tail.
- **Do not rely on shrinkage alone with tree models.** It rescued logistic regression here but left trees almost as badly hurt as the raw mean.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Equally likely levels.** Real categories have a few common levels and a long tail of rare ones. The common levels encode safely and the tail does the damage, so the harm is concentrated where there is least data, which also makes it harder to see in an overall score.
- **One sample size.** There were 4,000 training rows. What matters is rows per level, so the same damage should appear at 40,000 levels with 40,000 rows.
- **Default settings.** `TargetEncoder`'s shrinkage and the boosted trees' regularisation were left at their defaults. Stronger regularisation in the trees reduces their reliance on any single column, including this one.
- **Binary outcome.** The same mechanism applies to regression targets and to multi-class targets, which encode one column per class.

## Key points

1. Fitting the encoding on the training rows only protects the test set and nothing else. With 1,000 noise levels, boosted trees fell from AUC 0.763 to 0.640.
2. The training rows leak into their own encoding: the noise column alone ranked its training labels at AUC 0.774, and fresh labels at 0.498.
3. Shrinkage largely rescued logistic regression and not trees at one row per level: trees use the order of the encoded values, which shrinkage preserves among levels with the same number of rows.
4. Cross-fitting matched dropping the column on noise and kept the signal when there was one.
5. Encode a high-cardinality category with out-of-fold means on the training rows, and check how many rows the rarest levels have before trusting the column.

- Prokhorenkova et al., "CatBoost: unbiased boosting with categorical features", *NeurIPS* (2018): ordered target statistics, a way of encoding each row without its own label.
