# Missing, Selected and Shifted Data

Every table a model is trained on was produced by some process: a clinician decided which tests to order, a lender decided whose loans to grant, a business decided which customers, regions or devices to serve. The rows and cells that process recorded are the only evidence the model has, and the population it is later applied to is rarely the one that produced them. This chapter, the whole of Part V, is about what that process does to a model. A practitioner meets it in three forms: values missing from some cells, outcomes missing for whole rows because a decision withheld them, and inputs that drift between training and deployment. In each form the standard advice treats the gap or the drift as a nuisance to be patched, and in each form the patch can be wrong in a way that no score computed on the available data will reveal.

Each section tests one sentence:

- Section 15.1: *missing values are a nuisance; fill them in as accurately as you can and model the rest*;
- Section 15.2: *a model trained on the outcomes you observed predicts the outcomes you did not*;
- Section 15.3: *covariate shift costs accuracy*.

The sections move from the smallest gap to the largest and then to the general case. Section 15.1 removes cells from a complete data set in different ways and finds that the right strategy depends on why the values are missing. When gaps are random, the best imputation gives the best model. When gaps depend on the missing value or on the outcome, the pattern of gaps carries information that accurate imputation throws away, and a model that uses that pattern is betting on the process that produced it staying the same. Section 15.2 takes the extreme case in which a whole row's outcome is missing because of a decision, and builds a simulated lender. Selection on the features the model sees costs only misspecification. Selection on something the model cannot see miscalibrates it while leaving its ranking intact, validation on the accepted cases cannot tell, and the repair is a small sample of outcomes bought from outside the decision. Section 15.3 isolates the situation that selection on the features produces: the inputs move while the rule linking them to the outcome stays fixed. It shows that what the shift charges for is the model's misspecification, examines importance weighting, the standard remedy, and measures the price the weights exact in effective sample size.

Three ideas recur. First, whether a gap or a shift is harmless depends on what drove it, and in the cases that matter that cannot be read from the data alone; it has to come from knowing how the data were collected. Second, a correctly specified or flexible model is protected against much of the damage that the features alone can cause, while a misspecified one is charged for its form wherever the data move. Third, the dependable checks use data collected independently of the process: gaps redistributed at random, applicants approved at random, labelled rows from the new population. Section 13.3 met the repeated version of the selection studied here, a rule that learns from data its own decisions chose; this chapter studies a single round of it in detail.

## A Missing Value Is Information, Until the Process Behind It Changes

<!-- source: ml-38-missing; hero: ml138-hero.png -->


Sections 2.3 and 2.4 were about rows that resemble each other because of where they came from. Missing values are about rows that are incomplete because of where they came from. A blood test not ordered, a survey question skipped, a sensor that dropped out: each leaves a gap in the table. The standard advice treats every gap the same way, and this section tests it in the form *missing values are a nuisance; fill them in as accurately as you can and model the rest.* Almost every tabular pipeline contains an imputation step built on this sentence. If it is false, a careful imputation can throw away the most informative thing in the data, or a model can come to depend on a pattern of gaps that will not survive a change in how the data are collected.

This section first sets out the standard classification of missing data, which says in principle when the sentence is safe. It then removes values from a complete data set in three different ways and compares five strategies under each: gaps at random, gaps that depend on the missing value, and gaps that depend on the outcome. A fourth condition changes the gap mechanism between training and testing. The section ends with how to choose a strategy.

### Three kinds of missingness


Rubin's classification of 1976 distinguishes three mechanisms by what the probability of a gap depends on.

- **Missing completely at random (MCAR).** Whether a value is missing is independent of everything, observed or not. The gaps are then a pure loss of data: the observed rows are a random subsample of the complete ones.
- **Missing at random (MAR).** Whether a value is missing depends only on other values that were observed. The missing values can then be filled in, in a statistical sense, from the rest of the row.
- **Missing not at random (MNAR).** Whether a value is missing depends on the value itself, or on something outside the observed features, such as the outcome the model has to predict. The gap then carries information that the observed values do not.

The sentence under test is safe under the first mechanism and, with a good imputer, under the second. Under the third it is not obvious what "as accurately as you can" should mean.

### The experiment


The breast-cancer data, 569 tumours with 30 features each, are complete, so gaps can be made to order. In each of three ways, 30% of the cells are removed, over five random masks. Five strategies are compared, each followed by logistic regression except the last:

- **Mean.** Replace each gap with the column mean.
- **Mean plus indicator.** The same, plus one *missingness indicator* per feature: a binary column that is 1 where the value was missing, so the model can give the fact of absence its own weight.
- **5-NN imputation.** Fill each gap with the average of that feature over the five most similar tumours, similarity being measured on the observed features.
- **Iterative imputation.** Model each feature with missing values as a regression on the other features, fill the gaps with the predictions, and cycle through the features repeatedly (here for 10 rounds) so that each round's fills improve the next.
- **Boosted trees, native.** Gradient-boosted trees given the raw gaps. At each split such a learner decides which branch rows with a missing value should follow, choosing whichever direction most reduces the loss.

Every imputer is fitted inside the cross-validation folds, so no information from a test fold reaches the imputation. Models are compared by *log loss*, the average of $-\log$ of the probability the model gave to the true class, which penalises confident mistakes heavily, and by accuracy. Imputations are compared by their root mean squared error on the removed cells, in standard deviations of each feature.

The first block reports the complete data and the first two mechanisms.

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

m = rows.missing_study()          # 30% of cells, 5 masks
print(f"complete data: log loss {m['complete']['nll']:.3f}")
for kind in ("random", "large values"):
    print(f"missing: {kind}")
    for meth, r in m[kind].items():
        if not meth.startswith("_"):
            print(f"   {meth:22s} log loss {r['nll']:.3f}, "
                  f"accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}")
```

```text
   boosted trees, native  log loss 0.151, accuracy 0.953
```

### Gaps at random: the best imputation gives the best model


Missing completely at random, the sentence mostly holds. Iterative imputation recovers the removed values best, with a root mean squared error of 0.44 standard deviations against 1.01 for the column mean, and it gives the best model: log loss 0.091, against 0.113 for the mean and 0.079 for the complete data. The thirty features are strongly correlated measurements of the same tumours, so most of a missing value can be read off the others, and a regression on them recovers it well.

The indicator columns add nothing here, because a random gap has nothing to indicate. They cost a little: 0.124 against 0.113. The ordering between imputation accuracy and model quality is not perfect, though. Five-nearest-neighbour imputation recovers the values much better than the mean (0.63 standard deviations against 1.01) and still gives a slightly worse model (0.119 against 0.113). The figure below shows accuracy for every strategy under all four conditions this section examines.

### When the gap depends on the value


In the second setting the chance that a cell goes missing rises with its value: in every feature, the larger measurements are the ones that tend to be absent. This is missing not at random. The second half of the output changes sharply. Mean imputation now replaces precisely the large values with an average one, systematically, so a tumour with large measurements is made to look ordinary, and the model falls to accuracy 0.880 and log loss 0.261.

Iterative imputation does better, 0.143, but it can only fill a gap with what the observed features predict. Those observed features were selected by the same mechanism, so the regression that fills the gaps is fitted on values skewed towards the small end and is likely to pull its predictions towards them. The simplest fix wins: mean imputation plus an indicator, log loss 0.121. As an imputation it is the worst of the three, 1.74 standard deviations of error against 1.31, since the mean is far from the large values it replaces. But the indicator says "this value was probably large", and logistic regression gives that its own weight, recovering the information the imputation lost. Boosted trees, which learn a direction for missing values at each split, reach 0.151, between iterative imputation and the plain mean.

This is the first half of the sentence failing: filling in accurately and predicting well are different objectives. Josse and colleagues studied the question theoretically. A learner is *consistent* if, as the training set grows, its predictions approach the best possible ones. They showed that when the gaps are not informative, even imputing a constant is consistent for a learner flexible enough to recover the pattern from where the constant appears, and recommended letting trees treat missingness as its own branch, which also captures informative gaps. Logistic regression is not that flexible, so here the indicator has to be handed to it explicitly; a careful imputation that hides the pattern loses it. The table below collects all five methods under all four conditions.

### When the gap depends on the outcome


The third mechanism is the one hospitals produce. A cell is missing nine times as often for a benign tumour as for a malignant one, the way a follow-up test is ordered mainly when something looks wrong. Now the gap depends on the outcome itself. The next block also includes a fourth condition, described below, in which the gap mechanism changes after training.

```python
for kind in ("by diagnosis", "policy change"):
    print(f"missing: {kind}")
    for meth, r in m[kind].items():
        if not meth.startswith("_"):
            print(f"   {meth:22s} log loss {r['nll']:.3f}, "
                  f"accuracy {r['acc']:.3f}")
```

```text

   mean + indicator       log loss 0.012, accuracy 0.999
   5-NN imputation        log loss 0.064, accuracy 0.982
   iterative imputation   log loss 0.091, accuracy 0.972
   boosted trees, native  log loss 0.041, accuracy 0.988
missing: policy change
   mean                   log loss 0.124, accuracy 0.954
   mean + indicator       log loss 0.458, accuracy 0.817
   5-NN imputation        log loss 0.130, accuracy 0.952
   iterative imputation   log loss 0.087, accuracy 0.972
   boosted trees, native  log loss 0.362, accuracy 0.892
```

#### The pattern of gaps predicts the diagnosis

With indicators, logistic regression scores an accuracy of 0.999, with log loss 0.012. That is better than the same model on the complete data, 0.980, because the pattern of missing tests says almost everything about the diagnosis: a tumour with many gaps is very probably benign. Boosted trees find the same signal on their own (0.988). Five-nearest-neighbour imputation also beats the complete data (0.982), presumably because the neighbours it borrows from are chosen using the cells that happened to be observed, so tumours with similar gap patterns, and hence similar diagnoses, fill each other's gaps. Agniel, Kohane and Weber found the real version of this in hospital records: for most of the laboratory tests they studied, the timing of the test predicted three-year survival better than its result.

#### When the policy changes, the signal disappears

The model has learned the clinicians' behaviour, which is a legitimate signal for as long as the clinicians behave the same way. The policy-change condition shows what happens when they do not. The same trained models are scored on tumours whose cells are missing at random, as though the testing policy had changed: every test now ordered, or lost, regardless of suspicion. The indicator model falls to 0.817, and the boosted trees to 0.892. Their strongest feature has stopped meaning what it meant in training. Iterative imputation, which never looked at the pattern, scores 0.972, the same accuracy it had when tested under the mechanism it was trained on, with log loss 0.087 against 0.091. (With random gaps in both training and testing it scored 0.969.)

### Choosing a strategy


The three settings produce three different winners, and no strategy is safe by default.

- If the gaps are close to random, impute as well as possible and leave the pattern out.
- If the gaps depend on the missing values, give the model the pattern, through indicators or a learner that handles missing values natively.
- If the gaps depend on a decision made by people or systems upstream, the pattern will probably be the strongest feature in the model, and its value lasts only as long as the decision rule does.

Which case applies cannot be read from the data alone. Missing-not-at-random is, by definition, not *identifiable* from what was observed: two different mechanisms can produce exactly the same observed data, so no test on those data can tell them apart. The answer has to come from knowing how the data were collected. What can be measured is how much the model depends on the pattern: fit it with and without indicators, and if the indicators add a great deal, find out why the values are missing before trusting the gain. Section 15.2 takes the most extreme form of outcome-dependent missingness, in which an entire row's label is missing because of a decision.

## The Applicants You Refused Are the Data You Do Not Have

<!-- source: ml-39-selective-labels; hero: ml139-hero.png -->


Section 15.1 ended with gaps that record a decision: tests ordered mainly when something looked wrong, so that the pattern of missing cells predicted the diagnosis. A missing label always records a decision. A lender sees whether a loan was repaid only if it made the loan; a hospital sees whether a treatment worked only for patients who received it; a platform sees whether a recommendation was clicked only if it was shown. The next model is trained on those outcomes and then applied to everyone, including the kind of case the last decision turned away. The sentence that makes this feel routine is *a model trained on the outcomes you observed predicts the outcomes you did not.* Lakkaraju and colleagues called this situation the *selective labels problem*. If the sentence fails, a model can look well validated and still misprice exactly the cases it has never seen an outcome for.

This section builds a simulated lender with four worlds, varying whether the old decision used information the model lacks and whether risk is straight or curved in the features. It first shows that selection on the model's own features costs only misspecification. It then shows that selection on something the model cannot see miscalibrates it badly while leaving its ranking intact, and that validation on the accepted applicants cannot detect either problem reliably. Finally it measures the repair that works in every world: a small sample of applicants approved at random.

### A simulated lender


Each applicant has two features the model sees, $x_1$ and $x_2$, and a third, $u$, that it does not: something a loan officer reading the file might pick up. All three are standard normal. The chance of default depends on all three, through the log-odds

$$
-1.5 + 1.2\,x_1 + 0.8\,x_2 + 1.2\,u,
$$

with an extra term $0.8\,x_1^2$ in the two *curved* worlds; in the other two, risk is *straight* (linear) in the features.

An old policy accepted the half of each cohort with the lowest risk score, and outcomes exist only for them. The score was $1.2\,x_1 + 0.8\,x_2$ plus noise in two of the worlds. In the other two the officer's judgement of $u$ also went into it, as an added $1.2\,u$. A logistic model and boosted trees are trained on the accepted half of cohorts of 20,000 and applied to a fresh cohort, accepted and refused alike, so that their predictions for the refused can be compared with what the refused actually did. Each world is averaged over ten cohorts.

Two measures of quality matter here and must be kept apart. The *AUC*, the area under the ROC curve, is the probability that a randomly chosen defaulter receives a higher predicted risk than a randomly chosen repayer; it measures ranking only. *Calibration* asks whether predicted probabilities match observed rates: whether, among applicants given a particular risk, about that fraction default. A lender who prices loans by predicted probability needs calibration, not just ranking.

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

for r in rows.selective_labels():     # 10 cohorts of 20,000
    if r["model"] == "logistic":
        print(f"curved={r['curved']!s:5} officer={r['officer']!s:5} "
              f"refused default {r['refused_rate']:.3f}, predicted "
              f"{r['refused_pred']:.3f}; AUC {r['auc_all']:.3f}")
```

```text
curved=False officer=False refused default 0.432, predicted 0.453; AUC 0.770
curved=False officer=True  refused default 0.486, predicted 0.137; AUC 0.769
curved=True  officer=False refused default 0.538, predicted 0.238; AUC 0.559
curved=True  officer=True  refused default 0.597, predicted 0.136; AUC 0.450
```

In the simplest world the sentence holds. The policy used only what the model sees, risk is straight, and logistic regression predicts a default rate of 0.453 among the refused, who default at 0.432. Its AUC on the whole cohort, 0.770, is the same as that of a model trained on everyone's outcomes. The figure below compares predicted and actual default rates among the refused in all four worlds.

![When the officer saw more, the accepted-only model expected 0.137 of the refused to default; 0.486 did.](ml139-f0-refused.png){alt="Grouped bars for four worlds. In the first, all bars are near 0.43. In the straight-risk world where the officer saw more, the accepted-only bar is near 0.14 while the actual rate is about 0.49 and the other two bars about 0.39."}

### Selection on what the model sees costs only misspecification


The simplest world is covariate shift in another form, the setting Section 15.3 below examines on its own. *Covariate shift* is a change in the distribution of the inputs with the conditional distribution of the outcome given the inputs held fixed. Selecting applicants on the features changes which inputs the model sees, but not the relationship between inputs and outcome among them: for a given $x_1$ and $x_2$, an accepted applicant and a refused one have the same chance of default. A correctly specified model therefore learns the right relationship from the accepted half and carries it to the refused half. Section 15.3 shows that covariate shift charges only for misspecification, and so does this selection.

The charge appears as soon as the model cannot extend what it learned. Boosted trees, trained on the same accepted half, predicted 0.337 for the refused. Trees split only within the range of inputs they were trained on, so beyond it their predictions stay flat; Section 7.3 measured this for forests. The refused sit beyond that range. In the curved world, logistic regression is the misspecified model, and it predicted 0.238 for a group that defaulted at 0.538. Its AUC on the whole cohort fell to 0.559, against 0.746 for the same model trained on everyone, probably because a straight line fitted to the accepted region of a curved relationship does not extend to the refused region.

### Selection on what the model cannot see miscalibrates it


When the officer's judgement went into the old decision, the accepted are not only the applicants with good features but, among applicants with similar features, the ones with good $u$. In the training data, then, applicants with mediocre features repaid well, because those who were accepted had something else in their favour. The model sees only the features and learns that mediocre features are safe. Formally, selection now depends on a variable that also affects the outcome, so the outcome's distribution given the features differs between the accepted and the refused, and data from the accepted alone cannot show by how much.

Logistic regression predicted a default rate of 0.137 for the refused. They defaulted at 0.486. Its ranking survived, an AUC of 0.769 against 0.770 for a model trained on everyone, because the selection shifted the level of its predictions more than their order. Its log loss, defined in Section 15.1, records the damage: 0.637 against 0.494. A lender who used its probabilities to price risk would price the refused applicants at less than a third of their real default rate.

#### Two losses, one repairable

There are two losses here, and only one can be repaired. Even the model trained on every applicant's outcome, which no lender has, predicted 0.394 for the refused, below their 0.486. The refused were selected partly for a $u$ that the features do not contain, so no model of the features can know that they are worse than their features suggest. This is the subgroup miscalibration of Section 11.3, where a model calibrated on average was miscalibrated within groups, here produced by the decision itself. The gap from 0.137 to 0.394 is the *sample selection bias* Heckman described in 1979: the distortion of an estimated relationship when the sample is chosen by a process related to the outcome. That part can be fixed, given outcomes from outside the selection. The table below collects both models in all four worlds.

!['Everyone-trained' is the same model fitted on every applicant's outcome, which no lender has: the ceiling for a model that sees these features.](ml139-f1-table.png){alt="An eight-row table, two models in each of four worlds, with predicted and actual default rates among the refused and three AUCs."}

### Validation on the accepted can err in either direction


The same selection undermines the usual check. A model like this is validated on held-out accepted applicants, because they are the only ones with outcomes. In the straight world with a features-only policy, the AUC measured that way was 0.673, against 0.770 on the whole cohort. The accepted are a narrower, more similar group, and ranking within a group whose risks are close together is harder than ranking across the full range.

In the curved world it went the other way: 0.627 on the accepted against 0.559 on everyone, because the model fits the accepted region and fails outside it. In the curved world with the officer, the model ranked the whole cohort worse than chance, 0.450, while its validation on the accepted reported 0.610. Validation on the accepted can err in either direction, and nothing in it says which.

### Buying the missing outcomes with random approvals


The repair that works in every world is to stop selecting for some of the data. Suppose 5% of applicants, about 992 per cohort, had been approved at random, regardless of score and officer. The next block compares three predictions for the refused: a logistic model trained on those random approvals alone; the accepted-only model *recalibrated* on them, which keeps its coefficients but refits the intercept and slope of its log-odds; and the model trained on everyone.

```python
for r in rows.selective_labels():
    if r["model"] == "logistic":
        print(f"curved={r['curved']!s:5} officer={r['officer']!s:5} "
              f"5% random only: {r['explore_refused_pred']:.3f}; "
              f"recalibrated: {r['recal_refused_pred']:.3f}; "
              f"everyone: {r['oracle_refused_pred']:.3f}")
```

```text
curved=False officer=False 5% random only: 0.424; recalibrated: 0.425; everyone: 0.430
curved=False officer=True  5% random only: 0.389; recalibrated: 0.390; everyone: 0.394
curved=True  officer=False 5% random only: 0.531; recalibrated: 0.383; everyone: 0.530
curved=True  officer=True  5% random only: 0.493; recalibrated: 0.378; everyone: 0.492
```

A logistic model trained on those thousand or so randomly approved applicants alone predicted 0.389 for the refused in the officer world and 0.531 in the curved one, matching the model trained on everyone to within 0.01 in all four worlds. The random approvals are a sample from the whole population, so the relationship learned from them is the population's, not the accepted half's.

Recalibration worked when the shape of the model was right (0.390) and not when it was wrong (0.383 in the curved world, against 0.530). Refitting an intercept and a slope can correct a shift in the level and scale of the log-odds, which is what selection on $u$ produced. It keeps the direction in the features that the accepted-only model learned, and in the curved world that direction, fitted to the accepted region, probably does not suit the refused one; the straight model trained on everyone, with different coefficients, did.

Approving some applicants at random has a cost, paid in defaults among applicants who would have been refused. It is, however, the only source of outcomes from the refused region. The alternatives, collectively called *reject inference*, have to assume what the refused would have done. Hand and Henley's verdict in 1993 was that reject inference cannot work reliably unless assumptions or information from outside the accepted data are brought in; the exploration sample is that information, measured rather than assumed. Section 15.3 returns to the first of the four worlds, in which only the inputs moved, and asks what such a shift costs in general.

## Covariate Shift Charges for Misspecification

<!-- source: ml-29-covariate-shift; hero: ml129-hero.png -->


Section 15.2 found that selecting applicants on the features the model sees changes which inputs it is applied to, not the rule linking inputs to outcomes, and that only models unable to extend what they had learned paid for it. That situation is not peculiar to lending. Section 10.3 ended with a network scored on digits moved off centre, inputs of a kind it had not been shown, and this is the normal condition of a deployed model: the population it scores drifts away from the one it was trained on. The simplest version of this drift is the covariate shift of Section 15.2: the distribution of the inputs $x$ changes between training and deployment, while the conditional distribution of the outcome given the inputs, $P(y \mid x)$, does not. The sentence usually attached to it is the one under test: *covariate shift costs accuracy*. Practitioners meet it whenever a monitoring dashboard flags that input distributions have moved and the response is to retrain, reweight or simply expect worse performance. If the sentence is wrong about where the cost comes from, the remedy will be aimed at the wrong thing.

The sentence sounds obvious, but the setting makes it odd. If $P(y \mid x)$ is unchanged, the best possible classifier is unchanged too, and a model that had learned it would carry on being right. Whatever the shift costs, then, is not the shift's doing alone. This section measures the cost in a simulation where everything is visible, shows that it falls on a misspecified model and barely on a flexible one, examines importance weighting, the standard remedy, and measures the price the weights exact in effective sample size.

### A simulation in which only the inputs move


The *Bayes rule* is the classifier that, for each $x$, predicts the class with the higher true probability $P(y \mid x)$; its accuracy, the *Bayes accuracy*, is the best any classifier can achieve on a given input distribution. Under covariate shift the rule itself is the same in training and test: it labels each $x$ identically. Its accuracy can still change a little, because the test inputs may fall more or less often in regions where the outcome is uncertain.

In the simulation the input is a single number, drawn from $N(0, 1)$ in training and from $N(s, 1)$ in test, for shifts $s$ from 0 to 2 standard deviations. The label is 1 with probability

$$
P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma\big(3 \sin(1.5x)\big),
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. This is the same wavy curve everywhere, so the shift moves only the inputs. Two models are fitted to 500 training rows, twenty times over, and scored on 20,000 test rows per shift. The first is an ordinary linear logistic regression, which cannot follow the wave. The second is a logistic regression on cubic spline features, which can. *Spline features* represent $x$ by a set of piecewise-cubic basis functions joined smoothly at fixed points called knots, here 8 of them, so that a linear model on these features can trace a smooth curve; beyond the outermost knots the basis continues linearly. A model is *misspecified* when no setting of its parameters reproduces the true $P(y \mid x)$; the linear model is misspecified here and the spline model, over the range of the data, nearly is not.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

rows = nets.covariate_shift()        # 500 training rows, 20 repetitions
for r in rows:
    print(f"shift {r['shift']:.1f}  Bayes {r['bayes']:.3f}  "
          f"flexible {r['acc_flex']:.3f}  linear {r['acc_linear']:.3f}")
```

```text
shift 0.0  Bayes 0.838  flexible 0.834  linear 0.816
shift 0.5  Bayes 0.835  flexible 0.832  linear 0.802
shift 1.0  Bayes 0.839  flexible 0.831  linear 0.758
shift 1.5  Bayes 0.839  flexible 0.821  linear 0.666
shift 2.0  Bayes 0.832  flexible 0.805  linear 0.536
```

The Bayes accuracy stays between 0.832 and 0.839 at every shift: the task does not get harder. The flexible model stays within 0.018 of it up to a shift of 1.5. The linear model loses more at every step and ends at 0.536, not far above a coin. Covariate shift, in this setting, costs one model a great deal and the other very little.

![Unweighted, the line fits the rising stretch where the training data are and keeps rising where the test data are; on N(2, 1) it is right on 53.6% of rows. Weighted, it fits the falling stretch (77.7%).](ml129-f0-setup.png){alt="A sine-like probability curve over x; a blue density centred at 0 and an orange one at 2; a rising dashed line fitted to the training region and a falling one fitted to the test region."}

### Why the line breaks and the spline does not


The figure above shows what the linear model did. Most training inputs lie between −1 and 1, where the wave climbs from its trough to its peak, so the best line through them rises. In the test region around $x = 2$ the wave has turned over and is falling. The line, still rising, predicts class 1 where class 0 is now the more likely label.

Nothing about the line was wrong *for the training distribution*. Maximum likelihood finds the line that best approximates the true curve, weighting each region of $x$ by how often training inputs fall there. It was the best linear approximation to the wave over $N(0, 1)$. The best linear approximation therefore depends on where the inputs are: when the inputs move, the best line moves, and the fitted one does not. That is the whole mechanism. Covariate shift hurts a model by exposing the places where it was approximating, and a misspecified model approximates everywhere.

The spline model follows the wave wherever it has data, so its errors in the test region are mostly the irreducible ones that the Bayes rule makes too. It does lose something at the largest shift, 0.027 of accuracy at $s = 2$. That is plausibly because the right tail of the test distribution reaches $x = 4$ and beyond, where the training data have almost no points and the spline extrapolates linearly; the mechanism was not isolated. Shift far enough and every model is extrapolating. The difference between the two models is how early the cost starts, and for the line it starts at the first step.

Calibration says the same thing more loudly. The *expected calibration error* (ECE) groups predictions into bins of predicted probability and averages, weighted by bin size, the gap between each bin's mean predicted probability and the observed frequency of the positive class. The linear model's ECE at $s = 2$ is 0.44: its probabilities are confidently pointed the wrong way. The spline's is 0.08. The flexible model loses 0.027 at a shift of 2; the linear one loses 0.296.](ml129-f1-lines.png){alt="The Bayes line is flat near 0.84; the flexible model tracks it, dipping slightly at the largest shift; the linear model falls steeply; the weighted linear model dips then recovers."}

### Importance weighting aims the model at the test inputs


The standard remedy is *importance weighting*: weight each training row by how much more likely its input is under the test distribution than under the training one,

$$
w(x) = \frac{p_{\text{test}}(x)}{p_{\text{train}}(x)},
$$

and fit by minimising the weighted training loss $\sum_i w(x_i)\,\ell(x_i, y_i)$. Because $P(y \mid x)$ is the same in both populations, the weighted average of the training loss is an unbiased estimate of the expected loss on the test population. The fitted model therefore aims at the best model *for the test inputs* rather than for the training inputs. For two normal distributions with unit variance the ratio has a closed form, $w(x) = \exp(sx - s^2/2)$, which grows exponentially in $x$: rows in the right tail of the training data get very large weights. Here the ratio is known exactly; in practice it has to be estimated, which is a second source of error this simulation leaves out.

The next block prints, for the linear model, accuracy and log loss (Section 15.1) without and with weights, and the effective sample size of the weights, defined below.

The weighted log loss is lower than the unweighted at every non-zero shift; at zero the weights are all 1 and change nothing. To see what the weights aim at, the weighted model is compared with a linear model fitted to 200,000 rows drawn from the test distribution itself, the best linear model for those inputs. The weighted model comes within 0.048 of its accuracy and 0.035 of its log loss, closer at small shifts than at large ones. At $s = 2$ the weights turn the line around, so that it fits the falling stretch of the wave, and accuracy goes from 0.536 to 0.777.

At $s = 1$ accuracy goes the other way, from 0.758 to 0.680. This is not the weights failing. The best linear model for $N(1, 1)$ inputs, judged by log loss, is a nearly flat line (it scores 0.665 itself), because over that range the wave rises and falls about equally. Log loss rewards a line that hedges near 0.5; accuracy rewards one that commits to a side. Importance weighting optimises the loss it is given, and when the model is misspecified, the loss-optimal and the accuracy-optimal models are different models. With a correctly specified model the two would coincide at the true probabilities, and the distinction would not arise. The table below collects the comparison.

### The weights shrink the sample


Weights are not free. A weighted sample of 500 rows carries less information than 500 equally weighted rows, and Kong's *effective sample size* measures how much less:

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}.
$$

It equals $n$ when all weights are equal and falls towards 1 as a single weight dominates. At a shift of 1 it is 39% of the sample. At 1.5 it is 15%, and at 2 it is 6%: a few rows in the training set's right tail carry most of the weight, and the fit is effectively made from about 32 of them.

That is why the weighted linear model's accuracy varies more from one training set to the next. Its standard deviation over the twenty repetitions is 0.069 at $s = 2$, against 0.001 unweighted. The weights buy a smaller bias at the price of a larger variance, and the effective sample paying for it shrinks at every step of the shift. When the test inputs lie where the training data have none, there is nothing to reweight.

For the flexible model the weights make little difference in the region it can already follow, and a small one at $s = 2$: accuracy 0.805 unweighted and 0.816 weighted, with the spread across repetitions falling from 0.063 to 0.015. That is the extrapolating tail again. Weighting puts more of the fit's attention on the few rows near the test region, which plausibly steadies what the splines do just beyond them; the mechanism was not isolated here.

This section found that a model fitted over one range of inputs is charged for its form when used over another. Section 5.2 met the same problem with a curve instead of a classifier: a power law fitted to small models and used to predict much larger ones.

## In practice

### Missing values

- **Ask why each value is missing** before choosing an imputer. Find out, for each column with gaps, who or what decides whether it is recorded.
- **Fit the model with and without missingness indicators** and compare cross-validated scores. A small difference is consistent with gaps close to random; a large gain means the model depends on the collection process.
- **Do not judge an imputer by its reconstruction error alone.** In Section 15.1 the worst imputation with an indicator beat the best imputation without one when large values went missing.
- **Fit imputers inside the folds**, as with every preprocessing step.
- **Stress-test an indicator-dependent model** by scoring it with the gaps redistributed at random, which imitates a change in collection policy. If performance collapses, document the dependence and monitor the policy.

### Outcomes that exist only for selected cases

- **List every input to the decision that produced your labels**, and mark the ones the model does not see. If the list has unmarked entries only, expect selection to cost only misspecification; otherwise expect miscalibration on the refused.
- **Do not trust AUC on accepted cases as a measure for everyone.** In Section 15.2 it was too low in one world and too high in another.
- **Check calibration, not only ranking.** Selection on unseen information left the AUC intact while the predicted default rate for the refused was less than a third of the real one.
- **Prefer models that extrapolate sensibly** when they will be applied beyond the accepted region; trees cannot predict beyond the range they were trained on, and a misspecified linear model extrapolated badly too.
- **Budget for a random exploration sample.** A few per cent of decisions made at random gives outcomes from outside the policy's approvals, enough in Section 15.2 to match a model trained on everyone. Use it to train the model, or at least to check it; recalibrating on it repaired the accepted-only model only where its shape was right.

### Inputs that drift

- **Separate the shift from the model.** When inputs drift, ask whether $P(y \mid x)$ has plausibly stayed fixed; if so, the cost of the drift is the model's misspecification, and the first remedy to try is a more flexible model.
- **Compare a flexible model on the shifted rows before reweighting.** If its accuracy holds where the current model's falls, the current model was approximating, and that, not the shift, is the problem.
- **Choose the metric before reweighting.** Importance weighting optimises the loss it is given. With a misspecified model it can lower log loss while lowering accuracy; report both.
- **Always compute the effective sample size of the weights.** If it is a few percent of the sample, the fit rests on a handful of rows, its variance is large, and the test population sits where the training data are thin.
- **Check for drift in $P(y \mid x)$ with labelled data from the new population.** No reweighting can correct a change in the rule itself.

## Limits of the evidence

**Missing values (Section 15.1).**

- **Artificial mechanisms.** The three masks are clean versions of what real data mix together. Real missingness is usually partly random, partly value-dependent and partly decided by someone, in proportions nobody wrote down.
- **Thirty correlated features.** Breast-cancer measurements are redundant, which is why iterative imputation does so well at random. With weakly related features, every imputation is closer to the mean, and the differences shrink.
- **One rate.** Thirty percent of cells, everywhere. Real gaps concentrate in a few columns and a few rows, and dropping such columns or rows is sometimes the better choice.
- **Small data.** With 569 tumours, the differences between strategies at random are about two hundredths of log loss; under the informative mechanisms they are several times larger, which is the point.

**Selected labels (Section 15.2).**

- **One officer, one unobserved variable.** Real decisions use many unrecorded signals, some correlated with the recorded features. The size of the bias depends on how much the decision used that the model cannot see, which is rarely known.
- **Outcomes are clean.** A loan's outcome takes years and depends on the terms offered, which the policy also chose. The same structure holds for treatments and recommendations, with their own versions of these complications.
- **Random approvals of 5%.** Fewer would give noisier estimates. In practice exploration is often stratified, approving at random only near the decision boundary, which costs less and reaches less of the refused region.
- **Static.** Each new model sets the next policy, which selects the next training data. Feedback over several rounds can compound the bias measured here, and is not simulated; Section 13.3 studies such a loop in a simpler setting.

**Covariate shift (Section 15.3).**

- **The weights were exact.** Both densities were known. In practice the ratio is estimated, with a classifier trained to tell training inputs from test inputs or with a density-ratio method, and estimated weights add their own variance, worst exactly where the true weights are largest.
- **Only the inputs moved.** If $P(y \mid x)$ changes as well, the setting is *concept shift* and none of this applies: the Bayes rule itself moves, and no reweighting of old labels can learn the new one. In real drift the two usually come together, and telling them apart needs labelled data from the new population.
- **One dimension.** In one dimension the flexible model sees the whole range it will be tested on, up to the tail. In many dimensions a shift can move the inputs to combinations no training row came near, and every model extrapolates there. The lesson that misspecification is what the shift charges for still holds; a flexible model just runs out of support sooner.
- **A periodic rule.** The wave was chosen so that a line would fail. Where the true rule is close to linear over both ranges, a linear model loses little to the shift and weighting has nothing to correct.

## Key points

1. Missing at random, the best imputation gave the best model: iterative imputation, error 0.44 standard deviations, log loss 0.091; the mean, 1.01, log loss 0.113. Indicators added nothing.
2. Imputation accuracy and predictive quality are different objectives. 5-NN imputation recovered values better than the mean and still gave a slightly worse model; when large values went missing, the indicator mattered more than the imputation: 0.121 with mean imputation and indicators, 0.143 with iterative imputation alone.
3. When the gaps followed the diagnosis, the indicator model scored 0.999, above the complete data, and 0.817 once the gaps became random. Ask why each value is missing before choosing a strategy, and treat a large gain from missingness indicators as a dependence on the process that collected the data.
4. Selection on the features the model sees costs only misspecification: a correctly specified model predicted 0.453 for refused applicants who defaulted at 0.432; boosted trees, which cannot extrapolate, 0.337.
5. Selection on what the model cannot see miscalibrates it without warning: 0.137 predicted for a group defaulting at 0.486, with its AUC intact. Part of that loss cannot be repaired by any model of the observed features: even a model trained on everyone predicted 0.394.
6. AUC on accepted applicants was 0.673 against 0.770 on everyone in one world, and 0.627 against 0.559 in another: validation on the accepted can err in either direction.
7. A small randomly approved sample recovered what a model trained on everyone would know. Find out what information the past decision used, and buy some outcomes from outside its approvals.
8. Under covariate shift the best possible accuracy need not change: here the Bayes accuracy stayed between 0.832 and 0.839 while the inputs moved two standard deviations. What the shift costs is the model's misspecification, exposed: a flexible model stayed within 0.018 of Bayes up to a shift of 1.5; a linear one fell from 0.816 to 0.536.
9. Importance weighting refits the model for the test inputs. It lowered log loss at every non-zero shift and moved towards the best linear model for the test distribution (within 0.048 of its accuracy), which at a shift of 1 was a worse classifier (0.680 against 0.758). The weights shrink the sample: at a shift of 2 the effective sample was 6% of 500 rows.
10. Before reweighting, check whether a more flexible model makes the shift harmless, and check the effective sample size of the weights before trusting what they produce.
