# A Rule That Chooses Its Own Data Learns Its Own Choices

Every decision in Part X produces data. The cases flagged are the cases whose outcomes get checked; the customers offered a deal are the ones whose response is recorded; the places inspected are the places where violations are found. When the next model is trained on that record, its training data were chosen by the previous model. The comfortable assumption is that this loop corrects itself, and it is the sentence this chapter tests: *a model retrained on the data it collects corrects itself over time*. Practitioners meet it in inspection and audit targeting, fraud review, content recommendation and predictive policing, anywhere a model decides where to look. If it is false, the record converges not on the truth but on the model's own earlier choices, and the stability of that record is mistaken for confirmation.

Chapter 39 trained a model on outcomes that existed only because an earlier decision allowed them, a single round of selection. This chapter studies the repeated version. It sets up a deliberately small inspection programme, compares rules for allocating inspectors that learn from their own record, shows that the natural rules lock in to an answer fixed by early luck, and identifies the two repairs that work: estimating rates per unit of effort, and keeping a share of decisions independent of the record. It ends by drawing together what Part X measured.

## An inspection programme that learns from its own record

An inspection programme has ten inspectors a day and two districts, A and B. Each inspection finds a violation with a probability equal to the district's true rate, and a violation is recorded only where an inspector goes. Each morning a rule splits the day's ten visits between the districts using everything recorded so far; the day's visits are allocated binomially according to the rule's share. Every rule starts from one violation found and one visit made in each district, so that no ratio is undefined on the first day. Each rule runs for a year, 365 days, and the year is repeated 500 times.

There are two worlds. In the first the districts are identical, both at a rate of 0.10 per visit, so the right answer is an even split. In the second, district B's rate is 0.12, 20% higher than A's. If visits are allocated in proportion to the true rates, A's share is 0.45; this is the "truth" against which the rules are judged in the second world.

Three rules are compared first:

- **greedy**: send all of the day's inspectors to the district with more violations on record;
- **proportional**: split the visits in proportion to the violations on record;
- **per visit**: split the visits in proportion to each district's violations found per visit made.

The code runs the identical world and prints, for each rule, the range of A's share of visits at the end of the year over the middle 80% of the 500 runs, and the fraction of runs in which every visit was going to one district.

```python
from standarderror.ml import decide

eq = decide.feedback_loop()                   # equal rates, 0.10
for rule in ("greedy", "proportional", "per visit"):
    v = eq[rule]
    print(f"{rule:12s} A's final share: 10th-90th percentile "
          f"{v['q10']:.2f}-{v['q90']:.2f}, all in one district "
          f"{v['locked']:.0%}")
```

```text
greedy       A's final share: 10th-90th percentile 0.00-1.00, all in one district 100%
proportional A's final share: 10th-90th percentile 0.14-0.88, all in one district 6%
per visit    A's final share: 10th-90th percentile 0.47-0.53, all in one district 0%
```

## The greedy rule locks in

The greedy rule is the most natural: go where the problems have been found. In every one of 500 years, by the last day every inspector was going to one district. Which district was decided by the luck of the first day or two; in the identical world it was A in 56% of years.

The mechanism is simple. A district that falls behind in recorded violations is not visited, and a district that is not visited records no new violations, so it cannot catch up. The rule's information about the neglected district freezes at whatever it was when the district fell behind, while the favoured district's count keeps growing. The record then says, truthfully, that nearly all violations were found in one place, and that is because nearly all inspections were made there. Lum and Isaac showed in 2016, by applying a predictive-policing algorithm to Oakland's drug-arrest records, how such a system would keep returning to the same neighbourhoods. The figure below shows the spread of A's final share under each rule in the second world, where B's rate is higher and the true share for A is 0.45; the greedy rules span the whole range there too.

## Proportional allocation is not neutral

The proportional rule looks fairer. It rarely sends everyone to one place, only 6% of runs in the identical world, but it still does not learn the truth. Every violation found earns its district more visits, which find more violations. This is a *Pólya urn*: an urn holding balls of two colours, from which a ball is drawn at random and returned together with another of the same colour. The proportion of each colour in a Pólya urn does settle down, but at a random value fixed largely by the early draws, not at any value determined by the process itself. Ensign and colleagues used exactly this urn in 2018 to describe a runaway feedback loop in predictive policing.

In the identical world, A's final share under the proportional rule ranged from 0.14 to 0.88 across the middle 80% of years, and it was within 0.1 of an even split in only 21% of them. The next block runs the world in which B's rate is higher, and adds two more rules, described below. For each rule it prints the fraction of runs that ended with most visits in A, the district with the lower rate, and the fraction that ended within 0.1 of the true share.

```python
un = decide.feedback_loop(rates=(0.10, 0.12))  # B's rate 20% higher
for rule, v in un.items():
    if rule != "true_share":
        print(f"{rule:26s} mostly in A (worse): {v['mostly_a']:.0%}, "
              f"within 0.1 of truth: {v['within_10']:.0%}")
```

```text
greedy                     mostly in A (worse): 46%, within 0.1 of truth: 0%
proportional               mostly in A (worse): 26%, within 0.1 of truth: 21%
per visit                  mostly in A (worse): 3%, within 0.1 of truth: 100%
greedy per visit           mostly in A (worse): 30%, within 0.1 of truth: 0%
greedy per visit, explore  mostly in A (worse): 8%, within 0.1 of truth: 0%
```

The greedy rule ended with every visit in the lower-rate district in 46% of runs, scarcely better than a coin. When B's rate is really higher, the proportional rule does notice, and overdoes it. A's share averaged 0.35, against the 0.45 that matches the two rates, because each find by the higher-rate district earns it more visits faster. Under this rule the higher-rate district's share keeps growing the longer the programme runs, so the year-end figure depends on the horizon. Either way, the record of where violations were found has become a record of where inspectors were sent. The table below summarises the five rules in both worlds.

## Dividing by the effort

The repair for the counting rules is one division. A count of violations found mixes two things: how common violations are in a district, and how often anyone looked. Dividing by the number of visits made turns the count into an estimate of the district's rate per visit, which does not grow simply because a district is visited more. Under the per-visit rule, more visits to a district produce more evidence about its rate, not a larger rate, and the loop stops feeding itself.

With this rule A's final share was within 0.1 of its true share in every run, in both worlds, averaging 0.456 against a true 0.455 in the second world. In the identical world the same rule ended at an average share of 0.499, against an even split of 0.5. Only 3% of runs in the second world ended with most visits in the worse district.

### When the rule can stop looking

The division is enough here because the per-visit rule never stops visiting either district, so both estimates keep improving. A rule that sends all visits to the district with the higher rate per visit, labelled "greedy per visit" above, is what an organisation trying to find as many violations as possible would do, and it can stop looking. Once a district falls behind it is no longer visited, and its estimated rate freezes, perhaps at an unluckily low value. That rule ended the year with most visits in the worse district in 30% of runs.

Keeping 10% of visits split evenly, so that every district is always being measured, cut that to 8%. This is *exploration*: deliberately spending some decisions on options that the current estimates do not favour, in order to keep those estimates honest. Reinforcement learning has long treated it as a price of learning from one's own actions, a trade-off between exploiting what is believed and exploring to test the belief. Neither greedy rule came within 0.1 of the true share in any run, because the true share is a split and a greedy rule, with or without a small exploration share, sends nearly all visits to one place.

## What Part X measured

Part X followed predictions into decisions. A threshold of 0.5 assumed costs nobody had, and the cost-derived threshold assumed calibration (Chapter 46). The model with the best AUC was the wrong choice when misses were expensive (Chapter 47). The features a model relied on were consequences and fellow travellers of the outcome, not levers (Chapter 48). The people most at risk were helped no more than people chosen at random (Chapter 49). And a rule that learned from its own decisions learned its own decisions back (this chapter).

In each case the model did what it was built to do, and the decision assumed it had been built for something else. The repairs were small and specific: a cost ratio and a calibration check; an evaluation at the operating point; a causal adjustment or an experiment; a model of the difference a treatment makes; a division by effort and a share of decisions kept independent of the record. None of them is available from the model alone. Part XI turns from models a practitioner trains to language models somebody else trained, and to what a benchmark score does and does not measure about them.

## In practice

- **Audit where the training data came from.** For a model that decides where to look, whom to review or what to show, count how much of its training data came from units it selected and how much from anywhere else.
- **Estimate rates per unit of effort.** Replace counts of findings with findings per inspection, per review or per impression, so that looking more does not by itself make a unit look worse.
- **Keep a share of decisions independent of the model.** A fixed fraction allocated at random or evenly keeps every unit measured; here 10% cut wrong lock-ins from 30% to 8%.
- **Check the units the model rarely selects.** Compare its estimates for them with an independent measurement such as an audit, a survey or a random sample.
- **Treat a stable record as evidence about the rule first.** A loop that has locked in shows up as units the model is confident about and nobody has looked at.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Two districts, fixed rates.** Real programmes have many units whose rates change, partly in response to the inspections. That adds a second loop: deterrence lowers the rate where inspectors go, and the record then says those places improved.
- **Violations found perfectly when present.** Inspections that miss some violations, or find more where inspectors expect them, bias even the per-visit estimate.
- **Simple rules.** A real system retrains a model on features, not a ratio on counts. The mechanism is the same whenever the model's training data come from where its own predictions sent people.
- **One year.** Lock-in under the greedy rules is decided early, but under the proportional rule with unequal rates the year-end share depends on the horizon, and the effect of exploration over much longer horizons was not measured.

## Key points

1. Sending inspectors where the most violations had been found put every visit in one district by year's end in every run, the district with the lower true rate in 46% of runs.
2. Splitting visits in proportion to violations found settled at random, as a Pólya urn does: within 0.1 of the truth in 21% of runs.
3. Dividing by visits made put the split within 0.1 of the truth in every run, in both worlds.
4. When the rule could starve a district of visits, keeping 10% of visits for exploration cut wrong lock-ins from 30% to 8%.
5. When a model's decisions decide its data, estimate rates per unit of effort, keep a share of decisions independent of the model, and treat a stable record as evidence about the rule before treating it as evidence about the world.
