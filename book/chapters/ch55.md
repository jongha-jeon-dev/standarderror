# Every Judge Ranked Itself First

Arithmetic has exact answers. Most of what language models are now asked to produce does not: summaries, explanations, code that should be readable. Checking such outputs by hand is slow, and the common substitute is to ask a model to grade them, either one answer at a time or by choosing the better of two. This is the *LLM-as-a-judge* approach, and it rests on the sentence this chapter tests: *a model can grade other models' answers in place of people.* A practitioner relies on it whenever an automated grader decides which prompt, fine-tune or vendor wins. If it fails, the grader's ranking reflects the grader as much as the contestants, and a model can be promoted because it resembles the model that judged it.

Zheng and colleagues reported in 2023 that strong judges agreed with human preferences about as often as humans agreed with one another. They also documented biases for the position of an answer and for its length, and saw some signs of judges favouring their own answers that their data were too limited to confirm. This chapter measures that last bias with judges whose verdicts can be checked exactly. It first builds the simplest possible judge and shows that every judge ranked its own answers first. It then explains why a model's own mistakes look right to it, and what the judges nonetheless did well. Next it shows how a small set of human labels, used through prediction-powered inference, removes the bias, and when that repair costs precision. A final section draws together what Part XI has measured.

## A judge in its simplest form

The contestants are the three arithmetic models of Chapter 54, small transformers trained in this book's repository: the main model, its leaky twin, and a much weaker short run. Each answers 300 problems on each of three tasks (three-digit carries, multiplication, and problems mixing addition and multiplication), generating each answer *greedily*, that is, by taking the most likely next character at every step.

Each model then acts as a judge. It reads every answer, its own and the other two models', after the question, in a context different from the one the answer was written in, and reports its average log-probability per character. A high score means "this looks right to me". A real judge is prompted for a verdict instead of read for a likelihood, but its verdict plausibly draws on the same sense of what is likely. Panickssery and colleagues found that large models' preference for their own outputs rose with their ability to recognise them, though they did not test a likelihood mechanism.

To turn a score into a verdict, each judge needs a pass threshold. To give the judge every advantage, the threshold is set after the fact, so that over all 900 answers on a task it passes as many as are actually right, up to ties in its scores. The judge's overall pass rate is therefore correct by construction. Any bias that survives this is not a matter of the threshold being in the wrong place; it is a matter of which answers the judge passes.

The code shows the mechanics on six three-digit carries. The short run answers them; then the short run and the main model each judge those answers, and the per-character scores are printed beside the true answers.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import llmeval as le
from standarderror.schoolmath import arithmetic as ar
from standarderror.schoolmath import model as sm

main, short = sm.load(), sm.load_member("s1_6000")
sums = le.items("probe_carry", "add", 300)[:6]    # three-digit carries
gen = ar.fillers(6, seed=9)                       # contexts to answer in
ctx = ar.fillers(6, seed=21)                      # contexts to judge in
a = [r["got"] for r in ar.graded(short, sums, filler=gen)]
for judge, name in ((short, "short"), (main, "main")):
    lp = le.answer_logp(judge, [p.prompt for p in sums], a, ctx)
    print(f"{name:5s} judges short's answers:",
          np.round(lp / [len(x) + 1 for x in a], 2))
print("right answers:", [p.answer for p in sums])
print("short wrote:  ", a)
```

```text
short judges short's answers: [-0.02 -0.48 -0.19 -1.28 -0.01 -0.6 ]
main  judges short's answers: [-0.   -4.19 -0.87 -4.7  -0.15 -4.25]
right answers: ['1712', '1220', '907', '972', '1314', '721']
short wrote:   ['1692', '1191', '897', '960', '1294', '722']
```

All six of the short run's answers are wrong. Judging them itself, the short run gives several of them scores close to zero, the highest possible, such as -0.02 and -0.01. The main model gives most of the same answers far lower scores, down to -4.7. The same wrong answers look plausible to the model that wrote them and implausible to a stronger one.

## Nine judges, nine self-rankings

Across the full experiment, on every task each of the three judges passed a higher share of its own answers than of either other model's: **9 of 9** judge-and-task pairs ranked the judge first.

For the main model and its twin, which are close in ability, that self-preference reversed the true order whenever the judge was the weaker of the two on a task. On three-digit carries the main model is right 0.357 of the time and the twin 0.513. Judging, the main model passed 0.477 of its own answers and 0.353 of the twin's: it raised its own score, lowered the twin's, and put itself first.

The short run is the extreme case. Its answers to three-digit carries were right 1.3% of the time, and judging them itself it passed **73%**. A model that is the worst on every task by a wide margin was, in its own judgement, the best on every task. The figure below shows all nine judge-and-task pairs.

## A model's own mistakes look right to it

### The mechanism

The likely mechanism is simple. A greedy answer is built from the characters the model finds most likely at each step, so the same model, asked how likely that answer is, tends to find it likely, whether it is right or wrong. Because the judging context differs from the answering one, this is an expectation rather than an identity, and the scores bear it out. On problems mixing addition and multiplication the main model passed 29% of its own wrong answers and 6% of the twin's. On three-digit carries its average score for its own *wrong* answers, -0.098, was higher than its average score for the twin's *right* ones, -0.353.

### What the judges did well

What the judges did well deserves an equally precise statement. Within any one model's answers, the main model and the twin as judges separated right answers from wrong ones with AUCs from 0.59 on three-digit carries to 0.99 on multiplication. The AUC, as in Chapter 47, is the probability that a randomly chosen right answer receives a higher score than a randomly chosen wrong one. So the bias was not mainly an inability to tell right from wrong. Each judge ranked answers sensibly within a model but put its own answers on a higher scale, and a single threshold applied across models then favours the judge.

Judging two models that were neither of them, the main model and the twin ordered the remaining pair correctly in all six comparisons. That was the easy case: each such pair set a strong model against the short run, a gap of 0.31 to 0.53 in true accuracy. The only close comparison made by a judge not involved in it was the short run judging the main model against the twin, and it got their order wrong on three-digit carries, passing 0.100 of the main model's answers and 0.053 of the twin's against true rates of 0.357 and 0.513.

The usual advice is to use a judge from a different model family. These three models share their training data, so they cannot test that advice. What they do show is that a judge's verdict on itself is the least trustworthy thing it produces, and that a judge outside the contest is not automatically a good one. ## Correcting a judge with forty labels

### Prediction-powered inference

The repair that does not depend on finding an unrelated judge is a small set of answers checked by people. *Prediction-powered inference* (PPI), introduced by Angelopoulos and colleagues in 2023, uses the judge on every answer and the labels on a few. Let $f_i$ be the judge's verdict on answer $i$ (1 for pass, 0 for fail) over all $N$ answers, and let $Y_j$ be the true label on a random subset of $n$ answers. The estimate of the true accuracy is the judge's pass rate plus the judge's average error on the labelled answers,

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{PP}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \;+\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(Y_j - f_j\right).
$$

The second term, the *rectifier*, estimates how far the judge's pass rate is from the truth, and subtracting the bias in this way makes the estimate unbiased whatever the judge does. Its variance is roughly $\mathrm{Var}(f)/N + \mathrm{Var}(Y - f)/n$. When the judge agrees with the labels, $Y - f$ is mostly zero, the second variance is small, and the judge's large sample keeps the interval narrow. When the judge disagrees with the labels often, $Y - f$ can vary more than $Y$ itself, and the interval can be wider than one computed from the labels alone.

The *tuned* version, from the PPI++ paper of Angelopoulos, Duchi and Zrnic, multiplies the judge's terms by a weight $\lambda$ estimated from the labels. A weight of one gives plain PPI; a weight of zero ignores the judge and uses the labels alone. Estimating $\lambda$ lets the method lean on the judge only as far as the judge deserves.

### Coverage and width

The block below runs the study for one case: the main model judging its twin on three-digit carries, with 40 labelled answers drawn at random 1,000 times. For four estimators it prints the bias, the width of the nominal 95% interval, and its coverage: the judge alone, the 40 labels alone, plain PPI, and tuned PPI.

```python
models = {"clean": main, "leaky": sm.load(adjacent=True)}
sc = le.judge_scores(models, [("probe_carry", "add")])
v = le.judged(sc, "probe_carry/add", "clean", ["clean", "leaky"])
d = sc["probe_carry/add/clean/leaky"]     # the main model judges the twin
r = le.ppi_study(np.array(d["score"]) > v["threshold"],
                 np.array(d["ok"]), labeled=40, draws=1000)
print("true accuracy", round(r["target"], 3))
for k in ("judge", "labels", "ppi", "tuned"):
    print(f"{k:6s} bias {r[k]['bias']:+.3f}  width {r[k]['width']:.3f}"
          f"  coverage {r[k]['coverage']:.2f}")
```

```text
true accuracy 0.513
judge  bias -0.145  width 0.109  coverage 0.00
labels bias +0.002  width 0.306  coverage 0.94
ppi    bias -0.000  width 0.413  coverage 0.94
tuned  bias +0.002  width 0.304  coverage 0.93
```

The judge alone has a bias of -0.145, an underestimate of the twin's accuracy, and its interval is narrow, so it never covers the truth. The labels alone are unbiased but wide. Plain PPI removes the bias but is wider still, and tuned PPI is as narrow as the labels alone with similar coverage.

Across the three tasks, with the main model judging its own answers or the twin's, 300 judged answers and 40 labelled ones, the judge alone covered the true accuracy with its nominal 95% interval in at most 66% of 4,000 draws, and in one case in 0.0%. Its interval is narrow and in the wrong place: precision without accuracy. Prediction-powered inference covered the truth in 94% to 95% of draws. Where the judge was good, on the twin's multiplications, its interval was 0.19 wide against 0.29 for the 40 labels alone.

Where the judge was poor, it hurt. On three-digit carries the plain estimate's interval was 0.41 wide, against 0.31 from the labels alone, because correcting a noisy judge adds the judge's noise to the rectifier. The tuned version fell back to 0.30, about what the labels gave alone, and covered 94%. The table below collects the six cases.

Chapter 39 met the same structure from the other side. There a lender observed outcomes only for applicants it had approved, and a small sample that nobody had selected made the large selected one usable, provided the correction took the right form. Here the judged answers play the large, biased sample and the random labels the small, unselected one.

One technical caveat applies. The judge's pass threshold was set using all 900 true labels, a single number that is not independent of the labelled sample. Its effect on coverage is negligible at this size, but not zero.

## What Part XI measured

Part XI took the habits of the earlier parts of this book to models somebody else built and benchmarks somebody else wrote. A benchmark score was a sample of questions, and a question asked five ways was still close to one question (Chapter 51). Leaked answers raised the score on the leaked items while a loss-based detector barely noticed (Chapter 52). The prompt was part of the measurement, and the gap between two models depended on it (Chapter 53). Multiple choice measured the distractors as much as the model (Chapter 54). And a model asked to grade others graded itself highest (this chapter).

None of these needed a large model to show. Each is an older problem in new clothes: sampling error, leakage, a choice made on the test set, a metric that does not match the use, and labels that come from the system being judged. The tools that answered them in earlier parts answer them here: paired comparisons, fresh data, held-out selection, evaluation at the point of use, and a small random sample checked by people. Part XII turns to methods that work without labels, where there is no truth to grade against at all.

## In practice

- **Do not let a model grade itself or a close relative.** Self-preference survived a threshold calibrated on the truth in every case measured here.
- **Check any judge against a random sample graded by people,** and compute the judge's pass rate and the people's separately for each system being compared. A gap that differs between systems means the judge's ranking is partly a ranking of resemblance to the judge.
- **Use the labelled sample to correct the estimate, not only to decide whether to trust the judge.** Report the prediction-powered estimate, with its interval, alongside the judge's raw pass rate.
- **Prefer the tuned form of PPI.** It uses the judge only as far as the labels support; here, with a weak judge, its interval was no wider than the labels' alone, where plain PPI's was.
- **Distrust a narrow interval from a judge alone.** Its width reflects the number of judged answers, not the judge's error.

## Limits of the evidence

- **A likelihood judge.** Real judges are prompted, are often asked to compare two answers, and can reason before answering. Their biases include position and length as well as self-preference, and their sizes differ from these.
- **Exact answers.** Arithmetic made the truth free, which is what let every judge be checked. For open-ended outputs the "truth" is itself a human judgement with its own noise, and the labelled set in prediction-powered inference inherits that noise.
- **Three tiny models trained on the same data.** All three were trained on the same lines, and the main model and the twin differ only in their order, which is the case in which a judge is most likely to recognise a contestant. A judge from a different family might be biased differently or less, and that was not measured.
- **Fixed threshold.** A judge that outputs a score rather than a pass could be corrected by a calibration per model; that needs labels for each model, which is the prediction-powered recipe again.

## Key points

1. Each of three models, judging all three models' answers by its own likelihood with a threshold calibrated on the truth, ranked itself first on every task: 9 of 9.
2. The weakest model, right on 1.3% of three-digit carries, passed 73% of its own answers. A model's own greedy answers, right or wrong, look likely to it.
3. Judging the other two models, the strong judges always put a strong model above the far weaker one; the weak judge misordered the two close strong models on carries.
4. With 40 labelled answers, prediction-powered inference removed the bias and covered the truth in about 94% of draws; with a weak judge the plain version was wider than labels alone, and the tuned version was not.
5. Do not let a model grade itself or a close relative. Check any judge against a small random sample graded by people, and use that sample to correct its estimate, not only to decide whether to trust it.

## Further reading

- Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", *NeurIPS* (2023): agreement between model judges and human preferences, the position and length biases of judges, and tentative signs of self-preference.
- Panickssery, Bowman and Feng, "LLM evaluators recognize and favor their own generations", *NeurIPS* (2024): evidence that self-preference in large models rises with their ability to recognise their own outputs.
- Angelopoulos, Bates, Fannjiang, Jordan and Zrnic, "Prediction-powered inference", *Science* (2023): the method of combining many model predictions with a few labels to obtain valid confidence intervals.
- Angelopoulos, Duchi and Zrnic, "PPI++: efficient prediction-powered inference" (2023): the tuned version, which weights the predictions so that a poor predictor cannot make the interval worse than the labels alone.
