# The Applicants You Refused Are the Data You Do Not Have

Chapter 38 found that a missing value can record a decision. A missing label always does. A lender sees whether a loan was repaid only if it made the loan; a hospital sees whether a treatment worked only for patients who received it; a platform sees whether a recommendation was clicked only if it was shown. The next model is trained on those outcomes and then applied to everyone, including the kind of case the last decision turned away. The sentence that makes this feel routine is *a model trained on the outcomes you observed predicts the outcomes you did not.* Lakkaraju and colleagues called this situation the *selective labels problem*. If the sentence fails, a model can look well validated and still misprice exactly the cases it has never seen an outcome for.

This chapter builds a simulated lender with four worlds, varying whether the old decision used information the model lacks and whether risk is straight or curved in the features. It first shows that selection on the model's own features costs only misspecification. It then shows that selection on something the model cannot see miscalibrates it badly while leaving its ranking intact, and that validation on the accepted applicants cannot detect either problem reliably. Finally it measures the repair that works in every world: a small sample of applicants approved at random.

## A simulated lender

Each applicant has two features the model sees, $x_1$ and $x_2$, and a third, $u$, that it does not: something a loan officer reading the file might pick up. All three are standard normal. The chance of default depends on all three, through the log-odds

$$
-1.5 + 1.2\,x_1 + 0.8\,x_2 + 1.2\,u,
$$

with an extra term $0.8\,x_1^2$ in the two *curved* worlds; in the other two, risk is *straight* (linear) in the features.

An old policy accepted the half of each cohort with the lowest risk score, and outcomes exist only for them. The score was $1.2\,x_1 + 0.8\,x_2$ plus noise in two of the worlds. In the other two the officer's judgement of $u$ also went into it, as an added $1.2\,u$. A logistic model and boosted trees are trained on the accepted half of cohorts of 20,000 and applied to a fresh cohort, accepted and refused alike, so that their predictions for the refused can be compared with what the refused actually did. Each world is averaged over ten cohorts.

Two measures of quality matter here and must be kept apart. The *AUC*, the area under the ROC curve, is the probability that a randomly chosen defaulter receives a higher predicted risk than a randomly chosen repayer; it measures ranking only. *Calibration* asks whether predicted probabilities match observed rates: whether, among applicants given a particular risk, about that fraction default. A lender who prices loans by predicted probability needs calibration, not just ranking.

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

for r in rows.selective_labels():     # 10 cohorts of 20,000
    if r["model"] == "logistic":
        print(f"curved={r['curved']!s:5} officer={r['officer']!s:5} "
              f"refused default {r['refused_rate']:.3f}, predicted "
              f"{r['refused_pred']:.3f}; AUC {r['auc_all']:.3f}")
```

```text
curved=False officer=False refused default 0.432, predicted 0.453; AUC 0.770
curved=False officer=True  refused default 0.486, predicted 0.137; AUC 0.769
curved=True  officer=False refused default 0.538, predicted 0.238; AUC 0.559
curved=True  officer=True  refused default 0.597, predicted 0.136; AUC 0.450
```

In the simplest world the sentence holds. The policy used only what the model sees, risk is straight, and logistic regression predicts a default rate of 0.453 among the refused, who default at 0.432. Its AUC on the whole cohort, 0.770, is the same as that of a model trained on everyone's outcomes. The figure below compares predicted and actual default rates among the refused in all four worlds.

![When the officer saw more, the accepted-only model expected 0.137 of the refused to default; 0.486 did.](ml139-f0-refused.png){alt="Grouped bars for four worlds. In the first, all bars are near 0.43. In the straight-risk world where the officer saw more, the accepted-only bar is near 0.14 while the actual rate is about 0.49 and the other two bars about 0.39."}

## Selection on what the model sees costs only misspecification

The simplest world is Chapter 29's covariate shift in another form. *Covariate shift* is a change in the distribution of the inputs with the conditional distribution of the outcome given the inputs held fixed. Selecting applicants on the features changes which inputs the model sees, but not the relationship between inputs and outcome among them: for a given $x_1$ and $x_2$, an accepted applicant and a refused one have the same chance of default. A correctly specified model therefore learns the right relationship from the accepted half and carries it to the refused half. Chapter 29 found that covariate shift charges only for misspecification, and so does this.

The charge appears as soon as the model cannot extend what it learned. Boosted trees, trained on the same accepted half, predicted 0.337 for the refused. Trees split only within the range of inputs they were trained on, so beyond it their predictions stay flat; Chapter 15 measured this for forests. The refused sit beyond that range. In the curved world, logistic regression is the misspecified model, and it predicted 0.238 for a group that defaulted at 0.538. Its AUC on the whole cohort fell to 0.559, against 0.746 for the same model trained on everyone, probably because a straight line fitted to the accepted region of a curved relationship does not extend to the refused region.

## Selection on what the model cannot see miscalibrates it

When the officer's judgement went into the old decision, the accepted are not only the applicants with good features but, among applicants with similar features, the ones with good $u$. In the training data, then, applicants with mediocre features repaid well, because those who were accepted had something else in their favour. The model sees only the features and learns that mediocre features are safe. Formally, selection now depends on a variable that also affects the outcome, so the outcome's distribution given the features differs between the accepted and the refused, and data from the accepted alone cannot show by how much.

Logistic regression predicted a default rate of 0.137 for the refused. They defaulted at 0.486. Its ranking survived, an AUC of 0.769 against 0.770 for a model trained on everyone, because the selection shifted the level of its predictions more than their order. Its *log loss*, the average negative log probability assigned to the observed outcome, records the damage: 0.637 against 0.494. A lender who used its probabilities to price risk would price the refused applicants at less than a third of their real default rate.

### Two losses, one repairable

There are two losses here, and only one can be repaired. Even the model trained on every applicant's outcome, which no lender has, predicted 0.394 for the refused, below their 0.486. The refused were selected partly for a $u$ that the features do not contain, so no model of the features can know that they are worse than their features suggest. This is the subgroup miscalibration of Chapter 34, where a model calibrated on average was miscalibrated within groups, here produced by the decision itself. The gap from 0.137 to 0.394 is the *sample selection bias* Heckman described in 1979: the distortion of an estimated relationship when the sample is chosen by a process related to the outcome. That part can be fixed, given outcomes from outside the selection. The table below collects both models in all four worlds.

## Validation on the accepted can err in either direction

The same selection undermines the usual check. A model like this is validated on held-out accepted applicants, because they are the only ones with outcomes. In the straight world with a features-only policy, the AUC measured that way was 0.673, against 0.770 on the whole cohort. The accepted are a narrower, more similar group, and ranking within a group whose risks are close together is harder than ranking across the full range.

In the curved world it went the other way: 0.627 on the accepted against 0.559 on everyone, because the model fits the accepted region and fails outside it. In the curved world with the officer, the model ranked the whole cohort worse than chance, 0.450, while its validation on the accepted reported 0.610. Validation on the accepted can err in either direction, and nothing in it says which.

## Buying the missing outcomes with random approvals

The repair that works in every world is to stop selecting for some of the data. Suppose 5% of applicants, about 992 per cohort, had been approved at random, regardless of score and officer. The next block compares three predictions for the refused: a logistic model trained on those random approvals alone; the accepted-only model *recalibrated* on them, which keeps its coefficients but refits the intercept and slope of its log-odds; and the model trained on everyone.

```python
for r in rows.selective_labels():
    if r["model"] == "logistic":
        print(f"curved={r['curved']!s:5} officer={r['officer']!s:5} "
              f"5% random only: {r['explore_refused_pred']:.3f}; "
              f"recalibrated: {r['recal_refused_pred']:.3f}; "
              f"everyone: {r['oracle_refused_pred']:.3f}")
```

```text
curved=False officer=False 5% random only: 0.424; recalibrated: 0.425; everyone: 0.430
curved=False officer=True  5% random only: 0.389; recalibrated: 0.390; everyone: 0.394
curved=True  officer=False 5% random only: 0.531; recalibrated: 0.383; everyone: 0.530
curved=True  officer=True  5% random only: 0.493; recalibrated: 0.378; everyone: 0.492
```

A logistic model trained on those thousand or so randomly approved applicants alone predicted 0.389 for the refused in the officer world and 0.531 in the curved one, matching the model trained on everyone to within 0.01 in all four worlds. The random approvals are a sample from the whole population, so the relationship learned from them is the population's, not the accepted half's.

Recalibration worked when the shape of the model was right (0.390) and not when it was wrong (0.383 in the curved world, against 0.530). Refitting an intercept and a slope can correct a shift in the level and scale of the log-odds, which is what selection on $u$ produced. It keeps the direction in the features that the accepted-only model learned, and in the curved world that direction, fitted to the accepted region, probably does not suit the refused one; the straight model trained on everyone, with different coefficients, did.

Approving some applicants at random has a cost, paid in defaults among applicants who would have been refused. It is, however, the only source of outcomes from the refused region. The alternatives, collectively called *reject inference*, have to assume what the refused would have done. Hand and Henley's verdict in 1993 was that reject inference cannot work reliably unless assumptions or information from outside the accepted data are brought in; the exploration sample is that information, measured rather than assumed. The next chapter turns to a leak that arises not from how rows were selected but from how a feature is built from their labels.

## In practice

- **List every input to the decision that produced your labels**, and mark the ones the model does not see. If the list has unmarked entries only, expect selection to cost only misspecification; otherwise expect miscalibration on the refused.
- **Do not trust AUC on accepted cases as a measure for everyone.** In this chapter it was too low in one world and too high in another.
- **Check calibration, not only ranking.** Selection on unseen information left the AUC intact while the predicted default rate for the refused was less than a third of the real one.
- **Prefer models that extrapolate sensibly** when they will be applied beyond the accepted region; trees cannot predict beyond the range they were trained on, and a misspecified linear model extrapolated badly too.
- **Budget for a random exploration sample.** A few per cent of decisions made at random gives outcomes from outside the policy's approvals, enough here to match a model trained on everyone. Use it to train the model, or at least to check it; recalibrating on it repaired the accepted-only model only where its shape was right.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One officer, one unobserved variable.** Real decisions use many unrecorded signals, some correlated with the recorded features. The size of the bias depends on how much the decision used that the model cannot see, which is rarely known.
- **Outcomes are clean.** A loan's outcome takes years and depends on the terms offered, which the policy also chose. The same structure holds for treatments and recommendations, with their own versions of these complications.
- **Random approvals of 5%.** Fewer would give noisier estimates. In practice exploration is often stratified, approving at random only near the decision boundary, which costs less and reaches less of the refused region.
- **Static.** Each new model sets the next policy, which selects the next training data. Feedback over several rounds can compound the bias measured here, and is not simulated.

## Key points

1. Selection on the features the model sees costs only misspecification: a correctly specified model predicted 0.453 for refused applicants who defaulted at 0.432; boosted trees, which cannot extrapolate, 0.337.
2. Selection on what the model cannot see miscalibrates it without warning: 0.137 predicted for a group defaulting at 0.486, with its AUC intact.
3. Part of that loss cannot be repaired by any model of the observed features: even a model trained on everyone predicted 0.394.
4. AUC on accepted applicants was 0.673 against 0.770 on everyone in one world, and 0.627 against 0.559 in another.
5. A small randomly approved sample recovered what a model trained on everyone would know. Find out what information the past decision used, and buy some outcomes from outside its approvals.
