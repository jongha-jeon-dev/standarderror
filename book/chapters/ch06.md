# Linear Models

Linear models are the ones practitioners reach for first, and the ones whose textbook properties are stated most precisely. A logistic regression has maximum-likelihood coefficients with standard errors; a lasso has a penalty strength and a selected set of features; a model of any kind sits somewhere on a bias–variance curve and should have fewer parameters than rows. Those precise statements are what make linear models the cleanest place to see such properties fail, because each one rests on conditions the textbook states once and the practitioner rarely checks. This chapter opens Part II, which pushes the models people use most past those conditions, and it starts with the simplest of them.

Each section tests one sentence that is close enough to true to be repeated:

- Section 6.1: *logistic regression finds the maximum-likelihood coefficients.*
- Section 6.2: *regularisation strength is a property of the model.*
- Section 6.3: *lasso selects the relevant features.*
- Section 6.4: *test error is bias squared plus variance, and the trade-off is a U.*
- Section 6.5: *more parameters than data points means overfitting.*

A practitioner meets these sentences at the moments that matter most: when a coefficient is turned into an odds ratio, when a lasso's non-zero coefficients are reported as the variables that drive an outcome, when a model is tuned, and when a model is judged too big for its data.

The sections build on one another through a single thread, regularisation, and whether it is chosen deliberately or left to something nobody chose. Section 6.1 shows that on separable data the maximum-likelihood logistic fit does not exist, so the coefficients a library returns are set by its stopping rule, and that a penalty is what restores an answer. Section 6.2 asks what a penalty is a penalty *on*, and finds that its price depends on the units each feature happens to be recorded in, so that a lasso's selected set changes with the data dictionary. Section 6.3 holds the units fixed and asks whether cross-validated lasso, tuned to predict, keeps the features that are actually relevant; it compares the cross-validation minimum with the stricter one-standard-error rule and measures the stability of each selection and its sign with the bootstrap.

The last two sections widen the view from the penalty to the knobs a practitioner turns in general. Section 6.4 measures the bias–variance decomposition exactly on a simulation and steps briefly outside linear models to do it: the number of neighbours in k-nearest neighbours draws the textbook U, while the number of trees in a random forest draws none. Section 6.5 returns to least squares, on random features, and goes past the point where the model fits its training data exactly. The test error peaks there and falls again, and a ridge penalty removes both the peak and the need to go past it. The minimum-norm fit behind that second descent turns out to be implicitly regularised, in the same way as the gradient descent of Section 6.1, which closes the chapter where it began.

## On Separable Data, Your Logistic Coefficients Are Your Stopping Rule

<!-- source: ml-6-separable; hero: ml106-hero.png -->


Logistic regression is taught as maximum likelihood: *logistic regression finds the maximum-likelihood coefficients.* A practitioner relies on this sentence whenever a fitted coefficient is read as an effect, turned into an odds ratio or given a standard error, and whenever a predicted probability is passed on as a probability. Each use assumes there is one best coefficient vector and that the software found it. If there is none, the numbers that come back are set by something the analyst did not choose.

This section explains why the maximum-likelihood estimate does not exist when a hyperplane separates the classes, shows that this is the normal case on a standard teaching dataset, and then measures what scikit-learn returns, where gradient descent goes instead, and how a penalty restores an answer.

### When the likelihood has no maximum


#### The likelihood and the scaling argument

For binary labels $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and feature vectors $x_i$ (with a column of ones for the intercept), logistic regression models $P(y_i = 1 \mid x_i) = \sigma(x_i^\top w)$, where $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ is the logistic function. The fitted coefficients are the $w$ that maximise the log-likelihood

$$
\ell(w) = \sum_i \Big[ y_i \log \sigma(x_i^\top w) + (1 - y_i) \log\big(1 - \sigma(x_i^\top w)\big) \Big].
$$

Every term is the log of a probability, so $\ell(w) \le 0$. Its negative, averaged over rows, is the *log loss*: the average negative log of the probability given to the label that occurred.

The sentence assumes a maximum exists. It does not when the training data are *linearly separable*: some hyperplane has every positive row strictly on one side and every negative row on the other. Take a separating $w$, so that $x_i^\top w > 0$ for positives and $< 0$ for negatives, and replace it by $c\,w$ with $c > 1$. Every positive row's probability $\sigma(c\,x_i^\top w)$ moves towards 1 and every negative row's towards 0, so every term of the likelihood increases, and goes on increasing as $c$ grows. The likelihood approaches its bound and never reaches it: there is a supremum but no maximiser, and an optimiser climbing it drives the coefficients towards infinity. Albert and Anderson worked out the conditions for existence in 1984.

#### Separation is the expected case on informative data

This sounds like an edge case for tiny datasets. The code splits the Wisconsin diagnostic breast cancer data, bundled with scikit-learn, in half fifty times, stratified by class, and checks whether each training half is separable.

```python
from standarderror.ml import linear as ln

print(f"half splits separable: {ln.separable_fraction(splits=50):.0%}")
```

```text
half splits separable: 100%
```

Every stratified half, 284 tumours with 30 measurements, is perfectly separated by some hyperplane. With 30 features this informative that is the expected situation, not a fluke, and it is the informativeness, not the number of features per row, that produces it.

Cover counted the contribution of the feature count in 1965. For points in *general position* (no more on any hyperplane than must be) in $d$ dimensions with random labels, a separating hyperplane exists with high probability below about $2d$ points and with high probability does not above it, the switch sharpening as $d$ grows. Here $d$ is 31 with the intercept and there are 284 rows, far past $2d$, so random labels would not be separable. The real labels are not random: a modest difference between the classes in each of 30 features adds up to a margin in the combined space. The better a feature set predicts, the more likely the textbook estimator is not to exist. Maximum likelihood is expected to struggle on hard problems; here it fails on easy ones.

### What the library returns when there is no maximum


An optimiser does not know the maximum is missing. It iterates until the change in the objective or the gradient falls below a *stopping tolerance*, and returns wherever it is. The code fits unpenalised logistic regression in scikit-learn to one training half at five tolerances and reports iterations, the Euclidean norm of the coefficients and the held-out log loss.

```python
for r in ln.tolerance_sweep():
    print(f"tol {r['tol']:.0e}  {r['iterations']:>3} iterations  "
          f"norm {r['norm']:8.1f}  test log loss {r['test_log_loss']:.3f}")
```

```text
tol 1e-10   52 iterations  norm   1288.9  test log loss 1.575
```

The coefficient norm runs from 2.4 to 1,289, a factor of 548, set entirely by when the optimiser was told it was close enough. The gradient shrinks towards zero as the coefficients grow along a separating direction, so a tighter tolerance only lets the optimiser travel further along a path with no end.

At the library's default tolerance the model puts 95% of training rows within one in a million of certainty, and its held-out log loss is 1.47 against 0.08 for the loosest tolerance. Log loss charges $-\log p$ for probability $p$ on the true label, so a few confident mistakes dominate it: a log loss that size from a model right 95.4% of the time means its mistakes are made with near-total confidence. Even its accuracy is lower, 0.954 against 0.982. The loosest tolerance stopped after five iterations, and stopping early is a regulariser: it keeps the coefficients small, as a penalty would.

Most users never see this, because scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` is penalised by default, with an L2 penalty of inverse strength $C = 1$ that makes the optimum exist. The bottom row of the table below is that default, and it is the best of the six on log loss. The sentence is true of what the library does by default and false of the estimator the textbook describes.

### Where gradient descent goes instead


#### The norm grows without limit, logarithmically

Plain gradient descent on the unpenalised loss, from zero, does something definite even so. The next experiment uses the 1s and 8s of the UCI digits, whose training half is also separable, runs 100,000 steps of size 1, and records the weight norm, the training loss and the angle to scikit-learn's *hard-margin support vector machine* (SVM). Among all separating hyperplanes, the hard-margin SVM chooses the one with the largest *margin*, the distance to the nearest training point; equivalently it minimises $\lVert w \rVert^2$ subject to $\tilde y_i\,(x_i^\top w + b) \ge 1$ for every row, with $\tilde y_i = \pm 1$, $x_i$ here without the column of ones, and the intercept $b$ left out of the norm. Its direction is the *max-margin direction*.

```python
g = ln.gd_path(steps=100_000)
for r in g["rows"][::6]:
    print(f"step {r['step']:>6,}  norm {r['norm']:6.2f}  "
          f"angle {r['angle']:5.1f}  train loss {r['train_loss']:.1e}")
print(f"growth per tenfold steps: {ln.norm_per_decade(g['rows']):.2f}")
```

```text

step     10  norm   1.87  angle  29.5  train loss 8.7e-02
step     62  norm   3.43  angle  18.8  train loss 2.5e-02
step    366  norm   5.56  angle  13.9  train loss 5.2e-03
step  2,154  norm   7.96  angle  11.1  train loss 9.6e-04
step 12,663  norm  10.49  angle   9.4  train loss 1.7e-04
step 74,438  norm  13.08  angle   8.3  train loss 3.0e-05
growth per tenfold steps: 3.32
```

The training loss falls towards zero and the norm grows by about 3.3 per tenfold increase in steps: logarithmic growth, $\lVert w_t \rVert \propto \log t$, the rate Soudry and colleagues proved. It is slow because once every row is correct the gradient is dominated by the rows nearest the boundary, whose contribution decays exponentially in their margin, so each further increase in the norm is bought with exponentially smaller gradients. It never stops, as the figure below shows.

#### The direction converges, to the max-margin direction of the problem posed

What converges is the *direction*, $w_t / \lVert w_t \rVert$. Because the nearest rows dominate the gradient, the iterate is pushed to raise the smallest margins, and its direction tends to the max-margin one. The angle to the SVM falls from 46 degrees to 8.1 after 100,000 steps.

It will not reach zero. This gradient descent treats the intercept as one more weight on a column of ones, so it maximises the margin of the augmented problem, in which the intercept counts towards the norm; the SVM leaves its intercept free. On these digits the two max-margin directions are 5.9 degrees apart, and that is where the angle is heading, slowly. In the limit, unpenalised logistic regression trained by gradient descent is a hard-margin SVM for the problem as the optimiser poses it: a fact about the optimiser and its parametrisation, not about the likelihood, which approaches the same bound along every separating direction.

#### Accuracy and log loss part company

Accuracy depends only on the direction, since scaling $w$ does not change the sign of $x^\top w$, and on the test set it improves from 0.910 to 0.978 as the direction nears the max-margin one. Log loss depends on the scale too: as the norm grows, every prediction grows more confident, including the wrong ones. Test log loss reaches its minimum, 0.0626, at step 661 and then rises to 0.0830. The direction keeps improving and the confidence gets worse faster, as the figure below shows.

![Test log loss is lowest, 0.0626, at step 661 and 0.0830 at 100,000. Accuracy climbs to 0.978 as the direction nears the max-margin one.](ml106-f1-test.png){alt="A test log loss that falls to a minimum and then rises, and a dashed test accuracy that climbs in steps towards a dotted line."}

Section 8.1 finds the same split in boosting: test log loss lowest within the first few dozen rounds, test error never turning upwards after round 30.

### A penalty makes the question well posed


An L2 penalty adds $\lVert w \rVert^2 / (2C)$ to the negative log-likelihood; a larger $C$ is a weaker penalty. The penalty grows without bound while the likelihood gain from scaling is bounded, so the objective has a finite minimiser for any $C$, unique because it is strictly convex. The answer then depends on $C$, which at least someone chose. The code sweeps $C$ upwards in tenfold steps on the breast cancer split.

```python
for r in ln.c_path():
    print(f"C {r['C']:8.0e}  norm {r['norm']:7.2f}  "
          f"test accuracy {r['test_accuracy']:.3f}  "
          f"test log loss {r['test_log_loss']:.3f}")
```

```text

C    1e-01  norm    1.56  test accuracy 0.975  test log loss 0.110

C    1e+01  norm    7.86  test accuracy 0.968  test log loss 0.095
C    1e+02  norm   23.21  test accuracy 0.958  test log loss 0.301
```

The norm grows with $C$ without limit, roughly logarithmically again, because the problem underneath has no ceiling. Test log loss is best at $C = 1$, at 0.067, and degrades steadily beyond it. Choosing $C$ by cross-validation is choosing how far along that unbounded path to stop: the decision the tolerance made silently, made out loud.

#### Reading a coefficient under separation

Under separation a coefficient's size measures how long the optimiser ran, or how weak the penalty was, not an effect. Its sign and its ratio to the other coefficients carry information, because together they are the direction, and the direction converges. Its magnitude does not, and nor does anything computed from it, such as the *odds ratio* $e^{w_j}$, the factor multiplying the odds of the positive class per unit of feature $j$.

The next section asks whether the penalty strength that makes the answer exist is a property of the model at all, or of the units its features are recorded in.

## Change a Feature's Units and the Lasso Changes Its Mind

<!-- source: ml-7-units; hero: ml107-hero.png -->


Section 6.1 ended with a penalty as what turns an unanswerable question into one with an answer, an answer that then depends on the penalty's strength. Penalised regression is usually taught with that strength as a property of the model: *regularisation strength is a property of the model*, a single dial, λ, that says how much to shrink. A practitioner meets this sentence whenever a lasso is used to choose variables and its selected set is reported as a finding, "these are the measurements that matter". If the sentence is false, the selected set depends on something other than the data and the model: on the units in which each feature happened to be recorded. Two analysts with the same patients and the same outcome, one recording age in years and the other in decades, would then report different lists of important variables, and nothing in the cross-validated error would tell them which list to believe.

This section asks what the penalty is a penalty *on*. It first shows algebraically that rescaling one feature is the same as giving that feature its own penalty. It then measures the effect on the diabetes data by rescaling each feature in turn, checks whether the resulting models differ in predictive accuracy, and finally examines standardisation, the standard remedy, on a simulated binary feature.

### A penalty is a price per unit of coefficient


#### The lasso objective

The *lasso* (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) fits a linear model by minimising the squared error plus λ times the sum of the absolute coefficients. In the form scikit-learn uses,

$$
\min_{\beta_0, \beta} \; \frac{1}{2n} \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - \beta_0 - x_i^\top \beta\big)^2 \; + \; \lambda \sum_{j} |\beta_j| .
$$

The absolute-value charge is what makes the lasso select. Its slope does not shrink to zero as a coefficient approaches zero, so a feature whose contribution to the fit is not worth its price ends up with a coefficient of exactly zero, and drops out of the model. The usual teaching presents λ as "how much regularisation", chosen by cross-validation, with a footnote saying to standardise the features first.

#### Rescaling a feature is a private penalty

The footnote is the whole story. The price λ is charged per unit of *coefficient*, and a coefficient's size depends on the units of its feature. Measure age in decades instead of years and the coefficient that describes the same effect is ten times bigger, so it costs ten times as much. Nothing about the data or the relationship has changed; only the bill has.

The arithmetic takes one line. Multiply feature $j$'s column by a factor $k$. The coefficient that gives exactly the same predictions is then $\beta_j / k$, and its penalty becomes

$$
\lambda \frac{|\beta_j|}{k}.
$$

A finer unit ($k > 1$) makes the feature cheaper; a coarser one ($k < 1$) makes it dearer. Everything else in the objective, the fit to the data and the other features' prices, is unchanged. Rescaling one feature's units is therefore exactly equivalent to leaving the units alone and giving that one feature its own penalty, $\lambda / k$. No analyst would set per-feature penalties by consulting the units in a data dictionary, but that is what a lasso on unstandardised features does.

### On the diabetes data, the units decide seven features


The diabetes data of Efron and colleagues, bundled with scikit-learn, has 442 patients, ten baseline measurements and a disease-progression score, with the features already standardised. The experiment takes one feature at a time, multiplies its column by a factor from 0.01 to 100, which is the same as recording that one measurement in a coarser or finer unit, and refits *cross-validated lasso*: `LassoCV`, which chooses λ afresh each time by five-fold cross-validation over a grid of values. Each of the ten features is rescaled at nine factors, ninety refits in all.

```python

print("factors " + " ".join(f"{k:g}" for k in u["factors"]))
```

A `#` means the feature was kept and a `.` that it was dropped; the middle column, factor 1, is the standardised model. **Seven of the ten features change status with their units.** Every row that changes runs the same way, dropped at small factors and kept at large ones, as the algebra predicts: a small factor is a coarse unit and a high price. Age is dropped at every factor up to 0.3 and kept from 1 upward. s3, one of the blood serum measurements, is out of the standardised model and in it at a factor of 3.

Only sex, bmi and bp are kept whatever their units, and they are not simply the strongest features. In the standardised fit the largest coefficient belongs to s5, at +31.8, and s5 is dropped at a factor of 0.01. A coefficient that is large when measured in standard deviations is no protection once a coarse enough unit makes each unit of it expensive. The figure below shows the full grid.

If the different selections predicted differently, the units would at least be choosing between better and worse models, and cross-validation could arbitrate. The code reports the range of cross-validated mean squared error across all ninety refits beside the base, standardised model's.


Across the ninety refits the cross-validated error spans 5.3% of the base model's. Within that range, the coarse-unit models that drop age actually score a touch *better*. The data cannot tell these models apart. What told them apart was the scale each feature happened to be recorded in, which is arbitrary.

That is the practical risk. A lasso's selected set is routinely reported as a finding, and in fields with mixed units, such as a dose in milligrams, an age in years, a count and an indicator, the finding partly reflects which units the data dictionary used. The predictive model is fine. The list of "important" variables is not a property of the disease. The table below sets the selections beside their errors.

### Standardising is one choice of units, not the absence of one


#### Why standardisation does not remove the choice

The standard fix is to *standardise* every feature: subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation, so that each has unit variance. This removes the arbitrariness of the data dictionary by imposing one specific choice, in which every feature is measured in its own standard deviations. That is a choice of units like any other, and it has consequences for features whose standard deviation means something unusual. Binary features are the clearest case. A 0/1 feature that equals 1 with probability $p$ has standard deviation

$$
\sqrt{p(1-p)},
$$

which shrinks as the feature becomes rarer.

#### A rare binary feature, standardised and raw

The simulation places a binary feature with a fixed effect of 0.3 on the outcome per occurrence beside five continuous features, two of which have effects of 0.5, with unit noise and 500 rows per dataset. The binary feature's prevalence runs from 50% down to 2%, and at each prevalence 40 datasets are drawn. Cross-validated lasso is fitted twice to each: once with every column standardised, and once with the binary column only *centred* (its mean subtracted, its scale left as 0/1). The code reports the fraction of datasets in which the binary feature was kept.

```python
for r in ln.binary_prevalence(effect=0.3, reps=40):
    print(f"prevalence {r['prevalence']:4.0%}  kept: standardised "
          f"{r['kept_standardised']:.2f}   raw 0/1 {r['kept_raw']:.2f}")
```


At 50% prevalence both versions keep the feature nearly always. At 2%, the standardised lasso keeps it in 24 of 40 datasets and the raw one in 7, and the gap widens steadily as the feature becomes rarer, as the figure below shows.

The mechanism is the rescaling argument again. Standardising divides a rare indicator by its small standard deviation, which inflates its column; by the algebra above, that shrinks the coefficient needed to describe the same effect and so lowers its price. The raw version charges the effect per occurrence, and a rare occurrence has little data behind it: with few rows equal to 1, including the feature reduces the squared error only a little, and the reduction often fails to cover the price.

![At 2% prevalence the same feature is kept in 24 of 40 datasets standardised and 7 of 40 as 0/1. Neither is the neutral choice; there is not one.](ml107-f1-binary.png){alt="Two falling lines; the standardised one stays higher, and the gap widens as the feature gets rarer."}

Neither version is right. An effect per occurrence and an effect per standard deviation answer different questions, and the lasso answers whichever question its units ask. Gelman's suggestion, to divide continuous inputs by two standard deviations so that their coefficients compare with those of untouched binary inputs, is one principled answer to how the two kinds of feature should be priced against each other; it is still a choice.

The units are not the only thing that makes a lasso's selected set an unreliable finding. The next section holds the units fixed and asks whether cross-validated lasso, tuned to predict, keeps the features that are actually relevant.

## Cross-Validated Lasso Is Tuned to Predict, Not to Select

<!-- source: ml-8-selection; hero: ml108-hero.png -->


Section 6.2 showed that a lasso's selections depend on the units of its features. That matters because of what the selections are used for. The lasso is fitted for two reasons. One is prediction. The other, at least as common, is variable selection, resting on the sentence *lasso selects the relevant features.* A practitioner meets it whenever the lasso's non-zero coefficients are reported as the variables that drive an outcome, used to decide which measurements to keep collecting, or passed to a second analysis as the shortlist. If the sentence is false in the way the lasso is actually run, with its penalty chosen by cross-validation, the shortlist contains features with no effect, can name a correlated stand-in in place of the real one, and can carry coefficients whose signs flip from one resample of the data to the next.

This section holds the units fixed and asks whether the features the lasso keeps are the ones that matter. It first runs a simulation in which the truth is known and compares two ways of choosing the penalty: the cross-validation minimum and the stricter one-standard-error rule. It then explains, from the geometry of the L1 penalty, why neither rule can tell a feature from a near-copy of it. Finally it turns to the diabetes data, where the truth is unknown but the stability of each selection, and of its sign, can be measured with the bootstrap.

### Two jobs, one penalty


#### What the theory promises

The sentence has theory behind it. A selection method is *selection-consistent* if, as the number of rows grows, the probability that it picks exactly the true set of relevant features tends to one. Zhao and Yu showed in 2006 that the lasso is selection-consistent under an *irrepresentable condition*, which says, roughly, that the irrelevant features must not be too well explained by the relevant ones, and provided the penalty is chosen at a suitable rate. In practice the penalty is not chosen at that rate. It is chosen by cross-validation, and cross-validation chooses it to *predict*: it picks the penalty whose out-of-fold squared error is smallest. Whether the penalty that predicts best is also the penalty that selects correctly is an empirical question.

#### A simulation where the truth is known

The simulation separates the two jobs. Each dataset has ten standard-normal features and 100 rows, with unit noise. Features a and c have effects of 0.5. Feature b has no effect but is correlated with a at a chosen correlation ρ. The other seven features are pure noise. Sixty datasets are drawn at each correlation, and cross-validated lasso is fitted at the penalty that minimises cross-validated error. The code reports how often a and b are kept, how often b is kept without a, and how many of the seven noise features are kept on average.

```python
from standarderror.ml import linear as ln

# a, c: effect 0.5.  b: no effect, corr(a, b) = rho.  7 pure noise.
for r in ln.correlated_pair(rule="min"):
    print(f"rho {r['rho']:4.2f}  keeps a {r['a']:.2f}  b {r['b']:.2f}"
          f"  b-not-a {r['b_only']:.2f}  noise {r['noise_kept']:.2f} of 7")
```

```text
rho 0.50  keeps a 1.00  b 0.38  b-not-a 0.00  noise 2.78 of 7
rho 0.80  keeps a 1.00  b 0.32  b-not-a 0.00  noise 2.77 of 7
rho 0.90  keeps a 0.97  b 0.33  b-not-a 0.03  noise 2.80 of 7
rho 0.95  keeps a 0.93  b 0.33  b-not-a 0.07  noise 2.77 of 7
rho 0.99  keeps a 0.78  b 0.35  b-not-a 0.22  noise 2.73 of 7
```

The relevant feature a is kept nearly always until a and b become near-copies. The rest is not what "selects the relevant features" promises. On average the lasso keeps **2.8 of the 7 noise features**, and it keeps the irrelevant b about a third of the time at every correlation: at ρ = 0 as much as at ρ = 0.95. At the cross-validation minimum b is simply one more noise feature that receives a small coefficient, whatever its correlation with a. At ρ = 0.99 the lasso keeps b *instead of* a in 22% of datasets.

None of this is a malfunction. Giving a noise feature a small coefficient costs prediction almost nothing, and a slightly smaller penalty fits the real effects a little better, because every lasso coefficient, real ones included, is shrunk towards zero by an amount that grows with the penalty. The cross-validation minimum therefore lands at a penalty light enough that a few passengers get on. The figure below shows these rates beside those of the stricter rule introduced next.

![At the CV minimum the irrelevant b is kept 35% of the time even when it is uncorrelated with anything. The 1-SE rule fixes that and not the rest: at correlation 0.99 it keeps b 38% of the time and a only 70%.](ml108-f0-pair.png){alt="Four lines: the relevant feature near 1 falling at high correlation; the irrelevant one near a third for the CV minimum at every correlation, and rising from near zero with correlation under the 1-SE rule."}

### A stricter penalty, and what it cannot fix


#### The one-standard-error rule

The standard remedy when selection is the goal is the *one-standard-error rule* (1-SE rule). Cross-validation gives, for each candidate penalty, a mean error over the folds and a standard error of that mean. The 1-SE rule takes the largest penalty whose mean cross-validated error is within one standard error of the minimum. It deliberately gives up a little prediction, an amount the cross-validation cannot distinguish from zero, in exchange for a sparser model. The code repeats the simulation under this rule.

```python
for r in ln.correlated_pair(rule="1se"):
    print(f"rho {r['rho']:4.2f}  keeps a {r['a']:.2f}  b {r['b']:.2f}"
          f"  b-not-a {r['b_only']:.2f}  noise {r['noise_kept']:.2f} of 7")
```

```text

rho 0.50  keeps a 0.93  b 0.08  b-not-a 0.00  noise 0.23 of 7
rho 0.80  keeps a 0.93  b 0.17  b-not-a 0.00  noise 0.23 of 7
rho 0.90  keeps a 0.88  b 0.20  b-not-a 0.05  noise 0.25 of 7
rho 0.95  keeps a 0.85  b 0.28  b-not-a 0.08  noise 0.25 of 7
rho 0.99  keeps a 0.70  b 0.38  b-not-a 0.23  noise 0.27 of 7
```

The passengers mostly get off: 0.2 noise features on average, and b is kept only 2% of the time when it is uncorrelated with a. But the stand-in problem gets *worse* as the correlation rises. At ρ = 0.99, b is kept 38% of the time and a only 70%. Neither rule reliably tells two near-identical columns apart at 100 rows. The lasso does favour the real feature, keeping a without b in 55% of datasets at ρ = 0.99 and b without a in 23%, but in roughly one dataset in four a report of "the selected features" names the stand-in instead of the real effect.

#### Why near-copies cannot be told apart

The geometry explains why. When two columns are nearly identical, the fitted values depend almost entirely on the *sum* of their coefficients, $\beta_a + \beta_b$, and hardly at all on how the sum is split between them. The L1 penalty charges $|\beta_a| + |\beta_b|$, which for coefficients of the same sign is also just the sum. Every split of the shared effect between a and b therefore fits about equally well and costs exactly the same penalty. The objective is nearly flat along that line, and the L1 penalty, unlike a squared one, does nothing to favour the middle of it. The slight tilt the sample happens to give the fit therefore pushes the solution towards one end, often with one coefficient at zero, and which end is decided by small accidents of the sample.

*Ridge* regression, whose penalty is $\beta_a^2 + \beta_b^2$, behaves differently: for a fixed sum, the squared penalty is smallest when the two are equal, so ridge strictly prefers an even split and keeps both. The *elastic net*, whose penalty is a weighted mix of the L1 and squared penalties, combines the two precisely to recover that grouping of correlated features while still setting some coefficients to zero. Neither identifies which of the two is the real one. With ρ = 0.99 and 100 rows the data contain little information about that, and no penalty can create information the data do not hold.

#### Sparser is not better on both counts

The 1-SE rule also costs the relevant features something even when there is no stand-in. At ρ = 0 it keeps a in 93% of datasets, against 100% at the cross-validation minimum, and c, which is correlated with nothing, in 92% against 100%. A larger penalty shrinks every coefficient, and an effect of 0.5 in 100 noisy rows is not always large enough to survive it. A sparser model means fewer *false positives* (irrelevant features kept) and more *false negatives* (relevant features dropped); no penalty setting gives fewer of both. The table below collects both rules.

The penalty in this section is a knob that trades one kind of error for another. The next section asks whether the textbook picture of such trade-offs, a U-shaped curve of test error against model complexity, belongs to complexity itself or to the particular knob being turned.

## The Bias–Variance U Belongs to a Knob, Not to Complexity

<!-- source: ml-9-bias-variance; hero: ml109-hero.png -->


The penalty of the first three sections is one of many knobs that trade one kind of error for another, and the usual picture of such trades is drawn as often as any in machine learning: test error plotted against model complexity, a falling bias curve, a rising variance curve, and their sum forming a U whose bottom marks the model to choose. Behind it is the sentence *test error is bias squared plus variance, and the trade-off is a U.* A practitioner meets it whenever a model is tuned, and especially whenever the worry "this model is too big, it will overfit" decides whether to add capacity. If the U belongs to complexity in general, every knob that adds capacity should eventually hurt; if it belongs to particular knobs, effort spent guarding the others against overfitting is wasted. The picture is usually drawn, not measured.

This section measures it, and to do so it steps briefly outside linear models, to two knobs whose effects are easy to isolate. It sets out the bias–variance decomposition on a simulated regression where each term can be computed, then turns the two knobs: the number of neighbours in k-nearest neighbours, which draws the U, and the number of trees in a random forest, which does not. It explains the forest with the variance of an average of correlated predictors, and closes on what distinguishes the two kinds of knob.

### The decomposition, measured exactly


#### Bias, variance and noise

Suppose labels are generated as $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, where $f$ is the true function and $\varepsilon$ is noise with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$. A model trained on a random training set $D$ produces a prediction $\hat f_D(x)$, which varies with $D$. Write $\bar f(x)$ for its average over training sets. For squared error, the expected test error at a point $x$, averaged over training sets and over the noise in the test label, splits exactly into three terms:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat f_D(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\bar f(x) - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f_D(x) - \bar f(x))^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.
$$

The **bias** is the distance of the average prediction from the truth: how wrong the model is systematically. The **variance** is the spread of predictions across training sets: how much the model depends on which sample it saw. The **noise** is the irreducible floor, which no model can beat. The split is an identity in expectation, because the cross terms vanish: the test noise is independent of the training set, and deviations from the average prediction have mean zero.

#### A simulation in which every term is known

On real data there is one training set and the true function is unknown, so neither bias nor variance can be computed. On simulated data both can: fix a target, draw many training sets, fit the model to each, and record its predictions at fixed test points.

The target here is $f(x) = \sin(2\pi x) + x/2$ on $[0, 1]$, with $x$ uniform and normal noise of standard deviation 0.4, so the noise term is $0.4^2 = 0.16$. Each training set has 60 points. The terms are averaged over 200 evenly spaced test points, and the measured test error is computed separately on noisy test labels, so that the identity can be checked rather than assumed.

### The number of neighbours draws a U


The first knob is $k$ in a *k-nearest-neighbours regression*, which predicts at $x$ the average label of the $k$ training points nearest to $x$. The code fits it for six values of $k$ on 300 training sets.

```python
from standarderror.ml import linear as ln

# 300 training sets of 60 points; terms averaged over 200 test x
for r in ln.knn_sweep(ks=(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32)):
    print(f"k {r['k']:>2}  bias2 {r['bias2']:.4f}  var {r['variance']:.4f}"
          f"  noise {r['noise']:.2f}  sum {r['total']:.4f}"
          f"  measured {r['mse']:.4f}")
```

```text

k  2  bias2 0.0002  var 0.0834  noise 0.16  sum 0.2436  measured 0.2420
k  4  bias2 0.0005  var 0.0446  noise 0.16  sum 0.2051  measured 0.2034
k  8  bias2 0.0044  var 0.0271  noise 0.16  sum 0.1915  measured 0.1893
k 16  bias2 0.0295  var 0.0186  noise 0.16  sum 0.2081  measured 0.2058
k 32  bias2 0.0918  var 0.0167  noise 0.16  sum 0.2685  measured 0.2657
```

This is the textbook picture. At $k = 1$ the model copies the nearest training label, noise and all. Its variance, 0.161, is about the noise variance itself, because each prediction inherits one label's noise; its bias is almost nothing, because the nearest point is close and the target is smooth on that scale. At $k = 32$ the model averages over half the training set. Averaging many labels cancels most of their noise, so the variance drops to 0.017, but an average over such a wide window flattens the sine wave's peaks and troughs, and the bias squared rises to 0.092.

The sum of the three terms tracks the measured error to within 0.003 in every row, and the error forms a clean U with its bottom at **k = 8**, where it is 0.189 against the noise floor of 0.16. The figure below shows the two curves crossing and their sum.

### The number of trees draws no U


#### Variance falls at every step and bias does not move

The second knob is the number of trees in a *random forest*, an average of decision trees each grown on a bootstrap sample of the training rows. More trees is "more model" by any count of parameters, so if the U belonged to complexity, the forest should eventually overfit as trees are added. The code fits forests of five sizes, with scikit-learn's defaults, on 100 training sets, and reports the bias squared, the variance and the measured error. Its last line is explained below.

```python
sweep = ln.forest_sweep(trees=(1, 2, 5, 25, 100))
for r in sweep:
    print(f"trees {r['trees']:>3}  bias2 {r['bias2']:.4f}"
          f"  var {r['variance']:.4f}  measured {r['mse']:.4f}")
print(f"implied correlation between trees: {ln.forest_rho(sweep):.3f}")
```

```text
trees   1  bias2 0.0019  var 0.1599  measured 0.3189
trees   2  bias2 0.0011  var 0.1135  measured 0.2721
trees   5  bias2 0.0008  var 0.0934  measured 0.2524
trees  25  bias2 0.0007  var 0.0794  measured 0.2390
trees 100  bias2 0.0006  var 0.0747  measured 0.2345
implied correlation between trees: 0.462
```

It does not overfit. Bias squared is below 0.0019 at every size. Its slight fall from one tree to a hundred is about the size of the simulation's own error in estimating it: the average prediction is itself estimated from a finite number of training sets, and its error inflates the estimated bias squared by roughly the variance divided by the number of training sets, 0.0016 for one tree. The variance falls at every step, from 0.160 with one tree to 0.075 with a hundred, and the measured error falls with it. More trees never hurt.

#### The floor set by correlated trees

The variance does not fall to zero either. The reason is the textbook formula for the variance of an average of correlated predictors, given in section 15.2 of Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman; Breiman's 2001 bound for an infinite forest has the same shape. If each of $T$ trees has variance $\sigma^2$ and every pair has correlation $\rho$, their average has variance

$$
\rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{T}\,\sigma^2 .
$$

The second term is the part the trees do not share, and averaging removes it as $T$ grows. The first term is the part they do share, and no amount of averaging touches it. The trees here are correlated because they are grown on bootstrap samples of the same 60 points, which overlap heavily, and so they make many of the same errors.

Fitting $\rho$ to the variance with one tree and with a hundred gives **ρ = 0.46**, so whatever the number of trees, this forest's variance cannot go below 0.074. The dotted curve in the figure below is the formula; the intermediate sizes sit on it.

![Bias stays near zero. Variance falls from 0.160 with one tree to 0.075 with 100 and would not go below 0.074 with any number: averaging removes only the part of the variance the trees do not share.](ml109-f1-trees.png){alt="Variance falling steeply from one tree and levelling off at a dashed floor; bias squared flat near zero throughout."}

#### Why the forest is still not a good model here

None of this makes the forest a good model of this target. At a hundred trees its test error is 0.235, worse than k-NN at its best, 0.189. Fully grown trees on a single input copy their training labels almost as $k = 1$ does, and averaging a hundred of them removes only the unshared part of that variance. What would help is a knob on each tree, such as a minimum number of points per leaf or a maximum depth, which, like $k$, should trade bias for variance and draw a U; that was not measured here.

To lower the floor itself one must make the trees *less alike*, for example by considering fewer features at each split or using smaller bootstrap samples. That is a knob that does trade bias for variance, because decorrelated trees are individually worse.

### What the U is about


The two sweeps disagree because their knobs do different things. The number of neighbours changes *what each prediction is*: a wider average is smoother and less faithful to the target, so lowering variance raises bias. That is a trade, and a trade has an optimum. The number of trees changes only *how many roughly independent copies of the same kind of prediction are averaged*. Averaging copies leaves their mean, and so the bias, unchanged and can only reduce the variance. There is nothing to trade.

So "test error is U-shaped in complexity" is a claim about a particular knob, not about complexity as such. The depth of a single tree, the penalty of a ridge regression, the degree of a polynomial and $k$ in k-NN all trade bias for variance, and below the point where the model *interpolates*, fitting its training data exactly, each typically draws a U; only $k$ was measured here. Ensemble size, the number of bootstrap draws and the number of random restarts averaged do not. The table below sets the two sweeps side by side.

![The sum column and the measured column agree row by row: the decomposition is an identity in expectation, and the small gaps are simulation error. What differs between the two knobs is which terms move.](ml109-f2-table.png){alt="An eleven-row table of bias squared, variance, noise, their sum and the measured test error, for six values of k and five forest sizes."}

In practice the U is found by cross-validation, which estimates the sum and never its parts (Section 2.1 examines what that estimate targets). The decomposition explains the shape of the curve; it is not how anyone tunes a model on real data. The next section returns to linear models and adds a third kind of knob: the number of features in a model that interpolates its training data, where the error curve rises, then falls again.

## The Second Descent Is Real, and a Ridge Penalty Makes It Unnecessary

<!-- source: ml-10-double-descent; hero: ml110-hero.png -->


Section 6.4 measured the U-shaped curve of test error for a knob that trades bias for variance, and the classical advice is to stop at its bottom. The same advice sets a limit on size: classical statistics teaches that a model needs fewer parameters than data points, and the sentence that follows is *more parameters than data points means overfitting.* A practitioner meets it whenever the size of a model is weighed against the size of a dataset: when features are pruned so that the count stays below the row count, or when a network that fits its training set exactly is assumed to be useless. Modern practice contradicts it daily: large models have far more parameters than training rows, fit the training data exactly, and generalise anyway. Which of the two is right turns out to depend on a third ingredient, regularisation.

This section goes past the bottom of the U and past the point where the model interpolates, using least squares on random features, where the parameter count can be pushed far past the row count and the fit computed exactly. It locates the peak of the resulting curve, identifies what feeds it, asks whether the second descent ever beats the first, and then shows that a ridge penalty removes the peak and the need to go past it.

### Least squares on random features


#### The model and the minimum-norm fit

*Double descent*, named by Belkin and colleagues in 2019, is the curve that reconciles classical advice with modern practice: keep adding parameters past the point where the model fits its training data exactly, and test error comes down again. The cleanest place to see it is least squares on random features.

Draw $p$ random directions $w_1, \dots, w_p$ in input space. For each input $x$, form the features $\phi_j(x) = \max(0, x^\top w_j)$, each a *ReLU* (rectified linear unit) applied along one random direction. Stack them for the $n$ training rows into an $n \times p$ matrix $\Phi$, and regress the target on them by least squares. The directions are fixed and never trained; only the last-layer weights $\beta$ are fitted. This is a two-layer network whose first layer is frozen at random, which makes the fit an exact linear-algebra computation.

Below $p = n$ there is, in general, a unique least-squares fit, and it does not pass through every point. At $p = n$ the matrix $\Phi$ is square and, in general, invertible, so exactly one $\beta$ fits the training data perfectly. Above $p = n$ there are infinitely many exact fits. The *pseudo-inverse* $\Phi^{+}$ picks one of them, the *minimum-norm* solution:

$$
\hat\beta = \Phi^{+} y = \arg\min_{\beta} \; \lVert \beta \rVert \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Phi \beta = y .
$$

Below the threshold the pseudo-inverse gives the ordinary least-squares fit, so the same formula covers every $p$.

#### Three phases of the test error

The simulation has $n = 100$ training rows of 10 standard-normal inputs, a nonlinear target (a $\tanh$ of a fixed random projection plus a small sine term) and label noise of standard deviation 0.3. Test error is measured against the noise-free target on 5,000 fixed points, and every figure is a median over 30 training sets and feature draws. The code reports test error, training error and the weight norm $\lVert \hat\beta \rVert$ for eight values of $p$.

```python
from standarderror.ml import linear as ln

# n = 100 rows, d = 10 inputs, p random ReLU features, min-norm fit
c = ln.random_features(n=100)
for p in (20, 60, 90, 100, 110, 150, 500, 2000):
    print(f"p {p:>4}  test {c['test'][p]:7.3f}  "
          f"train {c['train'][p]:.3f}  |w| {c['norm'][p]:8.1f}")
```

```text

p   60  test   0.456  train 0.076  |w|      3.6
p   90  test   2.623  train 0.018  |w|     11.4
p  100  test  21.562  train 0.000  |w|     39.6
p  110  test   2.367  train 0.000  |w|     10.6
p  150  test   0.884  train 0.000  |w|      5.2
```

All three phases are present. Test error is 0.271 at $p = 20$, climbs to **21.6 at p = 100**, about 80 times worse, and comes back down to 0.376 at $p = 2000$. Training error is zero from $p = 100$ on.

The weight norm explains the peak. At $p = n$ there is exactly one way to pass through every training point, and with barely enough features to do it, that way needs enormous weights: 40, against 0.8 at $p = 2000$. In terms of the matrix, the solution is $\Phi^{-1} y$, and the component of $y$ along each singular direction of $\Phi$ is divided by the corresponding singular value. A square random matrix typically has a smallest singular value close to zero, so whatever part of $y$ lies along that direction, noise included, is amplified enormously, and the fitted function swings wildly between training points. With more features than rows there are many interpolators, the matrix's smallest singular values move away from zero, and the smallest-norm interpolator is smooth. The figure below shows the curve at three training-set sizes.

### Where the peak sits, and what feeds it


#### The interpolation threshold follows the rows

The peak tracks the number of rows, not any particular number of features. With 50 rows it sits at $p = 50$, and with 200 rows at $p = 200$. On an axis of features per row the three peaks line up, although away from the peak more rows give lower error. The location is the *interpolation threshold*: the smallest $p$ at which an exact fit to the training data exists.

#### Anything the fit must pass through but cannot represent

What makes the peak tall is anything the fit must pass through but cannot represent. Label noise is the obvious candidate, but not the only one. The code reruns the experiment with no label noise and with double the noise, and reports the height and location of the peak, the best model below the threshold, and the model at $p = 2000$.

```python
for sigma in (0.0, 0.6):
    c = ln.random_features(n=100, sigma=sigma)
    p, e = ln.best_under(c)
    print(f"noise sd {sigma}: peak {c['test'][ln.peak(c)]:.2f}"
          f" at p = {ln.peak(c)}; best below {e:.3f} (p = {p});"
          f" at p = 2000 {c['test'][2000]:.3f}")
```

```text
noise sd 0.0: peak 23.31 at p = 100; best below 0.225 (p = 40); at p = 2000 0.248
noise sd 0.6: peak 63.50 at p = 100; best below 0.318 (p = 20); at p = 2000 0.783
```

With no label noise at all, the peak is still 23.3. A finite set of random ReLU features cannot express this target exactly, and the part of the target it misses acts on the fit much like noise: something the interpolator must pass through but cannot represent, and which the near-zero singular value amplifies just as it amplifies noise. Doubling the noise makes the peak taller, 63.5, and raises every other point of the curve with it.

#### The second descent is a recovery

In both cases the best model below the threshold beats the model with 2,000 features: 0.225 against 0.248 without noise, and 0.318 against 0.783 with double the noise. The second descent here is a *recovery*, not an improvement. Going far past the threshold undoes the damage the threshold did; it does not reach the bottom of the first U. The closest call is at $n = 50$, where the best small model beats the 1,000-feature one by only 0.007. That is a near-tie on medians of 30 training sets, so there "never wins" means "does not win", not "loses badly".

### A ridge penalty removes the peak


#### Ridge regression on the same features

The minimum-norm fit has no penalty; it insists on interpolating. Ridge regression, whose squared penalty Section 6.3 set beside the lasso's, gives up some fit in exchange for smaller weights, minimising $\lVert y - \Phi\beta \rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert \beta \rVert^2$, with solution

$$
\hat\beta_\lambda = (\Phi^\top \Phi + \lambda I)^{-1} \Phi^\top y .
$$

In terms of singular values, ridge replaces division by a singular value $s$ with multiplication by $s / (s^2 + \lambda)$, which stays bounded as $s$ approaches zero. That is exactly the amplification that produced the peak. As $\lambda \to 0$ the ridge solution tends to the minimum-norm one. The code fits ridge at five penalties and reports the worst test error over $p$ and the errors at $p = 20$, $100$ and $2000$.

```python
for lam in (1e-3, 1e-2, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0):
    c = ln.random_features(n=100, ridge=lam)
    print(f"ridge {lam:<6g} worst {max(c['test'].values()):.3f}"
          f"  p=20 {c['test'][20]:.3f}  p=100 {c['test'][100]:.3f}"
          f"  p=2000 {c['test'][2000]:.3f}")
```

```text
ridge 0.001  worst 4.104  p=20 0.271  p=100 4.104  p=2000 0.376
ridge 0.01   worst 1.510  p=20 0.271  p=100 1.510  p=2000 0.375
```

Each penalty shrinks the peak, from 4.10 at λ = 0.001 to 0.346 at λ = 10. At λ = 10 there is no peak to speak of, and the best model is at **p = 80, error 0.162**. The curve is flat around it: every $p$ from 40 to 150 is within 0.01, $p = 100$ included at 0.165. The region where the unpenalised fit was worst has become the region where the penalised fit is best, and that best is better than the best of either unpenalised descent, 0.271 and 0.376. Nakkiran and colleagues proved a general version for certain linear regression settings in 2021: with the penalty optimally tuned, test error does not peak, and more data never hurts. #### The minimum-norm fit is implicitly regularised

The second descent happens at all because the minimum-norm fit at very large $p$ is itself *implicitly regularised*: among all the interpolators, it picks the one with the smallest weights, which is a preference imposed by the fitting procedure rather than by an explicit penalty. Section 6.1 above found the same kind of preference in gradient descent on separable data, which converges in direction to a max-margin solution that no term in the loss asked for. An explicit ridge penalty does the same job as the implicit one, without needing thousands of extra features.

Across this chapter, then, what a linear model returns is decided in part by a regulariser: a stopping tolerance, a penalty priced in the features' units, a cross-validation rule, or the preference of a fitting procedure for small weights. The chapter has pushed linear models past the conditions their textbook statements need. Chapter 7 turns to trees and forests, starting with whether a single decision tree is as interpretable as it is said to be.

## In practice

### Logistic regression on separable data

- **Test for separation before reporting coefficients.** Fit a linear SVM with a very large $C$ and read the training accuracy. At 100%, the unpenalised estimate does not exist.
- **Refit at two tolerances.** If the coefficients move by more than the precision you report, they were never estimates; report signs and ratios, not magnitudes, odds ratios or Wald p-values.
- **Know which estimator you ran.** scikit-learn penalises by default; turning the penalty off, or using a package that fits plain maximum likelihood, exposes you to all of Section 6.1.
- **Check the confidence, not just the accuracy.** If many training rows sit within a hair of 0 or 1, do not trust the probabilities on new data; add a penalty or recalibrate on held-out data (Section 11.3).

### Choosing and pricing a penalty

- **Choose the penalty deliberately**, by cross-validation on log loss if the probabilities matter, rather than letting a tolerance or iteration limit choose it.
- **Treat units as part of the model specification.** Before fitting a lasso, list each feature with its units and decide how each should be priced. Write the decision down.
- **Test the selection's sensitivity to units.** For the features you are least sure about, multiply the column by 10 and by 0.1, refit with cross-validation, and see whether any change status. If one does, its presence was partly a unit decision.
- **Decide how binary features are scaled.** Refit once with everything standardised and once with binaries left as 0/1. If the selected set changes, choose explicitly between an effect per occurrence and an effect per standard deviation, and say which.

### Reading a lasso's selected set

- **Do not use cross-validated error to choose between selections.** In Section 6.2 the selections differed while the errors were within a few per cent; the error cannot arbitrate.
- **Do not read a CV-minimum lasso's non-zero coefficients as a list of real effects.** At that penalty some passengers are expected. Use the CV minimum for prediction.
- **Use the 1-SE rule, or a stricter one, when selection is the goal,** and accept that it drops some weak real effects. Compare the two selected sets: features in both are the most defensible list; features only in the CV-minimum set are likely passengers.
- **Bootstrap the selection and tabulate signs.** Refit on 100 resamples and count, for each feature, how often it is kept with each sign. A feature kept often with both signs is not a finding about its direction.
- **Look for pairs that trade places.** Two features of which one is kept when the other is not are a single signal the data cannot attribute. Report the pair, not the member that happened to win.
- **Do not expect the elastic net or stability selection to resolve a near-copy.** They group or stabilise; they do not identify which of two correlated features is the real one.
- **Report selected sets conditionally.** State that a feature was selected *in the units used*, or do not present the selected set as a finding.

### Knobs, ensembles and the U

- **Before expecting a U, ask what the knob does.** If it changes the predictor itself (a neighbourhood size, a depth, a penalty, a degree), look for an optimum by cross-validation. If it only averages more copies of the same kind of predictor, expect a monotone curve that levels off.
- **Do not tune the number of trees in a random forest as if it could overfit.** Add trees until the validation error stops improving by an amount you care about; more trees cost compute, not accuracy.
- **Estimate the floor of an ensemble.** Compare the variance of one member with that of many to estimate $\rho$. If the ensemble is already near $\rho\sigma^2$, adding members is wasted compute; reduce the correlation between members instead.
- **Tune the members of an ensemble, not only its size.** A forest of fully grown trees on a smooth, low-dimensional target may be beaten by a simpler model; the per-tree knobs are where its bias–variance trade lives.
- **Use a simulation to understand a knob.** On a synthetic version of your problem, the decomposition shows which term a knob moves, which cross-validation on real data cannot.

### Model size and regularisation

- **Do not fear parameter count; ask about regularisation.** A model with more parameters than rows is not overfitted by that fact alone. What matters is whether it is regularised, implicitly or explicitly.
- **Avoid the threshold when fitting without a penalty.** If an unpenalised fit has about as many free parameters as rows, expect it to be at its worst. Move away from $p = n$, or add a penalty.
- **Prefer an explicit penalty to a second descent.** In Section 6.5 a ridge penalty taken from a small grid of five values, with about $p = n$ features, beat both the small model and the model with twenty times as many features as rows, at a fraction of the second model's size.
- **Record the weight norm alongside the error.** A peak in test error accompanied by a peak in the norm is the interpolation threshold; a peak in one without the other means something else is going on.
- **Tune the penalty for each model size** by cross-validation rather than using one fixed value across sizes; the best size moves with the penalty.

## Limits of the evidence

### Separable data (Section 6.1)

- **Separation is a property of the training sample.** A population can overlap while every sample of it you can afford is separable. The breast cancer data is not separable in principle, only in every half of it measured here. Whether the unpenalised maximum exists can change with one added row.
- **Quasi-complete separation behaves the same way for some coefficients.** In *quasi-complete separation* a hyperplane separates the classes except for rows lying exactly on it; some coefficient estimates diverge and the rest are fine. Software warnings for it are inconsistent.
- **Standard errors are the first casualty.** *Wald standard errors*, computed from the curvature of the log-likelihood at the fitted point, come out enormous under separation and the p-values beside them are meaningless. Firth's penalised likelihood, which keeps the estimates finite, is the usual repair when coefficients rather than predictions are the goal.
- **The gradient descent path uses one pair of digits.** The logarithmic growth of the norm and the max-margin limit are theorems. The step at which the test log loss turns depends on the data and on the step size.

### Units and selection (Sections 6.2 and 6.3)

- **Prediction is not the casualty.** Everything in these two sections concerns *which* features a lasso keeps, not how well it predicts. The CV-minimum lasso is a perfectly good predictor, and if a model is used only for its predictions the units matter far less, though the coefficients still do not mean what they appear to.
- **Ridge has the same dependence on units, without the visible flips.** A *ridge* penalty, λ times the sum of squared coefficients, is also charged per unit of coefficient, so rescaling a feature changes how much it is shrunk. Ridge never sets a coefficient to zero, so no feature visibly enters or leaves.
- **Cross-validation does not fix the units.** Every refit in Section 6.2 re-chose λ by cross-validation. The units are not a tuning problem: they change the shape of the penalty, the relative prices of the features, and not its overall strength.
- **Weighted penalties make the choice explicit rather than removing it.** The *adaptive lasso* gives each feature its own penalty weight, usually derived from a preliminary fit, and *group penalties* price whole blocks of features together. Both choose the per-feature prices on purpose instead of inheriting them from the units; neither makes the prices disappear.
- **One dataset and one simulation for the units.** The count of seven flips belongs to the diabetes data and to factors up to a hundred. A feature that flips only at a factor of 100 may never be recorded in a unit that coarse; one that flips at 3 easily could be.
- **Stability selection is the principled fix for passengers, and it changes the question.** Meinshausen and Bühlmann's procedure keeps the features selected in a large share of subsamples across many penalties, with a guarantee that controls the expected number of noise features selected. It is still blind to which of two near-copies is the real one; the guarantee is about noise features, not stand-ins.
- **The elastic net groups; it does not choose.** Adding a ridge component (half and half, tuned by the same cross-validation) keeps correlated features together instead of picking one. On the design of Section 6.3 it does so by keeping the stand-in *more* often, 42% of the time at ρ = 0 and 90% at ρ = 0.99, with 3.6 noise features kept on average. Grouping is not choosing, and it is no closer to the truth.
- **One simulation design for selection.** Ten features, 100 rows, effects of 0.5. How the number of passengers changes with more rows was not measured; a penalty tuned for prediction is known not to be selection-consistent in general, so do not count on it falling to zero. The stand-in problem should shrink with more rows: in this design b's correlation with the true features is ρ, below 1, so the irrepresentable condition holds and Zhao and Yu's theory says a suitably tuned lasso eventually separates a from b. The rows that takes grow as ρ approaches 1, and nothing here measures how many.
- **Bootstrap selection frequencies are themselves estimates.** A hundred resamples pin a share down to within about ±10 points at 95%; the sign splits quoted, with both shares far above zero, are well clear of that.

### The bias–variance decomposition (Section 6.4)

- **Squared error only.** The clean additive split into bias, variance and noise belongs to squared error. For 0-1 loss there are several competing decompositions, and in none of them does variance simply add.
- **One target, one sample size.** Theory says that with more training points the k-NN U should move to larger $k$, and with a rougher target to smaller $k$; neither was measured here. The forest's floor depends on the target too. The shapes, not the numbers, are the finding.
- **The decomposition needs many training sets.** On real data there is one. The bootstrap gives a rough stand-in, but it estimates variance from resamples that overlap heavily, and bias cannot be computed at all without the true function.
- **Not the whole story past interpolation.** A 1-nearest-neighbour fit reproduces its training labels exactly, a fully grown tree those of its bootstrap sample, and both sit at the high-variance end in Section 6.4. Section 6.5 showed a family of interpolating models whose error falls again as they grow, which the U has no room for.
- **Forest defaults.** scikit-learn's regression forest considers every feature at each split by default. With one input feature that makes no difference, but on wide data the setting changes $\rho$, and with it the floor.

### Double descent (Section 6.5)

- **The penalty was picked from a grid, not tuned.** Five values of λ, the largest 10. Where each is best moves with it: λ = 1 does best at $p = 40$ (0.212), λ = 10 around $p = n$. A larger λ, or one chosen by cross-validation for each $p$, might do better still and put its best elsewhere. The claim needs only one penalty that beats both descents, and λ = 10 is one.
- **Random features, not trained networks.** The features are fixed and only the last layer is fitted, which is what makes the minimum-norm solution computable. In trained networks double descent has been reported in width, in training time and in the number of samples, with the same role for label noise, but the implicit regularisation of gradient descent is harder to pin down than a pseudo-inverse.
- **"Never wins" is about these settings.** Theory gives cases where the overparameterised minimum-norm fit beats every underparameterised one, for instance when the signal is spread over many weak directions and the extra features add regularisation the small model lacks. The claim here is narrower: on this target, at these sizes and noise levels, it did not.
- **Medians, not means.** Near the threshold some training sets give errors far above the median, because the smallest singular value of the feature matrix is nearly zero. A mean would make the peak taller still; the median is the more conservative summary.
- **One kind of input.** Gaussian inputs, ReLU features, a smooth target. The threshold at $p = n$ is general for linear fits on fixed features; the heights are not.

## Key points

1. The maximum-likelihood logistic fit does not exist when a hyperplane separates the training classes, and informative features make separation more likely, not less: all 50 half splits of the breast cancer data were separable. Unpenalised, the coefficients a library returns are then set by its stopping rule, with a norm from 2.4 to 1,289 and a test log loss from 0.079 to 1.575 across tolerances.
2. Gradient descent's weight norm grows like $\log t$, about 3.3 per tenfold more steps, and its direction converges to the max-margin direction of the problem it solves. Along that path test accuracy can improve while test log loss worsens: the direction improves and the confidence overshoots.
3. A penalty gives the question an answer. Choose its strength deliberately; otherwise a tolerance chooses it.
4. A lasso penalty is a price per unit of coefficient, so rescaling a feature by $k$ is the same as giving it a private penalty $\lambda / k$. On the diabetes data, 7 of 10 features are kept or dropped depending on their units alone, while the cross-validated errors of all those models span 5.3% of the base model's.
5. Standardising is a choice of units, not the absence of one: a binary feature at 2% prevalence is kept in 24 of 40 datasets standardised and 7 of 40 raw.
6. Cross-validation tunes the lasso to predict. At its minimum the lasso kept 2.8 of 7 pure-noise features on average; the 1-SE rule dropped most of them (0.2 on average) at some cost in real effects missed, and neither rule reliably separated a feature from a highly correlated stand-in at 100 rows.
7. Report sign stability, not only selection frequency: on the diabetes data age was kept in 82% of bootstrap fits, with its sign split 37% positive and 45% negative. Treat a lasso's selected set as a property of the penalty, the units and the sample as much as of the process that generated the data.
8. For squared error, bias squared, variance and noise add up exactly to the expected test error. $k$ in k-NN trades bias for variance and drew a U with its bottom at $k = 8$; adding trees to a random forest lowered variance every time, from 0.160 to 0.075, and drew no U.
9. The variance of an average of correlated models has a floor of $\rho\sigma^2$, with $\rho = 0.46$ for the forest measured. Lower it by decorrelating the members, not by adding more, and before drawing a U ask whether the knob changes the predictor or only averages copies of it.
10. For least squares on random features, test error peaked at $p = n$ and fell again beyond it. The peak needs something the fit must interpolate but cannot represent; without any label noise it was still 23.3. In every setting measured the best model below the threshold beat the model with twenty times as many features as rows: the second descent was a recovery, not an improvement.
11. A ridge penalty removed the peak, and at λ = 10 the best models sat around $p = n$, with error down to 0.162, better than anything the unpenalised fit reached. Double descent is a reason not to fear large models that are regularised, implicitly or explicitly, not a reason to expect that adding parameters to an unregularised fit will help.
