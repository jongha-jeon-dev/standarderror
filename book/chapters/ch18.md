# Distance and Anomalies

Many methods decide what is similar, and what is unusual, by measuring distance. A nearest-neighbour classifier labels a point by the training points closest to it. An anomaly detector flags a point because it sits far from the others, or in a sparser spot than its neighbours, or is cut off from the rest by few random splits. Chapter 17 clustered points by distance; this chapter turns to the methods that use distance most directly, and to two beliefs about them that shape practice. One is a warning: that distance stops meaning anything once the data have many columns, so that distance-based methods should be avoided on wide tables. The other is a promise: that an anomaly detector, given the data, returns the anomalies in them. A practitioner who believes the warning too readily rules out good methods by a column count; one who believes the promise investigates whatever a detector flags, and nothing else.

The chapter's two sections each test one of these sentences:

- Section 18.1: *in high dimensions, nearest neighbours stop meaning anything*;
- Section 18.2: *an anomaly detector finds the anomalies in your data*.

Section 18.1 measures the warning first on points spread uniformly over a cube, where it holds, and then on the handwritten digits, where it does not: their 64 pixels behave like far fewer dimensions, because the pixels move together. Appending columns of pure noise brings the curse back for a nearest-neighbour classifier, much more than for logistic regression, and a feature-selection step that removes most of the noise makes distance useful again. The section's conclusion is that the curse of dimensionality counts the directions in which the data actually spread and the columns that carry nothing, not the number of columns.

Section 18.2 builds on that conclusion. Its detectors, the isolation forest and the local outlier factor, rank points by how poorly they fit a description of normality, and the local outlier factor builds that description from distances to neighbours. The section separates three things a detector's output mixes together: how many points are flagged, which is set by a parameter and not estimated; how well the underlying score ranks the true anomalies; and what in the data changes that ranking. Two such things are found. Anomalies that arrive as a tight group shield one another from any score that looks only at a small neighbourhood, and anomalies that are unusual in two features are diluted by many irrelevant ones, the same dilution Section 18.1 measured for nearest neighbours. The question is then replicated on the breast-cancer data, with malignant tumours hidden among benign ones.

Both sections reach the same lesson from different directions. What is near and what is unusual are decided by the features a distance or a score is given, and by how many of those features carry information. A method that weights or selects its features, or a practitioner who chooses them deliberately, recovers most of what an unweighted distance over every column loses. Read together, the sections say that the column count is the wrong thing to worry about and the choice of features the right one.

## The Curse of Dimensionality Counts the Dimensions the Data Use

<!-- source: ml-33-dimensions; hero: ml133-hero.png -->


Section 17.1 clustered by distance and found that the clusters depended on what the distance could see. Nearest-neighbour methods depend on distance even more directly: to classify a point, they find the training points closest to it and take a vote among them. The textbook warning attached to these methods is the *curse of dimensionality*, the collection of ways in which methods that work in a few dimensions degrade as the number of dimensions grows. For distances it is usually stated as the sentence under test: *in high dimensions, nearest neighbours stop meaning anything*. A practitioner meets it as a reason to avoid distance-based methods on wide tables, or to reduce dimension first. If it is false as usually applied, good methods are ruled out by a column count; if true, distance-based results on wide data are close to random.

This section first states the warning precisely and measures it on uniform points, where it holds. It then finds that the 64-pixel handwritten digits behave like far fewer dimensions, and explains why. It next adds columns of pure noise, which reproduce the curse for a nearest-neighbour classifier much more than for logistic regression. Finally it removes most of the noise with a feature-selection step, and distance becomes useful again.

### Distances concentrate when points fill their space


A *k-nearest-neighbour classifier* (k-NN) predicts a point's class by the majority class among the $k$ training points nearest to it, here in *Euclidean distance*, the square root of the sum of squared differences over all columns. Throughout this section $k = 5$.

Beyer and colleagues gave the warning a precise form. For a wide class of distributions, as the number of dimensions $d$ grows, the distance from a query point to its nearest neighbour and the distance to its farthest become nearly equal, so that "nearest" no longer singles anything out. The mechanism is easy to see for points spread uniformly over a cube. The squared Euclidean distance between two such points is a sum of $d$ independent terms, one per coordinate. Its mean grows in proportion to $d$, but its standard deviation grows only in proportion to $\sqrt{d}$, so the spread of distances relative to their typical size shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$. In many dimensions almost every pair of points is at almost the same distance. This shrinking of relative spread is called the *concentration of distances*.

Two ratios measure it. The first is the distance to the nearest point divided by the median distance to all points: near 0 when the nearest point is much nearer than the rest, near 1 when it is not. The second is the gap between the farthest and the nearest distance as a multiple of the nearest, $(D_{\max} - D_{\min}) / D_{\min}$, the quantity in Beyer and colleagues' result. The code computes both, averaged over query points, for 1,000 points uniform on the unit cube in dimensions from 1 to 1,024.

```python
from standarderror.ml import around

for r in around.contrast():          # 1,000 uniform points per d
    print(f"d = {r['d']:5d}: nearest / median distance {r['ratio']:.3f}, "
          f"(farthest - nearest) / nearest {r['spread']:.2f}")
```

```text
d =     1: nearest / median distance 0.001, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 2011.69
d =     2: nearest / median distance 0.028, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 70.79
d =     4: nearest / median distance 0.144, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 11.06
d =     8: nearest / median distance 0.339, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 3.55
d =    16: nearest / median distance 0.526, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 1.66
d =    64: nearest / median distance 0.774, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 0.56
d =   256: nearest / median distance 0.886, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 0.25
d =  1024: nearest / median distance 0.944, (farthest - nearest) / nearest 0.12
```

In two dimensions the nearest of 1,000 points is at 0.03 of the median distance, and the farthest is 71 times farther than the nearest. In 1,024 dimensions the nearest is at 0.94 of the median, and the farthest is only 12% farther than the nearest. Every point is about equally far from every other. A vote among the "nearest" five is then a vote among five points chosen almost at random.

So the sentence is true of uniform points. Whether it is true of data is another question. The figure below plots the first ratio for uniform points, with the digits, examined next, marked against the curve.

![The digits' 64 pixels have the ratio that uniform data have in about 8 dimensions. Noise columns move them up the curve.](ml133-f0-ratio.png){alt="A rising curve for uniform points from near 0 at one dimension to about 0.94 at 1,024; the digits sit well below it at 64 columns and climb towards it as noise columns are added."}

### Sixty-four pixels behave like eight dimensions


The digits data are 1,797 images of handwritten digits, each an 8 × 8 grid of 64 pixel intensities, here divided by 16 so that every pixel lies between 0 and 1. By column count this is a high-dimensional data set. The code computes the same nearest-to-median ratio for the digits, together with the five-fold cross-validated accuracy of 5-NN and of logistic regression. It then appends columns of uniform noise on the same 0-to-1 scale to each image, unrelated to the digit, and repeats the measurements; the first column of the output is the total number of columns.

```python
for r in around.neighbours_noise():  # digits plus uniform noise columns
    print(f"{r['d']:5d} columns: nearest / median {r['ratio']:.3f}, "
          f"5-NN {r['knn']:.3f}, logistic {r['logistic']:.3f}")
```

```text
   64 columns: nearest / median 0.339, 5-NN 0.986, logistic 0.969
   80 columns: nearest / median 0.504, 5-NN 0.981, logistic 0.960
  128 columns: nearest / median 0.695, 5-NN 0.960, logistic 0.950
  320 columns: nearest / median 0.859, 5-NN 0.867, logistic 0.924
 1088 columns: nearest / median 0.938, 5-NN 0.659, logistic 0.871
```

On the digits alone, the first row, the nearest image is at 0.34 of the median distance. Uniform points reach that ratio at about 8 dimensions, not 64. And 5-NN, the method the curse is supposed to defeat, scores 0.986, higher than logistic regression's 0.969.

#### Why the pixels do not fill their dimensions

The 64 pixels are not 64 independent directions of variation. Pen strokes are continuous, so neighbouring pixels rise and fall together. Most of the grid is blank for most digits, so many pixels barely vary. And the images of one digit differ from each other in a few ways, such as slant, stroke thickness and size, rather than in 64 independent ways. The points therefore occupy a thin, curved region inside the 64-dimensional cube, and distances measured within that region behave like distances in a much smaller space. The concentration argument above needed the coordinates to vary independently; when they move together, the effective number of terms in the sum is small.

Levina and Bickel call the dimension of such a region the *intrinsic dimension* of the data and estimate it from exactly this kind of nearest-neighbour statistic.

The second ratio agrees. For the digits, the gap between a point's farthest and nearest neighbours, as a multiple of the nearest distance, is 3.0; for uniform points it is 3.6 in 8 dimensions and 0.56 in 64. By either measure the digits look like a space of a handful of dimensions, not of sixty-four. What matters for the curse is not how many columns the data have but how many directions they actually spread in.

### Columns that carry nothing bring the curse back


The noise columns show where the curse does bite. Each fills its dimension, which the pixels do not, and is unrelated to the digit. Sixteen of them barely matter. With 256, the ratio is 0.86 and 5-NN falls to 0.867. With 1,024, the ratio is 0.94, about what 1,024 uniform dimensions give, and 5-NN scores 0.659.

The mechanism is that Euclidean distance weighs every column equally. The squared distance between two images is the sum of the squared differences in the pixels and the squared differences in the noise. The pixel part is unchanged; the noise part grows with the number of noise columns and varies at random from pair to pair. Once the noise columns outnumber the pixels sixteen to one, the distance between two images is mostly the distance between their noise, and the nearest neighbours are nearest in noise. The figure below shows the accuracy of the two classifiers as noise columns are added.

![With 1,024 noise columns nearest neighbours score 0.659 and logistic regression 0.871.](ml133-f1-accuracy.png){alt="Two falling curves: nearest neighbours start higher and fall steeply past a few hundred columns; logistic regression starts a little lower and falls much less."}

Logistic regression suffers far less, falling from 0.969 to 0.871. It learns a weight for every column, and can give the noise columns weights near zero, which a plain distance cannot do. The drop for 5-NN is 3.3 times the drop for logistic regression. The practical curse is therefore a curse on methods that treat every column as equally informative, applied to data with many uninformative columns. The table below collects the ratios for uniform data and for the digits with noise.

![The digits sit between uniform data in 4 and 16 dimensions. The noise columns carry them to where 1,024 uniform dimensions are.](ml133-f2-table.png){alt="A thirteen-row table of distance ratios for uniform data in growing dimension and for the digits with growing numbers of noise columns, with accuracies for the digits."}

### Removing the noise from the distance restores the neighbours


If the trouble is columns that carry nothing, the repair is to keep them out of the distance. The bluntest version is *univariate feature selection*: score each column separately and keep the best. Here each column is scored by its analysis-of-variance *F statistic*, the ratio of the spread of the column's class means around the overall mean (the between-class mean square) to its variance within classes, which is large when the column's values differ between digit classes. The 64 columns with the largest F are kept, and the selection is made inside each training fold so that the test images play no part in it; Section 3.1 measures how much a selection step fitted on all the rows can leak.

```python
r = around.neighbours_repair()   # 1,024 noise columns, keep 64 by F
print(f"5-NN on the {r['keep']} selected columns: {r['knn']:.3f}; "
      f"real pixels among them: {r['real_share']:.0%}")
```

```text
5-NN on the 64 selected columns: 0.982; real pixels among them: 88%
```

With the 1,024 noise columns still in the data, 5-NN on the 64 kept columns scores 0.982, against 0.659 without selection and 0.986 on the clean digits. The selection is not perfect: 88% of the columns it keeps are real pixels, and the rest are noise columns that happened to differ between classes by chance in the training folds. It is enough. Distance is useful again as soon as most of it is computed on columns that carry information.

That is the honest reading of the curse. It is not a property of a number of columns, which a selection step can change without changing the problem. It is a property of how the information in the data is spread across the columns. Many columns, few of them informative, is the hard case. Many columns, all of them moving together with the thing being predicted, is the easy one, and the digits are much closer to it.

The same arithmetic applies wherever a method decides what is unusual by measuring distance, which is how many anomaly detectors work. The next section turns to them.

## An Anomaly Score Ranks; the Contamination Setting Only Cuts

<!-- source: ml-59-anomaly; hero: ml159-hero.png -->


Section 18.1 above used distance to find what is similar. Anomaly detection uses it, among other things, to find what is not, and it is the unsupervised task with the most practical pull: fraud nobody has labelled yet, a failing machine, a data-entry error among a million rows. With no labels there is no model of the anomaly, only a model of normality, and a point is flagged for not fitting it. In scikit-learn a detector is one line, and its output is a flag on each row, anomalous or not. That output invites the sentence under test in this section: *an anomaly detector finds the anomalies in your data*. If the sentence is false, the rows a detector flags are not the anomalies but something else, a share fixed in advance or the points that happen to be unusual in the features supplied, and a team that investigates the flags and nothing else will both waste effort and miss what it was looking for.

This section first defines the two detectors used, the isolation forest and the local outlier factor, and the measures used to score them. It then plants anomalies whose number and nature are known and asks three questions separately: how many points get flagged, how well the score ranks the planted anomalies, and what in the data changes that ranking, first anomalies that arrive in a group and then anomalies hidden among many irrelevant features. Finally it replicates the ranking question on the breast-cancer data, with malignant tumours hidden among benign ones as the anomalies to find.

### Two detectors and how to score them


The *isolation forest* of Liu, Ting and Zhou builds many random trees, each on a subsample of the data. Each tree splits the data recursively by choosing a feature at random and a split value at random between that feature's minimum and maximum, until each point sits alone in a leaf or a depth limit is reached. A point that lies far from the rest is separated by few random splits; a point inside a dense mass needs many. The anomaly score is based on the average depth at which a point is isolated, normalised so that short paths give high scores. The forests here have 100 trees.

The *local outlier factor* (LOF) of Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander compares a point's local density with the densities of its neighbours. Each point's *local reachability density* is, roughly, the inverse of its average distance to its $k$ nearest neighbours. The LOF of a point is the average ratio of its neighbours' densities to its own: about 1 for a point as dense as its surroundings, much larger for a point in a sparse spot next to a dense region. A simpler score used below as a comparison is the plain *k-NN distance*, the distance from a point to its $k$-th nearest neighbour.

A detector produces two things: a continuous score and a flag obtained by cutting the score at a threshold. They are scored separately here. The ranking is scored by the *AUC*, the area under the ROC curve, which equals the probability that a randomly chosen planted anomaly receives a higher score than a randomly chosen normal point: 1 for a perfect ranking, 0.5 for a random one, and below 0.5 for a ranking that puts anomalies systematically *below* normal points. The flags are scored by *precision*, the share of flagged points that are truly anomalous, and *recall*, the share of true anomalies that are flagged.

### The contamination setting decides how many are flagged


scikit-learn's detectors take a `contamination` parameter, and it reads like an estimate of how much of the data is anomalous. It is not an estimate. It sets the threshold on the score so that exactly that share of the training points is flagged. The first simulation has 5,000 standard Gaussian points in five dimensions, with a share of them, 0.5%, 2% or 10%, replaced by anomalies shifted by 2.5 in every coordinate with random signs. The block below fits an isolation forest at two true anomaly rates with two contamination settings, 5% and the default `'auto'`, and prints the flagged share and the precision.

```python
from standarderror.ml import unsup

for r in unsup.anomaly_contamination(rates=(0.005, 0.1),
                                     settings=(0.05, "auto")):
    for c in ("0.05", "auto"):
        v = r[c]
        print(f"true {r['rate']:5.1%}  contamination={c:4s}  flagged "
              f"{v['flagged']:.3f}  precision {v['precision']:.2f}")
```

```text
true  0.5%  contamination=0.05  flagged 0.050  precision 0.10
true  0.5%  contamination=auto  flagged 0.134  precision 0.04
true 10.0%  contamination=0.05  flagged 0.050  precision 1.00
true 10.0%  contamination=auto  flagged 0.118  precision 0.84
```

With 0.5% of the points truly anomalous, a setting of 5% flagged 5% and gave a precision of 0.10: nine flags in ten were normal points. That is arithmetic, not a fault of the score: when the cut admits ten times as many points as there are anomalies, at most one flag in ten can be right. In the other direction, with 10% truly anomalous, a setting of 1% gave perfect precision and caught 10% of them; the cut was too tight to admit more. The default, `'auto'`, does not adapt to the data either. It applies a fixed threshold to the isolation forest's score, and here it flagged 10.5% to 13.4% of the points whether the truth was 0.5%, 2% or 10%. Underneath every one of those settings, the score ranked the planted anomalies with an AUC of 0.99 or more.

So the detector's real output is a ranking, and the flag is a cut through it that someone chose. Section 12.1 made the same point for classifiers: the threshold that turns a score into a decision is a decision about costs, not a property of the model. Here there is not even a calibrated probability to anchor the cut. Choosing it needs either a labelled sample, from which precision at each cut can be estimated, or a budget, the number of cases someone can afford to investigate. The table below shows every combination tried.

### A crowd of anomalies looks normal from inside


The ranking depends on what the score compares a point with. The second simulation plants fifty anomalies, all as if from one fault, packed tightly together (standard deviation 0.1, centred at (5, 5)) far from 2,000 normal points. A local method that looks at a point's $k$ nearest neighbours sees, for any $k$ up to 49, only other anomalies, densely packed. The block compares LOF and the k-NN distance at two neighbourhood sizes, and the isolation forest.

```python
m = unsup.anomaly_masking(ks=(20, 100))      # a group of 50 anomalies
for r in m["rows"]:
    print(f"k={r['k']:3d}  LOF AUC {r['lof']:.2f}  k-NN distance AUC "
          f"{r['knn']:.2f}")
print(f"isolation forest AUC {m['iforest']:.2f}")
```

```text
k= 20  LOF AUC 0.54  k-NN distance AUC 0.17
k=100  LOF AUC 1.00  k-NN distance AUC 1.00
isolation forest AUC 1.00
```

With $k$ of 5 or 20, LOF scored the group with an AUC between 0.54 and 0.58, barely better than a coin. The plain distance to the $k$-th neighbour did worse, between 0.17 and 0.19: by that score the anomalies looked *more* normal than the normal points, because they were more tightly packed than any normal neighbourhood. Section 22.2, which reran single-draw results from across the book on fresh seeds, repeated this on five other seeds; the distance score's worst AUC ranged from 0.19 to 0.24, always far below chance.

Once the neighbourhood is larger than the group, the picture changes. At $k = 50$ each anomaly's $k$-th neighbour is outside the group, far away, and the distance score found them perfectly; LOF still did not (0.57). At $k = 100$ both did. The isolation forest, which subsamples the data and isolates points with random splits rather than comparing neighbours, scored 1.00 throughout: a tight group far from the bulk is still cut off from it by a few splits.

This is *masking*, an old problem in outlier statistics: one anomaly is conspicuous, but many similar ones form a cluster and shield one another from detection. A fault that produces many identical bad records is exactly that case. The figure below plots the AUC against the neighbourhood size.

![AUC of three detectors against the neighbourhood size k for a group of 50 anomalies. At k = 20 the distance score's AUC was 0.17: the anomalies looked more normal than the normal points.](ml159-f1-masking.png){alt="Two curves near or below 0.5 for k of 5 and 20; the distance score reaches 1 at k = 50 and the outlier factor at 100; a flat line at 1 for the isolation forest."}

### Two features that matter among many that do not


An anomaly is usually unusual in a few features, not in all of them. The third simulation shifts 40 anomalies among 2,000 points by 3.5 standard deviations in two features, and gives the data 2, 10, 50 or 200 features in all; the extra features are pure noise. Averaged over six draws of the data and the forest, the isolation forest's AUC went from 1.00 to 0.96, 0.76 and 0.58. Single draws at 200 features ranged between 0.50 and 0.68: little or no better than chance. Given only the two relevant features, it scored 0.997.

The isolation forest's construction suggests why. Each random split chooses a feature at random, so with two relevant features among 200, almost every split is spent on noise, and an anomaly is isolated little faster than a normal point. The control on the two relevant features alone supports that explanation without measuring it directly.

LOF behaved differently. Its AUC rose from 0.67 with two features to 0.87 with 50 and fell to 0.75 with 200. The fall is the dilution Section 18.1 above measured for nearest neighbours: distances computed over many dimensions still carry the two shifted features, but less and less as noise dimensions are added. The low start is probably the masking described above. In two dimensions the 40 anomalies form a little cluster of their own, and LOF looks at 20 neighbours, fewer than the group; extra noise features spread the anomalies apart and break the cluster up.

Zimek, Schubert and Kriegel's survey of outlier detection in high dimensions makes the general case: irrelevant attributes hide anomalies that exist only in a subspace, and which method suffers most depends on how it uses distances. The practical lesson is older than either method: a detector can only find what the features make unusual, so the choice of features is most of the model. The table below collects the averages.

![AUC of the isolation forest and LOF as noise features are added, and of the isolation forest on the two relevant features alone. Values are means over six draws of data and forest; with 40 anomalies, an AUC near 0.5 has a standard error of about 0.05.](ml159-f2-dims.png){alt="A four-row table. The isolation forest falls from 1.00 to about 0.58 on average; LOF rises then falls; on the two relevant features alone the forest stays near 1."}

### Malignant tumours among benign ones


The replication uses the breast-cancer data. It takes the 357 benign tumours, adds eight malignant ones drawn at random, standardises all 30 features, and repeats the draw 20 times. The isolation forest ranked the eight with an AUC of 0.95 on average, between 0.85 and 0.99 across draws; LOF averaged 0.93. Malignant tumours are larger and more irregular, so in these features they are unusual, and an unsupervised score finds most of them.

Turning the ranking into flags shows the cost of having no labels. Flagging 5% of the 365 tumours, about eighteen, caught 71% of the malignant ones on average, but between 38% and 88% depending on which eight were drawn and on the forest's seed, with a precision of 0.30. Which malignant cases look unusual, and which benign ones also do, decides the result, and nothing in the procedure says which it will be on a given day.

An anomaly detector, then, ranks rows by how poorly they fit a description of normality built from the features and neighbourhoods it is given, and someone else decides where to cut.

## In practice

### Distance and the number of columns

- **Measure before blaming dimension.** For a sample of rows, compute each row's nearest distance divided by its median distance to the rest, and compare with uniform data in a range of dimensions. The matching uniform dimension is a rough guide to how many directions your data really use.
- **Do not rule out distance-based methods by column count.** On 64 correlated pixels 5-NN beat logistic regression.
- **Look for columns that carry nothing.** Irrelevant columns enter an unweighted distance on equal terms with informative ones. Remove them, down-weight them, or scale features so that the distance reflects what matters.
- **Do any selection inside the cross-validation folds.** Choosing columns on all the rows lets the test rows influence the choice.
- **When many columns may be irrelevant, use a method that weights or selects them.** Logistic regression, which learns per-column weights, tolerated 1,024 noise columns far better than an unweighted distance, and 5-NN after selection inside the folds did better still.

### Anomaly detection

- **Treat the score as the output and the flag as your decision.** Keep the full ranking; do not let `contamination` or `'auto'` choose how many rows are flagged.
- **Choose the cut from a budget or a labelled sample.** Flag as many rows as can be investigated, or label a sample and estimate precision at several cuts. Do not read the contamination setting as an estimate of the anomaly rate.
- **Ask whether anomalies could arrive in groups.** If one fault can produce many similar records, a local method needs a neighbourhood larger than the group; check results at several values of $k$, or compare with an isolation forest.
- **Select features before detecting.** Anomalies unusual in a few features are diluted by many irrelevant ones, as nearest neighbours were by noise columns. Use the features in which an anomaly would be expected to show, and compare with a run on that subset.
- **Inspect the top of the ranking.** Look at the flagged rows and those just below the cut, preferably with someone who knows the data, before trusting either the score or the cut.

## Limits of the evidence

### The curse of dimensionality (Section 18.1)

- **Uniform noise is the worst kind.** Real irrelevant features are often correlated with each other or nearly constant, and fill fewer dimensions than independent uniform columns; they hurt less.
- **Euclidean distance, unweighted.** Scaling or selecting features, learning a metric, or using a representation from a trained model all change what "near" means, and are the standard repairs. None was applied here, by design, apart from the selection step.
- **One data set with low intrinsic dimension.** Data that genuinely spread in many directions, such as some text or genomic data, can be nearly as hard as the uniform case. The column count does not say which case a data set is in; the distance ratio, or an intrinsic-dimension estimate, does.
- **$k = 5$, one split scheme.** Other choices of $k$ move the numbers a little and not the pattern.

### Anomaly detectors (Section 18.2)

- **Planted anomalies.** The simulated anomalies were shifted versions of normal points, the easiest kind to define. Real anomalies include points that are individually ordinary and unusual only in combination or in sequence, which these detectors do not look for.
- **Scores on the training data.** Every score was computed on the data the detector was fitted to, which is how the tools are commonly used. Fitting on clean data and scoring new data would presumably remove some masking, since the group would not be among the neighbours, and none of the feature problem; neither was measured.
- **Two detectors, default settings.** Other methods, such as one-class support vector machines or autoencoders, use distances or reconstructions differently and fail differently. The masking and irrelevant-feature effects were measured for these two.
- **Breast-cancer features are good ones.** Its 30 features were chosen by people who knew what malignancy looks like. Features chosen without that knowledge would be closer to the 200-feature simulation.

## Key points

1. For points that fill their space, distances concentrate: the nearest-to-median ratio rose from 0.03 in 2 uniform dimensions to 0.94 in 1,024.
2. Real data rarely fill their columns. The digits' 64 pixels had the ratio of about 8 uniform dimensions, and 5-NN scored 0.986.
3. Irrelevant columns do fill their dimensions. With 1,024 noise columns 5-NN fell to 0.659; logistic regression, which weights columns, fell to 0.871. Selecting 64 columns by F statistic inside the folds, with the noise still present, restored 5-NN to 0.982.
4. Before blaming dimension, measure the nearest-to-median distance ratio of your data, and remove or down-weight columns that carry nothing.
5. The contamination parameter flagged exactly its own value; `'auto'` flagged 10.5% to 13.4% whatever the true rate. The ranking underneath scored an AUC of 0.99 or more throughout.
6. Fifty grouped anomalies looked normal to local scores until the neighbourhood was larger than the group; at $k = 20$ the distance score's AUC was 0.17, and on five other seeds its worst AUC stayed far below chance.
7. Anomalous in 2 features of 200, the planted anomalies were found by the isolation forest with an average AUC of 0.58; on the two features alone, 0.997.
8. Eight malignant tumours among 357 benign ones were ranked well (average AUC 0.95), but flagging 5% caught between 38% and 88% of them depending on the draw and the forest's seed.
9. Treat an anomaly score as a ranking under assumptions about features and neighbourhoods. Choose how many to flag from a budget or a labelled sample, and check what the top of the ranking actually contains.

2. If the matching dimension in Exercise 1 is far below your column count, the curse is not your problem. If it is close, check how many of your columns actually carry information about the target, for instance with an F statistic computed inside each training fold.
3. Explain why the relative spread of Euclidean distances between uniform points shrinks as the dimension grows, and which assumption of that argument the digits' pixels violate.
4. Explain why logistic regression is less affected by appended noise columns than a nearest-neighbour classifier.
5. Take the rows your detector flagged most recently and the next 50 below the cut. Look at both groups by hand, or have someone who knows the data do it. If the two groups look alike, the cut is arbitrary and the ranking is all there is. If the flagged group is dominated by one kind of row, check whether many similar rows are sitting just as far from normal, and whether your neighbourhood size could see them.
6. Explain why the k-NN distance score can rank a tight group of anomalies below normal points, giving an AUC under 0.5, while the isolation forest ranks the same group perfectly.
7. A colleague sets `contamination=0.01` because "about 1% of transactions are fraud". Explain what the setting actually does and what you would need to know to choose the cut instead.

## Further reading

- Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan and Shaft, "When is 'nearest neighbor' meaningful?", *ICDT* (1999): the conditions under which nearest and farthest distances become indistinguishable.
- Levina and Bickel, "Maximum likelihood estimation of intrinsic dimension", *NeurIPS* (2004): estimating how many dimensions data actually occupy from nearest-neighbour distances.
- Liu, Ting and Zhou, "Isolation forest", *ICDM* (2008): the original isolation forest and its path-length score.
- Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander, "LOF: identifying density-based local outliers", *SIGMOD* (2000): the local outlier factor and the idea of comparing a point's density with its neighbours'.
- Zimek, Schubert and Kriegel, "A survey on unsupervised outlier detection in high-dimensional numerical data", *Statistical Analysis and Data Mining* (2012): how irrelevant attributes hide anomalies and how different detectors suffer.
- Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits, UCI Machine Learning Repository: the digits data, as bundled with scikit-learn.
