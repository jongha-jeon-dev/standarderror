# An Average of Models Beats the Average Model, Not the Best One

The first forty chapters of this book measured single models and the data under them. Part IX turns to what is done with several models: combining them, stacking them, and deciding which is better. The most common combination is the simplest. Train a few different models, average their predicted probabilities, and report the average, on the strength of a sentence that sounds like a theorem: *averaging several models gives a better model than any of them.* A practitioner meets it whenever an ensemble is assembled from the models to hand and adopted without being compared with its strongest member. If the sentence is false, the ensemble can be worse than a model the team already had, and costlier to run.

This chapter tests the sentence on six classifiers and the handwritten digits. It counts how many averaged combinations beat their best member, traces the worst failures to one mechanism, confidence weighting, and then states the theorem that does stand behind the sentence and shows on a regression problem what it compares the average with. It closes with the conditions under which averaging pays.

## Few averages beat their best member

The data are the UCI handwritten digits bundled with scikit-learn, 1,797 images. Six classifiers are trained on half the images and scored on the other half, over five stratified half splits: regularised logistic regression (C = 0.1), an RBF support vector machine, five-nearest-neighbours (5-NN), a random forest of 300 trees, Gaussian naive Bayes and a single, fully grown decision tree. Their accuracies range from 0.825 for the tree to 0.981 for nearest neighbours. The SVM's probabilities come from *Platt scaling*, a logistic regression fitted from its decision values to the labels on held-out folds of the training data; the other models output class probabilities natively.

For every combination of two or more models, 57 in all, the code averages the members' class probabilities with equal weights, predicts the class with the largest average, and compares the result's accuracy with two reference points: the best member's accuracy and the members' mean accuracy.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

t = combine.averaging_table()   # 57 combinations, 5 half-splits
best = sum(r["acc"] > r["best_acc"] for r in t)
below = sum(r["acc"] < r["mean_acc"] for r in t)
print(f"beat their best member: {best} of {len(t)}")
print(f"below their members' mean accuracy: {below} of {len(t)}")
top = max(t, key=lambda r: r["acc"])
print(f"best combination: {' + '.join(top['members'])}, "
      f"{top['acc']:.4f} against its best member's {top['best_acc']:.4f}")
```

```text
beat their best member: 11 of 57
below their members' mean accuracy: 8 of 57
best combination: SVM + 5-NN + forest, 0.9873 against its best member's 0.9813
```

Only 11 of the 57 averages beat the best model they contain. The best average, of the SVM, nearest neighbours and the forest, gained 0.0060 over nearest neighbours alone, about half a point. And 8 averages did worse than the *mean* accuracy of their own members, a reference point an average is widely assumed to beat by construction. The figure below plots each combination against its best member; most of the damage is done by combinations containing the tree.

![One of the 31 combinations containing the decision tree beat its best member, pairing it with naive Bayes; 11 of 57 combinations did.](ml141-f0-combos.png){alt="Scatter of 57 combinations. Combinations containing the decision tree range from about 0.83 to 0.98, all below the line but one; those with naive Bayes but no tree sit around 0.85 to 0.98; those with neither sit near or slightly above the line."}

## Averaging probabilities weights each model by its confidence

### A certain model wins every disagreement

The worst averages contain the decision tree, and the worst of those are pairs. Averaged with logistic regression, the SVM or the forest, the pair scored exactly the tree's accuracy, 0.825.

The reason is in what a fully grown tree predicts. It splits until every leaf holds one class, and the probability it reports is the class proportion in the leaf a new image reaches. Every leaf is pure, so the tree gives probability 1 to one digit and 0 to the rest, on 100% of the digits. The other three models are almost never that certain; the forest is certain on 0.3% of digits. The next block averages the SVM and the tree and records, where the two disagree, whose label the average adopted.

```python
p = combine.averaging_pair("SVM", "tree")
print(f"SVM + tree: accuracy {p['acc']:.4f}")
print(f"on {p['disagree']} disagreements the average sided with "
      f"the tree {p['with_b']:.0%} of the time")
```

```text
SVM + tree: accuracy 0.8251
on 819 disagreements the average sided with the tree 100% of the time
```

On all 819 disagreements the average sided with the tree. The arithmetic makes this inevitable. Write $p_k$ for the tree's probability of class $k$ and $q_k$ for the SVM's; the average gives class $k$ the value $(p_k + q_k)/2$. The tree's class has $p_k = 1$ and so receives at least one half. Any other class $j$ has $p_j = 0$ and receives $q_j/2$, which reaches one half only if the SVM is certain of $j$. An average of probabilities is therefore not a vote of equals: a member's influence on a row grows with how far its probabilities are from uniform, and a member certain about everything has the largest possible influence everywhere.

### Larger averages dilute the certain member, but not enough

In an average of $M$ members, the tree's class is guaranteed only a share of $1/M$, and confident agreement among the others can outweigh it. Averages containing the tree recover as members are added, but only one of the 31 combinations containing the tree beat its best member: the tree with naive Bayes, the two weakest models of the six.

Naive Bayes shows the same mechanism to a lesser degree. It is certain about 52% of digits; averaged with the SVM it won 86% of their disagreements, and the pair scored 0.857. The table below collects each member's accuracy, log loss and share of certain predictions, and four averages with the share of disagreements each second member won.

Calibrating it removes that, but the pair still trails the SVM alone.](ml141-f2-table.png){alt="An eleven-row table: seven members with accuracy, log loss and share of certain predictions, then four averages with the share of disagreements won by the second model."}

### Calibration removes the certainty, not the weakness

*Isotonic recalibration* fits a non-decreasing step function from a model's scores to observed class frequencies. Done by internal cross-validation, as here with five folds, the mapping is learned on data the tree was not fitted on, so it reflects how often the tree's confident predictions are right (Chapter 34 examined how many rows such a recalibration needs). Recalibrated, the tree is certain about no digit, and the SVM-plus-tree average rises to 0.973.

That is far better, and still below the SVM alone, 0.977. Calibration fixed the tree's influence but not its quality: averaging with a weaker model is a cost unless it is right where the stronger one is wrong.

## What the theorem guarantees: the average member, not the best

### The ambiguity decomposition

There is a real theorem behind the sentence, and it says less. Take $M$ regression models $f_1, \dots, f_M$ and their equally weighted average $\bar f = \frac{1}{M}\sum_m f_m$. Krogh and Vedelsby showed in 1995 that at every point with target $y$,

$$
(\bar f - y)^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(f_m - y)^2 \;-\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(f_m - \bar f)^2 .
$$

The first term is the mean member's squared error; the second is the members' spread around their average, which they called the *ambiguity*. The identity follows from writing $f_m - y = (f_m - \bar f) + (\bar f - y)$ and squaring: the cross terms vanish because the deviations $f_m - \bar f$ sum to zero. Averaged over test points it holds for mean squared error too. Since the ambiguity cannot be negative, the average is never worse than the average member, and it improves on that member by exactly as much as the members disagree. The best member does not appear.

The next block checks the identity on the diabetes data of Efron and colleagues, one half split, with four regressors: ridge regression with a cross-validated penalty, 10-nearest-neighbours, a random forest and a single tree.

```python
a = combine.ambiguity()          # diabetes, four regressors
print(f"mean member {a['mean_member']:.1f} - spread "
      f"{a['ambiguity']:.1f} = average {a['ensemble']:.1f} "
      f"(gap {a['identity_gap']:.0e})")
print("members: " + ", ".join(f"{k} {v:.0f}"
                              for k, v in a["members"].items()))
```

```text
mean member 4018.6 - spread 576.3 = average 3442.4 (gap 9e-13)
members: ridge 3041, 10-NN 3152, forest 3552, tree 6329
```

The identity holds to twelve decimal places. The average improves on the mean member by 576, all of it the members' disagreement, and is still worse than ridge regression alone, 3442 against 3041. The tree's error was so large that the mean member started far behind the best one, and the spread did not close the gap, as the figure below shows.

![The ambiguity decomposition on diabetes: 4019 − 576 = 3442, to within 9e-13; ridge alone scored 3041.](ml141-f1-ambiguity.png){alt="Bars for ridge, 10-NN, forest and tree, the tree much higher; a bar for the mean member and a lower bar for the average, which is still above ridge."}

### Log loss has a similar guarantee; accuracy has none

*Log loss* is the mean of $-\log \hat p_i$, where $\hat p_i$ is the probability given to row $i$'s true class. Because $-\log p$ is convex, its value at an average of probabilities is at most the average of its values (Jensen's inequality), so the averaged probabilities' log loss cannot exceed the members' mean. On the digits it was at or below the members' mean in all 57 combinations, and below the best member's in 13. Accuracy, which depends only on which class has the largest probability, is not convex and has no such guarantee; that is how 8 combinations fell below their members' mean.

## When averaging pays

The combinations that beat their best member share two properties: members of similar quality, wrong on different digits. The SVM and nearest neighbours, 0.977 and 0.981 alone, disagreed on 123 digits across the five splits and averaged to 0.9867, better than either. By analogy with the squared-error decomposition, when members have similar errors the mean member is close to the best, so even modest disagreement can carry the average past both. Dietterich's account of why ensembles work rests on exactly these conditions, *accuracy* and *diversity*, and random forests are built to meet them: many trees of equal quality, made different on purpose.

The useful version of the sentence is conditional. An average of models that are about equally good and differently wrong is usually better than each; an average that includes a much weaker or much more confident model is usually worse than the best. Calibrating members first stops confidence from deciding the vote, and weighting them by validated performance stops a weak model diluting a strong one. Learning those weights is stacking, the subject of Chapter 42.

## In practice

- **Compare an ensemble with its best member** on the same held-out rows, not only with the members' mean. An average that does not beat its best member is cost without gain.
- **Check each member's confidence.** Compute the share of predictions each makes with probability above 0.99. A member certain about most rows will dominate wherever it disagrees.
- **Calibrate members before averaging probabilities,** especially single trees and naive Bayes. Calibration removes overconfidence; it does not make a weak member strong.
- **Measure diversity directly:** the share of rows on which each pair of members disagrees. Averaging helps when members of similar quality disagree, and hurts when the disagreement comes from a much weaker member.
- **Do not cite the ambiguity decomposition as a promise of gains over the best model.** It guarantees improvement over the mean member for squared error, as convexity does for log loss; nothing is guaranteed for accuracy.

## Limits of the evidence

- **One data set where a single model is excellent.** Nearest neighbours alone scores 0.98 on the digits, leaving little room for any combination to gain. Harder tasks with more balanced members may leave more room; that was not measured here.
- **Unweighted averages.** Every member counted equally. Weighted averages and stacking are the obvious refinements, and their failure modes are the subject of Chapter 42.
- **A deliberately bad member.** A fully grown single tree is not something anyone would put in an ensemble on purpose; it is here because its certainty makes the mechanism visible. Any overconfident member, such as an unregularised boosted model late in training, does the same in a milder form.
- **Five splits.** The counts of combinations above and below their best member come from one set of five half splits and could shift with others; the tree's capture of every pair it joined with a less certain model follows from its probabilities and would not.

## Key points

1. Of 57 averaged combinations of six classifiers on the digits, 11 beat their best member and 8 fell below their members' mean accuracy.
2. Averaging probabilities weights each model by its confidence. A tree certain about every digit won all 819 disagreements with the SVM, the pair scored 0.825, and only one of the 31 combinations containing the tree, the one pairing it with naive Bayes, beat its best member.
3. The guarantee is against the mean member: for squared error, the average's error is the mean member's minus their spread, exactly. On diabetes that still left it behind ridge regression alone.
4. Log loss has a similar guarantee by convexity; accuracy has none.
5. Average models of similar quality that make different mistakes, calibrate them first, and compare the average with the best single model before adopting it.
