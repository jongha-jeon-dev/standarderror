# Resampling Moves the Threshold and Breaks a Logistic Model's Probabilities

When the positive class is rare, as with fraud, a disease or a manufacturing defect, a classifier trained on the data as it comes predicts the negative class almost everywhere, and at the default cut of 0.5 it catches few positives. The first fix most courses teach is to balance the classes before training. The sentence behind it is that *imbalance is a problem in the data, and resampling fixes it.* A practitioner meets this sentence in nearly every tutorial on rare-event classification and in many production pipelines. If it is false, the resampling step buys nothing that a different threshold would not, and it may damage the model's probabilities, which other systems then consume as if they were real.

This chapter separates three things a classifier produces and measures what each correction does to each of them. It starts with logistic regression on a simulated task at 2% prevalence: first the ranking, then the decisions, then the probabilities, including a correction that undoes the damage. It then repeats the comparison with boosted trees, where the picture is different in both directions, and checks the ranking result on the digits.

## Ranking, probabilities and decisions

The usual corrections come in four forms:

- *Oversampling*: duplicating minority-class rows until the classes are balanced.
- *SMOTE* (synthetic minority over-sampling technique): creating new minority rows by interpolating between a minority row and one of its nearest minority-class neighbours.
- *Undersampling*: discarding majority-class rows until the classes are balanced.
- *Class weights*: keeping the data as it is but weighting the minority class up in the training loss.

To see what each changes, it helps to separate three things a classifier produces.

Its **ranking** is the order in which it places cases from most to least likely positive. It is measured by the *ROC AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative; by *average precision*, a summary of the precision–recall curve that weights the region where positives are found; or by precision at a fixed recall. *Recall* is the fraction of true positives that are flagged; *precision* is the fraction of flagged cases that are truly positive.

Its **probabilities** are *calibrated* if a predicted 10% comes true 10% of the time. *Log loss*, the average negative log of the probability assigned to the true class, rewards good ranking and good calibration together, and so penalises probabilities that are systematically too high or too low.

Its **decisions** are which cases get flagged at whatever threshold is applied to its scores.

A simulated task makes all three measurable at once. Each training set has 5,000 rows of eight standard-normal features, with positives at 2%, about 100 positives, differing from negatives in the means of three features and the spread of a fourth. The test set has 200,000 rows at the same prevalence, large enough that its own noise is negligible. The five versions of logistic regression, plain and with each correction, are trained on the same rows in each of twenty replicates.

```python
from standarderror.ml import imbalance as im

lo = im.compare(model="logistic", reps=20)   # 2% positive
for m, v in lo["summary"].items():
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {v['auc']:.4f}  mean prob {v['mean_p']:.3f}  "
          f"log loss {v['log_loss']:.3f}  recall@0.5 {v['recall_half']:.2f}")
```

```text
plain         AUC 0.9252  mean prob 0.020  log loss 0.061  recall@0.5 0.19
oversample    AUC 0.9240  mean prob 0.215  log loss 0.334  recall@0.5 0.83
SMOTE         AUC 0.9240  mean prob 0.192  log loss 0.313  recall@0.5 0.82
undersample   AUC 0.9213  mean prob 0.232  log loss 0.359  recall@0.5 0.84
class weights AUC 0.9240  mean prob 0.216  log loss 0.336  recall@0.5 0.84
```

The first column, the ranking, barely moves. The second and third, the probabilities, move a lot. The last column, recall at the 0.5 cut, is the one resampling is used for, and it moves from 0.19 to 0.82 or more. The following sections take the three in turn.

## The ranking: no better, slightly worse

Because every version trains on the same rows in each replicate, the differences from the plain model are *paired*, in the sense of Chapter 1: replicate-to-replicate variation common to all versions cancels in the difference, and the standard errors of the differences are small enough to show their sign. The code prints the mean paired difference from the plain model in AUC and in precision at 80% recall, each with its standard error.

```python
for m, d in lo["paired"].items():
    a, p = d["auc"], d["precision_r80"]
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {a['mean']:+.4f} +- {a['se']:.4f}   "
          f"precision at 80% recall {p['mean']:+.4f} +- {p['se']:.4f}")
```

```text
oversample    AUC -0.0012 +- 0.0004   precision at 80% recall -0.0023 +- 0.0007
SMOTE         AUC -0.0012 +- 0.0004   precision at 80% recall -0.0023 +- 0.0008
undersample   AUC -0.0039 +- 0.0006   precision at 80% recall -0.0066 +- 0.0011
class weights AUC -0.0012 +- 0.0005   precision at 80% recall -0.0022 +- 0.0009
```

Oversampling, SMOTE and class weights each cost about 0.0012 of AUC: tiny, and consistently negative. None of them improved the ranking in the measurement, and precision at 80% recall fell slightly too. Undersampling, which discards roughly 98% of the majority rows and so trains on far less information, costs about three times as much.

That is what the algebra predicts for logistic regression. The model's score for a row is its log-odds, a linear function of the features plus an intercept. Duplicating positives, or giving them more weight, tells the fit that positives are more common than they are, and the fit responds mostly by raising the intercept: every row's log-odds goes up by roughly the same amount. A shift applied equally to every row cannot reorder them, so the ranking is unchanged. What little reordering there is comes from the slopes being fitted to a reweighted sample, and here it does not help. ## The decisions: a threshold does the same job

If the ranking did not change, the jump in recall must have come from somewhere else, and it did. Raising every log-odds by the same amount and then cutting at 0.5 is equivalent to leaving the log-odds alone and cutting lower. The direct test is to take the plain model and move its threshold until it flags as many positives as each corrected model does at 0.5, then compare precision at that matched recall.

```python
for m in ("oversample", "SMOTE", "class weights"):
    v = lo["matched"][m]
    print(f"{m:13s} recall {v['recall']:.3f}: precision "
          f"{v['method_precision']:.3f} at 0.5, plain "
          f"{v['plain_precision']:.3f} at {v['plain_threshold']:.3f}")
```

```text
oversample    recall 0.834: precision 0.105 at 0.5, plain 0.106 at 0.021
SMOTE         recall 0.819: precision 0.112 at 0.5, plain 0.113 at 0.023
class weights recall 0.836: precision 0.104 at 0.5, plain 0.105 at 0.021
```

At the same recall the plain model is as precise as every corrected one, or slightly more. The oversampled model's cut at 0.5 corresponds to a plain-model threshold of about 0.021, almost exactly the prevalence.

That value has a meaning. For a calibrated model, decision theory gives the threshold that minimises expected cost: flag a case when its probability exceeds $C_{\mathrm{FP}} / (C_{\mathrm{FP}} + C_{\mathrm{FN}})$, where $C_{\mathrm{FP}}$ is the cost of a false alarm and $C_{\mathrm{FN}}$ the cost of a missed positive. A threshold near the prevalence is what this rule gives when a missed positive is weighted like 49 false alarms. Balancing the classes at 2% prevalence imposes that weighting implicitly.

That is the real content of "fixing" imbalance: choosing a different trade-off between missed positives and false alarms. It is a decision about costs, and a threshold expresses it directly, can be changed after training as costs change, and does not touch the model. Chapter 46 develops the threshold as a cost ratio in full.

## The probabilities: what resampling breaks

For logistic regression, the cost of making that choice by resampling shows up in the probabilities. The plain model's mean prediction is 0.020 against a true rate of 0.02: it is calibrated, as maximum-likelihood logistic regression with an intercept tends to be on average. The oversampled model's mean prediction is 0.215, about 11 times too high, and its log loss is 5.4 times worse. It was trained on a world in which positives are as common as negatives, and it reports probabilities for that world.

Anyone who reads those numbers as probabilities, to rank cases by expected cost, to set a threshold by a business rule, to combine the model with another, or to tell a patient a risk, is reading the wrong world. Van den Goorbergh and colleagues found exactly this in clinical risk models: imbalance corrections improved no discrimination measure and produced strongly overestimated risks.

### The prior correction

For logistic regression the damage is reversible, because it is mostly an intercept shift. If the model was trained at a positive rate $\pi_{\mathrm{train}}$ and will be used where the true rate is $\pi$, each predicted probability $\hat p$ can be mapped back by multiplying its odds by the ratio of the true prior odds to the training prior odds:

$$
\frac{\tilde p}{1 - \tilde p} = \frac{\hat p}{1 - \hat p} \cdot \frac{\pi / (1 - \pi)}{\pi_{\mathrm{train}} / (1 - \pi_{\mathrm{train}})}.
$$

This is the adjustment for a change in class priors described by Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker. It is a monotone transformation, so it leaves the ranking exactly as it was. The code applies it to the oversampled model.

```python
c = lo["corrected"]
print(f"oversampled, prior-corrected: mean prob {c['mean_p']:.4f}  "
      f"log loss {c['log_loss']:.4f}  AUC {c['auc']:.4f}")
```

```text
oversampled, prior-corrected: mean prob 0.0206  log loss 0.0620  AUC 0.9240
```

Mean prediction and log loss are back to the plain model's, and the AUC is the oversampled model's, unchanged. That is a long way round to arrive where the plain model started. The figure below shows the three models' calibration.

![Oversampled, a logistic model's predicted probability is about ten times the real rate: mean prediction 0.215 against a prevalence of 0.02. One line of algebra puts it back.](ml105-f1-calibration.png){alt="A plain-model line on the diagonal, an oversampled line far below it, and a corrected line back on the diagonal."}

## For boosted trees it is not even neutral

Logistic regression can only shift and tilt a hyperplane, so resampling has little room to change its ranking. A flexible model has more room. The next block runs the same paired comparison with gradient-boosted trees over ten training sets, printing the change in AUC with its standard error and the change in average precision.

```python
bo = im.compare(model="boosted trees", reps=10)
for m, d in bo["paired"].items():
    print(f"{m:13s} AUC {d['auc']['mean']:+.4f} +- {d['auc']['se']:.4f}"
          f"   avg precision {d['ap']['mean']:+.4f}")
```

```text
oversample    AUC -0.0093 +- 0.0011   avg precision +0.0006
SMOTE         AUC -0.0133 +- 0.0031   avg precision -0.0457
undersample   AUC -0.0013 +- 0.0030   avg precision -0.0562
class weights AUC -0.0099 +- 0.0012   avg precision -0.0060
```

### The ranking gets worse

Oversampling, SMOTE and class weights each lower the AUC by about 0.01, well outside their standard errors, and SMOTE loses 0.046 of average precision. Undersampling barely moves the AUC (-0.0013, inside its standard error) but loses 0.056 of average precision, which matters because average precision concentrates on the top of the ranking, where a rare-class problem is decided. Why the trees lose ranking was not measured here. A likely part of it is that a flexible model can fit the duplicated or synthetic positives themselves rather than the region they came from. At its own operating point the SMOTE model reaches 0.221 precision; the plain model with its threshold moved to the same recall reaches 0.288.

### The probabilities can get better

The probabilities tell a different story from logistic regression's. The plain trees are *under*-confident about positives: their mean prediction is 0.008 against a prevalence of 0.02, and their log loss, 0.102, is worse than the plain logistic model's. Oversampling and class weights move the mean to 0.017 and 0.018, closer to the truth and not past it, and cut the log loss to 0.087 and 0.087. SMOTE and undersampling overshoot, to log losses of 0.124 and 0.495. So "resampling breaks the probabilities" is a statement about a model that starts well calibrated. For one that starts miscalibrated, resampling can move it either way, and the only way to know is to measure.

The same fact explains why the prior correction does not help the trees. It assumes the corrected model's probabilities are inflated by the change in priors, as logistic regression's were. The oversampled trees' probabilities were never inflated: their mean, 0.017, is below the prevalence. Dividing their odds by about 49 therefore pushes them far too low, and the log loss gets worse, not better. Chapter 34 treats recalibration methods that learn the correction from data instead of assuming it.

### The digits agree on the ranking

The digits, recast as the question "is this an 8?" at about 10% positive, with logistic regression over 30 splits, agree with the simulation. The plain model has the highest AUC (0.9749) and the highest precision at 80% recall (0.746), against 0.707 oversampled and 0.649 undersampled. The table below collects the results.

## In practice

- **Separate ranking, probabilities and decisions when evaluating a correction.** Report AUC or average precision for the ranking, calibration or log loss for the probabilities, and precision and recall at the operating point for the decisions.
- **Move the threshold before resampling.** If the goal is more recall, lower the plain model's threshold until it reaches the recall you need. Here that matched every correction's precision or beat it, without retraining.
- **Choose the threshold from costs.** A threshold near the prevalence corresponds to weighting a miss like many false alarms; make that weighting explicit and set the threshold from it.
- **Check what consumes the probabilities.** If a resampled or reweighted logistic model feeds a dashboard, an expected-cost calculation or another model, compare its mean prediction with the true rate, and apply the prior correction or retrain on the data as it comes.
- **For flexible models, measure rather than assume.** On boosted trees the corrections lowered the ranking but improved under-confident probabilities. Check calibration before deciding either way.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Severe imbalance with tiny counts.** With about 100 positives the plain model is well estimated. With ten, every method is noisy, and some regularisation from reweighting can help a badly overfitted model; the fix there is regularisation or more positives, not balance.
- **Models that ignore the threshold.** Some pipelines take the argmax and have no threshold to move, such as a deep network's softmax inside a larger system. There reweighting is the only lever available, and it is the threshold in disguise; it should still be corrected before the probabilities are used.
- **Losses that are not proper.** A *proper* loss, such as log loss, is one minimised by reporting the true probabilities. The comparison assumes the model is trained on log loss. Trained to maximise accuracy directly, a model on imbalanced data can genuinely learn to ignore the minority class, and reweighting then changes what it learns.
- **One simulated task.** The tree result may depend on how closely the trees can fit individual rows; their regularisation was not varied here. The direction, no gain in ranking, held in all three settings measured.

## Key points

1. Separate ranking, probabilities and decisions. Imbalance corrections change the last two and, at best, leave the first alone.
2. On logistic regression at 2% prevalence, oversampling, SMOTE and class weights changed AUC by about -0.0012; undersampling by -0.0039. The recall they gain at 0.5 the plain model gets with a threshold near the prevalence (0.021), at the same precision or better.
3. On logistic regression they inflate predicted probabilities about 11-fold, and the prior correction undoes it.
4. On boosted trees they made the ranking worse: about -0.01 AUC, and 0.046 of average precision for SMOTE. The plain trees' probabilities were too low (mean 0.008), and oversampling and class weights improved them; check calibration before assuming either way.
5. Imbalance is not a defect in the data. It is a cost question, and a threshold is where costs belong.
