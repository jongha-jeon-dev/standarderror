# How Far More Data Goes

Chapters 1 to 4 were about reading a score that already exists: its error bar, what cross-validation estimates, what a leak adds to it and how a search inflates it. This chapter, the last of Part I, turns to decisions that come before most of the data exist: how much more data, or how much larger a model, is worth paying for, and what more data will and will not fix. A practitioner meets these decisions when a pilot study on a few hundred labelled rows has to justify a labelling budget, when a ladder of cheap small models is used to choose the size of an expensive one, and when a project with millions of rows is assumed to be safe from the problems that afflict small ones. In each case the answer is usually given by extrapolation or by reassurance, and in each case it can be checked.

The chapter tests three sentences, one per section:

- Section 5.1: *a learning curve tells you whether more data will help.*
- Section 5.2: *scaling laws predict performance.*
- Section 5.3: *with enough data, these problems go away.*

The first two sections make the same move along two different axes. Section 5.1 extrapolates a model's error along the number of training rows. It fits a power law to the early part of a learning curve on a simulated task where the best achievable error is known, compares the forecasts with what the models actually reach, and repeats the check on the digits, where a second problem appears: the model that leads at pilot size is not the one that leads at full size. Section 5.2 extrapolates a language model's loss along the number of parameters, fitting the same kind of law to a ladder of small character-level transformers trained under one fixed budget. Both find that a power law fitted early is optimistic, that the reason is a floor the early points cannot show, and that the usual repair, a third parameter for that floor, works only where the curve has already bent. Section 5.2 adds a lesson about how to judge such a law: by the pattern of its residuals across the whole ladder, not by a single correct prediction.

Section 5.3 then asks the converse question. Granted that more data buys less than an early forecast promises, which of the problems measured in this book does it remove at all? It rebuilds seven of them in compact form, from a test score's error bar and two kinds of leak to selective labels, repeated rows from the same people and a misspecified mixture model, and measures each at 200, 800 and 3,200 rows. The seven sort cleanly by the old distinction between variance and bias: problems of noise shrink as the data grow, problems in the design of the data stay, and one problem in the model's assumptions grows.

Read together, the three sections give one account of what data buys. More rows reduce variance: they make estimates less noisy and leave less luck for a search or a leak to exploit, and they bring a model closer to its own floor. They do not reveal what lies beyond the range already measured, whether that is the floor of a learning curve or the bend in a scaling law, and they do not repair a sample drawn from the wrong population or a model of the wrong shape. Sections 5.1 and 5.2 can be read on their own; Section 5.3 draws on problems from Chapters 1 to 4 and from later chapters, and summarises each where it is used.

## A Learning Curve Fitted Early Promises More Than More Data Delivers

<!-- source: ml-4-learning-curves; hero: ml104-hero.png -->


The first decision is made before there is much of a score at all. A team has run a pilot study on a few hundred labelled rows and must decide whether labelling ten or a hundred times as many is worth the cost. The standard tool is the learning curve, and the sentence under test is the one that justifies it: *a learning curve tells you whether more data will help.* If the usual way of reading a learning curve forward is biased, the team will buy labels that do not deliver the promised improvement, and may pick the wrong model on the pilot data as well.

This section builds a simulated task where the curve can be followed far beyond any pilot study and where the best achievable error is known. It fits the standard two-parameter power law to the early part of the curve and compares its forecasts with what the models actually reach. It explains the failure through the local slope of the curve, then tests the textbook fix, a power law with a floor, and shows where that fix helps and where it misleads. It ends with the same check on the digits, where a second problem appears: the model ranking at pilot size turns over.

### Learning curves and the power-law forecast


A *learning curve* plots a model's test error against the size of its training set. The standard way to extrapolate it is a power law,

$$
\mathrm{err}(n) \approx a\,n^{-b},
$$

where $n$ is the training-set size and $a$ and $b$ are fitted constants. Taking logarithms gives $\log \mathrm{err} = \log a - b \log n$, a straight line on log-log axes with slope $-b$. The procedure is to fit the line to the pilot sizes, extend it, and read off the error at the size you could afford. It is the same move as the scaling laws used to plan large neural-network training runs, made at the scale of a spreadsheet; Section 5.2 below examines those laws directly.

Whether this works can be checked only where the curve can be followed much further than the fit. The task here is built for that. It has two Gaussian classes in ten dimensions with different covariance matrices, so the best possible decision boundary is quadratic. Training sets grow from 50 rows to 25,600. Every model is scored on the same 20,000-point test set, on which the *Bayes error* is also computed. The Bayes error is the error of the true decision rule, the rule that assigns each point to the class with the higher true posterior probability; no classifier, with any amount of data, can beat it. Because the densities are known, that rule's error is computed from them on the same test points.

Three models are compared: logistic regression, which draws a linear boundary and so cannot represent the quadratic one; 15-nearest-neighbours (15-NN), which can represent a quadratic boundary but learns it slowly; and gradient-boosted trees, which can represent it too and learn it faster. The code prints the Bayes error and each model's test error at three training sizes.

```python
from standarderror.ml import curves as cu

task = cu.GaussianTask()          # quadratic Bayes boundary, d = 10
curve = cu.task_curve(task)
print(f"Bayes error {task.bayes:.4f}")
for n in (200, 1600, 25600):
    row = "  ".join(f"{k} {curve['error'][k][n]:.4f}"
                    for k in curve["error"])
    print(f"n = {n:>6,}  {row}")
```

```text
Bayes error 0.2684
n =    200  logistic 0.3896  15-NN 0.3830  boosted trees 0.3806
n =  1,600  logistic 0.3734  15-NN 0.3357  boosted trees 0.3230
n = 25,600  logistic 0.3672  15-NN 0.3128  boosted trees 0.2885
```

Logistic regression stalls, as a linear model on a quadratic problem must. 15-NN improves slowly. At 25,600 rows the boosted trees reach 0.289, still 0.020 above the Bayes error and still improving.

### The two-parameter power law runs through the floor


Now pretend the pilot study stopped at 1,600 rows. The next block fits $a\,n^{-b}$ to each flexible model's errors up to that size and extends it to 25,600 rows.

```python
for model in ("15-NN", "boosted trees"):
    f = cu.fit(curve, model, until=1600, floor=False)
    print(f"{model:13s} a n^-b from n <= 1,600 predicts "
          f"{f['predict'](25600):.4f} at 25,600;  "
          f"actual {curve['error'][model][25600]:.4f}")
```

```text
15-NN         a n^-b from n <= 1,600 predicts 0.2698 at 25,600;  actual 0.3128
boosted trees a n^-b from n <= 1,600 predicts 0.2524 at 25,600;  actual 0.2885
```

The boosted trees' prediction, 0.252, is below the Bayes error of 0.268: it forecasts an error that no classifier can achieve. The 15-NN prediction, 0.270, is 0.001 above the Bayes error, a forecast that the slowest of the flexible models will very nearly reach the best possible error, when it is in fact still 0.044 away.

These are not isolated cases. Across all nine fits, three models with pilot studies stopping at 400, 1,600 and 6,400 rows, the two-parameter power law predicted a lower error than the model actually reached at 25,600 every time, and 4 of the nine predictions fell below the Bayes error.

#### Why: the log-log slope is not constant

The reason is visible in the *local slope* of the curve on log-log axes: the change in log error divided by the change in log training size between two neighbouring sizes. A power law has a constant local slope, $-b$, at every size. The next block prints every third local slope for each model, from the smallest sizes to the largest.

```python
for model in curve["error"]:
    sl = cu.log_slope(curve, model)
    print(f"{model:13s} local log-log slope  "
          + "  ".join(f"{x:+.3f}" for x in sl[::3]))
```

```text
logistic      local log-log slope  -0.048  -0.045  -0.002
15-NN         local log-log slope  -0.113  -0.060  -0.018
boosted trees local log-log slope  -0.119  -0.096  -0.051
```

The slopes are not constant. In full they are noisy and do not shrink at every step: the boosted trees' slope stays between -0.096 and -0.063 from 100 to 1,600 rows, so over the whole pilot range their curve looks as straight as a power law. But every curve ends far flatter than it starts, the boosted trees at -0.015 between 12,800 and 25,600 rows, as it approaches a floor it cannot go through. No model's error can fall below the Bayes error, and each model has its own limit at or above it, so the curve must bend.

For the boosted trees that bend comes after the pilot stops. A straight line fitted to the early, steep part of the curve carries that steepness forward indefinitely and overshoots. The fit has no way to know that the curve will bend, because nothing in the pilot data shows it. The figure below shows the real curves flattening and the fitted ones continuing to fall.

![Fitted on the shaded range, the power law for the boosted trees predicts 0.252 at 25,600 rows, below the Bayes error of 0.268. They reach 0.289.](ml104-f0-curves.png){alt="Three falling learning curves that flatten; two dashed fitted curves that keep falling and cross a dotted Bayes-error line the real curves stay above."}

### Adding a floor, and where that fails


The textbook fix, going back to Cortes and colleagues, is a third parameter:

$$
\mathrm{err}(n) \approx a\,n^{-b} + c,
$$

where $c$, the *floor* or asymptote, is the error the model would reach with unlimited data. The power-law part then describes only the excess over the floor, so the fitted curve can flatten.

#### It works where the curve has already bent

For logistic regression the floor works. Its curve has flattened by about a thousand rows: its local slope is -0.045 between 400 and 800 rows and within ±0.011 from 800 on. Because the flattening lies inside the fitted range, the fit can see the floor, and from 6,400 rows it predicts 0.3672 against 0.3672 actual. Its fitted floor, 0.365, is far above the Bayes error, and correctly so: a linear boundary on a quadratic problem has its own limit, set by the model class rather than by the data.

#### It guesses where the curve is still falling

For the boosted trees, which are still improving at every size measured, the floor is not something the fit can see, so it guesses. Fitted to 400 rows, the floor is 0.330, above the 0.289 the trees actually reach. The fit concludes that more data will barely help and predicts 0.333 at 25,600 rows, when the trees in fact get to 0.289. Fitted to 1,600 or 6,400 rows, the floor is 0.219 and 0.214, below the Bayes error. The predictions at 25,600 rows are closer, 0.278 and 0.277 against 0.289, but the parameter that is supposed to say how far the model can go has no meaning: no model can reach an error below the Bayes error.

The underlying problem is that, on a stretch of curve with no visible bend, many combinations of $b$ and $c$ fit almost equally well. A steeper decay towards a higher floor and a shallower decay towards a lower floor trace nearly the same path over the pilot range and diverge only beyond it. The data cannot choose between them, so the fitted floor moves a great deal when the fit range changes.

15-NN sits in between. It is still falling at every size measured, yet its floored fits from 1,600 and 6,400 rows miss by only -1.4% and -0.4%, with floors of 0.288 and 0.295, above the Bayes error. A floor can work on a curve that is still falling; what the fit cannot tell you is whether it has.

So the floor is least reliable where it is most needed. A curve that has visibly bent already tells you that more data will not help much, with or without a fit. A curve still falling fast is the case where the labelling decision matters, and there the third parameter is poorly determined: for the boosted trees it moved from 0.330 to 0.219 when the fit range grew from 400 to 1,600 rows. The first figure below traces the fitted floors; the table after it collects all nine predictions.

![For the boosted trees the floor is 0.330 from 400 rows, above what they reach, and 0.219 from 1,600, below the Bayes error. A floor nobody can reach is not an estimate of anything.](ml104-f1-floors.png){alt="Three lines of fitted floors; the boosted-trees line starts above its dashed actual error and drops below the dotted Bayes-error line."}

![Every two-parameter prediction is too low. The floor helps logistic regression, and 15-NN from 1,600 rows, and misleads about the boosted trees.](ml104-f2-table.png){alt="A nine-row table: the two-parameter predictions are all too low; the three-parameter ones are close for logistic regression and miss in both directions for boosted trees."}

### On the digits, and the ranking that turns over


The digits allow only 1,200 training images after a fixed test set is set aside, so the check there is shorter: fit up to 300 images, predict 1,200. The models are those of Section 1.1: logistic regression, an RBF SVM and 3-NN. The code prints each model's test error at four training sizes.

```python
dig = cu.digits_curve()
for n in (50, 100, 300, 1200):
    print(f"{n:>5,} images  " + "  ".join(
        f"{k} {dig['error'][k][n]:.3f}" for k in dig["error"]))
```

```text
   50 images  logistic 0.230  RBF SVM 0.355  3-NN 0.256
  100 images  logistic 0.133  RBF SVM 0.163  3-NN 0.132
  300 images  logistic 0.067  RBF SVM 0.060  3-NN 0.052
1,200 images  logistic 0.040  RBF SVM 0.022  3-NN 0.020
```

All nine two-parameter fits, three models and three pilot sizes, were optimistic again. From 300 images the predictions were 0.022 against an actual 0.040 for logistic regression (-46%), 0.019 against 0.022 for the SVM, and 0.015 against 0.020 for 3-NN. The three-parameter fit put the floor at zero for 2 of the three models: on curves this short and steep there is no floor to see.

The table also shows the second way a pilot study misleads. Logistic regression has the lowest error at 50 images and the highest at 1,200, double the error of the other two. It is the most constrained of the three models, and constraint is what small data rewards: a model with fewer ways to fit the data has less room to fit noise when there is little data, and less room to fit the signal when there is a lot. A model chosen on the pilot would be the wrong one. The simulated task shows the same turnover at a smaller scale: logistic regression leads at 50 rows, 15-NN at 100, and the boosted trees from 200 on.

The same extrapolation is made at a far larger scale whenever a ladder of small models is used to plan a large one, with the number of parameters in place of the number of training rows. Section 5.2 tests it there.

## A Power Law Fitted on Small Models Cannot See the Floor

<!-- source: ml-30-scaling; hero: ml130-hero.png -->


Section 5.1 extrapolated a learning curve along the number of training rows. The same move made along the number of parameters, from small models to large ones, is more ambitious, and the sentence that licenses it is the one that justifies building large language models at all: *scaling laws predict performance*. Kaplan and colleagues found in 2020 that a language model's loss falls as a power of its parameter count over many orders of magnitude, so that a line drawn through cheap small models says what an expensive large one will achieve before it is trained. Practitioners meet the sentence whenever a pilot ladder of small models is used to choose the size of the model that will actually be trained, or to forecast what more parameters will buy. If the law holds only over a short reach, such forecasts will be optimistic, and the error will appear only after the expensive model has been built.

This section trains a ladder of eight small transformers under one fixed training budget and fits the law to the smallest of them. It checks the predictions for the larger models and examines the pattern of residuals rather than a single hit. It then rules out overfitting as the main cause of the misses, relates them to the number of training characters each model sees per parameter, and shows that a loss floor, the usual repair, cannot be estimated from the small models alone.

### A ladder of eight models under one budget


A *scaling law* in model size is the power law of Section 5.1 with the number of parameters in place of the number of training rows:

$$
L(N) = a\,N^{-b},
$$

where $L$ is the validation loss, $N$ the number of parameters and $a$, $b$ positive constants. As before, it is a straight line on log-log axes with slope $-b$, which is what makes it easy to fit and tempting to extrapolate: a few points fix the line, and the line can be extended.

The ladder here consists of eight *character-level transformers*, language models that read and predict text one character at a time, trained on Shakespeare. They range from 4,288 to 1,789,248 parameters outside the embeddings. The *embedding* parameters are the lookup tables that turn each input character into a vector; the remaining *non-embedding* parameters, in the attention and feed-forward layers, are the count Kaplan's law uses. The models are pre-norm transformers with 4 heads and a context of 64 characters, with widths from 16 to 192 and from 1 to 4 layers.

Every model gets the same recipe: 2,000 steps of AdamW, learning rate 3e-3 with warm-up and cosine decay, weight decay 0.1, batches of 32. The training text is the first 90% of a million-character Shakespeare corpus and validation uses the last 10%. Each model sees 4.1 million characters in all, about four passes over the training text. Training the ladder took 1.9 hours on two CPU threads, so the results are stored and read back. Loss is measured in *nats per character*, the average negative natural logarithm of the probability the model assigns to the next character.

```python
from standarderror.ml import scaling as sc

r = sc.results()     # written by scripts/train_scaling.py
for run in r["runs"]:
    print(f"{run['width']:3d} wide x {run['layers']} layers: "
          f"{run['non_embedding']:7,d} parameters, training loss "
          f"{run['final_train']:.3f}, validation {run['final_val']:.3f}")
```

```text
 16 wide x 1 layers:   4,288 parameters, training loss 2.307, validation 2.333
 24 wide x 2 layers:  15,864 parameters, training loss 2.153, validation 2.192
 32 wide x 2 layers:  27,296 parameters, training loss 1.956, validation 2.033
 48 wide x 2 layers:  59,376 parameters, training loss 1.736, validation 1.888
 64 wide x 3 layers: 153,472 parameters, training loss 1.569, validation 1.744
 96 wide x 3 layers: 340,800 parameters, training loss 1.461, validation 1.641
128 wide x 4 layers: 799,616 parameters, training loss 1.384, validation 1.578
192 wide x 4 layers: 1,789,248 parameters, training loss 1.333, validation 1.542
```

Validation loss falls with every step up the ladder, from 2.333 to 1.542 nats per character. Larger models are better throughout; the question is whether the law fitted to the small ones says by how much.

### Fitting on five models and predicting three


The next block fits the law to the five smallest models and asks it about the three it has not seen.

```python
small = r["runs"][:5]
law = sc.fit([x["non_embedding"] for x in small],
             [x["final_val"] for x in small], floor=False)
print(f"L = {law['a']:.3f} N^-{law['b']:.4f}")
for run in r["runs"][5:]:
    guess = sc.predict(law, run["non_embedding"])
    print(f"{run['non_embedding']:7,d} parameters: predicted "
          f"{guess:.3f}, measured {run['final_val']:.3f}")
```

```text
L = 4.821 N^-0.0846
340,800 parameters: predicted 1.640, measured 1.641
799,616 parameters: predicted 1.526, measured 1.578
1,789,248 parameters: predicted 1.425, measured 1.542
```

The exponent is 0.085. The sixth model, 2.2 times the size of the fifth, comes in +0.04% from the prediction: the law did exactly what it is famous for. The seventh, 5.2 times the fifth, is +3.4% off, and the eighth, 12 times the fifth, is +8.2% off. Every miss is in the same direction: the large models are worse than the line promised. The figure below shows the fit.

![The sixth model lands on the line (+0.0%). The largest, 12 times the fifth, is +8.2% off.](ml130-f0-law.png){alt="Points on log-log axes falling along a dashed straight line for the five smallest models; the next point sits on the line and the two largest sit above it."}

### Judge a law by its residuals, not by one hit


A more cautious analyst would fit on fewer models and check on more, and might be reassured. Fitted to the three smallest models, the law's exponent is 0.070, and its prediction for the largest model, 66 times the third, is off by only -0.1%. Taken alone, that looks like a strong confirmation of the law.

The *residuals*, the relative differences between measured and predicted loss across the whole ladder, show how it got there. For the four models in between, the three-model law was too *pessimistic*, by as much as 5.3%: its slope was too shallow for the middle of the ladder, and the bend at the top happened to cancel the error. The figure below shows the residual profiles for laws fitted to three, five and seven models. Fitted to seven models, the law misses the eighth, only 2.2 times the seventh, by +6.2%.

![Fitted to three models, the law is too pessimistic for the middle of the ladder and lands on the largest by cancellation (-0.1%). Fitted to seven, it misses the eighth by +6.2%.](ml130-f1-profiles.png){alt="Three lines of residuals: the three-model fit dips below zero through the middle of the ladder and returns to zero at the end; the five- and seven-model fits stay near zero and then rise above it for the largest models."}

One correct prediction is not evidence that the law holds; the pattern of residuals is. If the law were right, residuals would scatter randomly about zero at every size. Instead, the three-model fit's residuals form a U, below the line through the middle of the ladder and back at the top. The five- and seven-model fits scatter by a few percent about the line through the models they were fitted to (up to 3.1% for the five) and then rise sharply above it at the top. A systematic curve in the residuals means the functional form is wrong, and no straight line on log-log axes can follow that bend.

The scatter matters too, because it sets the size of a miss that means something. The seventh model's +3.4% is comparable to the 3.1% scatter of the fitted models, so on its own it would not be convincing. Only the eighth's +8.2% clearly exceeds it.

### The misses are not mainly overfitting


The obvious suspect is overfitting. The largest model has more non-embedding parameters, 1,789,248, than the training text has characters to constrain them, about a million, and the gap between training and validation loss does widen up the ladder, from 0.026 for the smallest model to 0.210 for the largest.

The test is to fit the same law to training loss. If the large models were merely overfitting, they would do *better* than the line on the data they trained on and worse only on validation data. In fact the law fitted to the five smallest models' training loss misses in the same direction: the seventh and eighth models' training losses are +5.2% and +11.0% above prediction. These models do worse than the line on both. They are not learning as much per parameter as the small ones did, on any data.

The table below suggests why. The number of training characters seen per non-embedding parameter falls from 955 for the smallest model to 2.3 for the largest, because every model sees the same 4.1 million characters while the parameter count grows. Hoffmann and colleagues found that a *compute-optimal* language model, one that achieves the lowest loss for a fixed amount of training computation, sees on the order of twenty tokens per parameter. Their tokens are subwords and these are characters, so the comparison is only rough. The small models here are trained far past that point; the two largest, at 5.1 and 2.3, have been given the same budget and stopped well short of it. Kaplan's law in $N$ describes models that are not limited by data or training steps. The ladder's largest rungs are plausibly limited by both, though the budget was never varied to show it.

![The first five rows fix the law; the last three test it. Tokens per parameter is the number of training characters seen per non-embedding parameter.](ml130-f2-table.png){alt="An eight-row table: tokens per parameter fall from about 950 to about 2; the law's error is small for the first six models and grows for the last two."}

### The floor the small models cannot see


The standard repair, which Section 5.1 above tried on learning curves, is to add a floor:

$$
L(N) = a\,N^{-b} + c,
$$

where $c$ is the loss no model size can get below with this data and this budget. On log-log axes this curve is close to a straight line while $aN^{-b}$ is much larger than $c$, and bends towards the horizontal as $aN^{-b}$ approaches it. The block fits it to the five small models and to all eight.

```python
floor = sc.fit([x["non_embedding"] for x in small],
               [x["final_val"] for x in small])        # a N^-b + c
everyone = sc.fit([x["non_embedding"] for x in r["runs"]],
                  [x["final_val"] for x in r["runs"]])
print(f"floor fitted to the 5 smallest: c = {floor['c']:.3f}")
print(f"floor fitted to all {len(r['runs'])}:     c = {everyone['c']:.3f}")
```

```text
floor fitted to the 5 smallest: c = 0.000
floor fitted to all 8:     c = 0.855
```

From the five small models the fitted floor is 0.000, the lowest value the fit allows. In their range the curve is still a straight line on log-log axes, so the data contain no curvature from which to estimate a floor, and the best fit puts it as low as it can go. With all eight models the floor is 0.86, and the curve bends towards it. The information that the curve bends lives only in the models that have already bent, which are exactly the models the law was supposed to make unnecessary.

That is Section 5.1's finding met again at a larger scale. There, a learning curve fitted early on a pilot study was optimistic in all nine fits, and adding a floor fixed only the curves that had already bent. Here the law fitted to five small models was optimistic about every model past the sixth, and the floor could not be seen from them. The arithmetic is the same in both cases: a power law extrapolated past the range that determined it assumes nothing new happens there, and within a fixed budget of data and steps, something eventually does.

Chapter 10 and Section 15.3 meet the same pattern in other forms: what a network's fit shows depends on conditions the fit itself does not reveal, such as whether labels are noisy, whether anything keeps pushing the network after the fit, whether an augmentation's assumption holds and where the inputs lie. Here the hidden condition is whether the budget still suffices at the next size up.

## More Data Cures the Noise, Not the Design

<!-- source: ml-68-more-data; hero: ml168-hero.png -->


Sections 5.1 and 5.2 asked how far more data, or a larger model, goes towards a better score, and found that a forecast made early promises more than it delivers. This section asks a different question about the same resource: which problems does more data remove? Many of the problems in this book are demonstrated on small data: 200 rows, 300 tumours, 1,797 digits. A practitioner with millions of rows is entitled to wonder which of them still apply, and the common reassurance is that most of them fade: *with enough data, these problems go away*. The sentence matters because it decides where attention goes on a large project. If it is true, a big data set lets one stop worrying about leaks, error bars and validation design; if it is false for some problems, those problems survive into exactly the projects where nobody checks for them. Some problems do fade. The question is which, and the answer follows a simple rule.

This section takes seven problems from across the book, rebuilds each in a compact version that can be run at any size, and measures each at 200, 800 and 3,200 rows. It sorts them by whether a sixteenfold increase in data made them smaller, explains the mechanism behind each group, and ends with a test that tells the groups apart before any data are collected.

### How the problems were measured


Each problem was rebuilt in a compact version of its original setting and run at 200, 800 and 3,200 rows, on ten simulated data sets per size. Each is measured as the size of the error it causes, in its own unit: points of accuracy, points of AUC, points of a default rate, or a count of components. The question for each is the same: did sixteen times the data make it smaller?

The code below runs two of the seven at the smallest and largest sizes, on five data sets each. The first is a leak from selecting features on all rows; the second is the bias of a model trained only on approved loan applicants, both described in detail below.

```python
from standarderror.ml import course

for n in (200, 3200):
    leak = course._selection_leak(n, reps=5)        # a variance problem
    bias = course._selection_bias(n, reps=5)        # a design problem
    print(f"{n:5d} rows: selection leak {100 * leak:5.1f} points, "
          f"refused-applicant bias {100 * bias:6.1f} points")
```

```text
  200 rows: selection leak  25.2 points, refused-applicant bias  -42.4 points
 3200 rows: selection leak   7.1 points, refused-applicant bias  -45.5 points
```

The leak falls from 25.2 points to 7.1 as the rows grow sixteenfold. The bias does not fall at all: it is -42.4 points at 200 rows and -45.5 at 3,200. The comments in the code anticipate the explanation: one is a variance problem, the other a design problem. The rest of the section uses all ten data sets per size, so its figures differ slightly from this five-data-set run.

### Four problems of noise, which shrink


#### A test score's standard error

The error bar of Section 1.1 is the textbook case. A test accuracy $\hat p$ on $n$ independent rows has standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$. At an accuracy of 0.8 it was 2.83 points at 200 rows, 1.41 at 800 and 0.71 at 3,200, halving with each fourfold increase in rows, as the $1/\sqrt{n}$ predicts.

#### The optimism of a tuned score

Section 4.1 showed that the best cross-validated score in a search is optimistic, a *winner's curse*: each setting's score is an estimate with noise, and the setting that wins is disproportionately the one whose noise was favourable. Tuning a nearest-neighbour classifier over 32 settings, the optimism was 3.7 points at 200 rows, and -0.1 and +0.1 at 800 and 3,200. With more rows each setting's cross-validated score is less noisy, the best of them has less luck to exploit, and the search's reported score converges on what it delivers.

#### Selecting features on all rows

Section 3.1's leak selects 20 of 1,000 noise features using every row, then cross-validates a model on them. It turned coin flips into 75% accuracy at 200 rows, 63.5% at 800 and 57.1% at 3,200. With more rows the noise features' chance correlations with the label are smaller, so the selection finds less to exploit. But at 3,200 rows it was still 7.1 points of pure fiction.

#### A target encoding's leak into training

*Target encoding* replaces each level of a categorical feature with the mean of the label over the training rows at that level. Section 3.2 showed that, fitted on the training rows, it leaks into training: each row's own label is part of its encoded value. A 200-level noise category encoded this way ranked its own training labels at 43 points of AUC above chance with one row per level on average, 28 with four and 14 with sixteen. The more rows share a level, the less any one row's label moves the level's mean.

#### What the four have in common

All four are the same kind of problem: an estimate fitted to a sample's noise, which shrinks as the sample grows. More data is the cure, at a rate that differs by problem: one over the square root of $n$ for the standard error, faster here for the tuning optimism, roughly in proportion to rows per level for the encoding. With a fixed number of features, settings or categories, the cure eventually arrives. The figure below shows all seven problems as the rows grow.

### Two problems of design, which stay


#### Outcomes observed only for the approved

Section 15.2 studies *selective labels* in full: a lender observes whether a loan defaults only for applicants who were approved. In the compact version, a loan officer approves on information the model lacks as well as on the model's features. A model trained on the approved applicants underestimated the refused applicants' default rate by 47.7 points at 200 rows, 46.0 at 800 and 45.4 at 3,200.

More approved applicants teach the model more precisely about approved applicants. They say nothing about the refused, because the refused are never in the data, and the officer's extra information makes the two groups differ in ways the features do not show. Meng's analysis of big-data surveys makes the same point with a memorable result: a large non-random sample can carry no more information than a small random one, because its bias does not shrink with its size.

#### Rows from the same people

Section 2.4 showed that rows from the same people are not new data. In *row-level cross-validation*, rows are assigned to folds at random, so each person's rows appear in both training and validation folds and the model is scored partly on people it has already seen. With every row from the same 20 people, it overstated accuracy on new people by 13.2 points at 200 rows, 14.8 at 800 and 15.6 at 3,200. More rows from the same people made the model better at recognising those people, which row-level folds reward and new people do not, so the gap grew slightly. The fix is more people, not more rows, and folds that keep each person's rows together.

### One problem that grows


The *Bayesian information criterion* (BIC) chooses a number of components for a Gaussian mixture by trading the fit's likelihood against a penalty on the number of parameters. For a single uniform square, which contains no clusters at all, it chose 4 components at 200 rows, 6 at 800 and 8 at 3,200. Section 17.2 finds the same rising count up to 20,000 points, though not with the same numbers, on a different draw and with fewer starts of the fit here.

The reason is that the BIC is a *consistent* criterion: as the data grow, it selects the model that best approximates the data among those it is offered. A square is not a mixture of Gaussians, so no number of components is right, and as the data grow the evidence that one more component would approximate it better grows too. A consistent criterion becomes more confident about a misspecified model's answer. The answer is about the model's shape, not about clusters in the data. The table below collects all seven problems.

### The rule: variance shrinks, bias does not


Sorted, the seven fall into the old distinction between variance and bias. The four that shrank were variance: errors that come from fitting a sample's accidents, which average away. The two that stayed were bias in the data's design, who is in the sample and how the rows relate to each other, which no amount of the same data can reveal. The one that grew was bias in the model's assumptions, which more data make more confidently.

For a practitioner with a large data set, the book's problems therefore split in two. Error bars, tuning optimism and leaks from fitting on noise can mostly be retired, though not entirely: a leak that is 7 points at 3,200 rows is still a leak. Selection, grouping, drift, misspecified assumptions and the gap between a metric and its use keep their full force, and often hide better, because a large sample produces narrow error bars around the wrong number.

A practical test separates the two kinds before any data are collected: ask whether the problem would disappear if the sample grew to the whole population it was drawn from. A standard error, a tuned score's luck and a leak from fitting on noise would. A sample of approved applicants, rows from twenty people and a misspecified model would not: every approved applicant there is still contains no refused ones, and every row from those twenty people still describes only them.

Taken with Sections 5.1 and 5.2, this gives the chapter's answer to how far more data goes. It goes as far as variance: it narrows error bars, starves a search or a leak of luck, and brings a model closer to its own floor. It does not show in advance where that floor lies, and it does not change who is in the sample, how the rows relate to each other or what the model assumes.

## In practice

### Forecasting what more data will buy

- **Do not trust a two-parameter power-law forecast from a pilot study.** In every fit in Section 5.1, simulated and real, it promised a lower error than the model reached. Expect it to be optimistic, and do not report it as an estimate.
- **Plot the local log-log slope.** Compute the slope between neighbouring training sizes. If it is shrinking, there is a floor ahead and a straight-line fit will overshoot it.
- **Refit with the largest pilot size left out.** If the forecast at your target size moves by more than the improvement you are hoping for, the curve cannot tell you what the extra data will buy.
- **Distrust a fitted floor on a curve that is still falling.** It can land above what the model will reach or below the Bayes error, and it moves with the fit range. It is reliable mainly where the curve has already bent, and there you hardly need it. The same holds for a floor fitted to a ladder of small models that still lie on a straight line.
- **Choose the model at the size you will train on.** The best model at pilot size need not be the best at full size. Check the ranking at the largest size you can afford to test.

### Validating a scaling law

- **Hold back more than one size.** A single correct prediction can come from cancelling errors. Fit on some sizes, check on several larger ones, and plot the residuals across the whole ladder.
- **Read the residuals, not the headline.** If they curve rather than scatter about zero, the functional form is wrong, and extrapolation will drift further the further it reaches.
- **Compare misses with the fitted models' scatter.** A miss no bigger than the scatter about the fitted points is not yet evidence of a bend; one well beyond it is.
- **Fit the law to training loss as well.** If training loss misses in the same direction, the cause is not mainly overfitting but something that limits learning, plausibly data or training steps.
- **Track data seen per parameter.** When it falls far below the level at which the small models of the ladder were trained, a law in parameter count alone may be misspecified; scale data and steps with the model, or expect a floor the small models cannot reveal.

### Deciding what a large data set has fixed

- **Classify each problem as noise or design** before deciding whether a large data set has dealt with it, using the whole-population test of Section 5.3.
- **Retire noise problems only relative to complexity.** A selection leak, an encoding leak or tuning optimism shrinks with rows only if the number of features, categories and settings does not grow with them.
- **Do not let a large sample excuse a biased one.** For selective labels, ask who never enters the data and how their outcomes could be measured, for example by approving a random sample.
- **Count independent units, not rows.** Where rows come from people, sessions or sites, validate with folds that keep each unit together, and grow the number of units.
- **Expect criteria such as the BIC to grow more confident, not more correct,** when the model family is wrong.

## Limits of the evidence

### Learning curves (Section 5.1)

- **These are small models on small data.** The scaling laws used to plan large neural-network runs are fitted over many orders of magnitude, far into the straight part of the curve, and have predicted well there. The failure here is specific to fitting early, which is exactly where a pilot study fits.
- **Least squares on the error scale weights the early points.** The large errors at small $n$ dominate an unweighted fit. Fitting on log error, or weighting by each point's variance, changes the numbers; it does not remove the curvature, so it does not change the direction of the bias.
- **The task was chosen to have a known floor.** Real problems have one too, but nobody computes it, which is the point: the only check on a fitted floor is the Bayes error, and in practice it is unavailable.
- **Few training sets at the largest sizes.** The 25,600-row errors average three training sets, and the digits' 1,200-image errors come from one. Their noise is small next to the gaps quoted, but not zero.

### Scaling laws (Section 5.2)

- **One budget for every model.** The laws used to plan large models are fitted to models each trained with enough data and compute for their size, or along a compute-optimal frontier. This ladder holds the budget fixed on purpose, which is the setting in which a law in $N$ alone is misspecified. A ladder with data growing alongside the models would be expected to bend later, or not at all in this range; that was not run.
- **Small models, one seed each.** The models run from four thousand to two million non-embedding parameters, each trained once, and the run-to-run spread was not measured. The sixth model's agreement with the line, to a fraction of a percent, may be partly luck, and so may the seventh's miss, which is about the size of the scatter of the fitted models. The eighth's miss is more than twice that scatter, which seed noise of that size would be unlikely to produce.
- **One learning rate.** Every model used 3e-3. Larger models often want smaller rates, and part of the shortfall at the top of the ladder may be a rate that suits the small models better.
- **Character-level Shakespeare.** A million characters of one author is a narrow distribution with a low ceiling on what any model can learn from it. Kaplan's laws were measured on billions of tokens of web text, where the floor is far away.

### What more data fixes (Section 5.3)

- **Compact versions.** Each problem was rebuilt in a small simulation. The rates at which the real versions shrink depend on the number of features, categories and settings, which grow with data in real projects.
- **Fixed complexity.** With 1,000 noise features held fixed, the selection leak shrank. If the number of features grows with the data, as it often does, the leak need not shrink at all.
- **Ten data sets per size.** Enough to separate shrinking from staying; not enough to pin the third digit of any value.
- **Seven problems of many.** Problems such as drift (Section 20.1) and feedback loops (Section 13.3) depend on time, not sample size, and were not on the list.

## Key points

1. A power law $a\,n^{-b}$ fitted to a pilot study predicted too low an error at the target size in all 18 fits of Section 5.1, simulated and real. On a task with a known Bayes error of 0.268, 4 of 9 such predictions were below it: errors no classifier can reach.
2. Learning curves flatten towards a floor; the local log-log slope shrinks overall, if not at every step, so a straight line fitted early overshoots.
3. The floored form $a\,n^{-b} + c$ worked for logistic regression and, from 1,600 rows, for 15-NN, and failed for the boosted trees, still falling fastest: their fitted floor went from 0.330 (above what they reach) to 0.219 (below Bayes) with the fit range.
4. The best model at pilot size need not be the best at full size. On the digits, logistic regression went from best at 50 images to worst at 1,200.
5. A scaling law fitted to five small transformers predicted the next size to +0.04%, but was +3.4% off for a model 5.2 times the largest fitted and +8.2% for one 12 times, always optimistic. A law fitted to three models hit the largest by cancelling two errors, and one fitted to seven missed it by +6.2%: judge a law by its residuals, not by one hit.
6. Training loss missed the same way, so the cause was not mainly overfitting. With a fixed budget, characters per parameter fell from 955 to 2.3, consistent with the largest models being limited by data and steps rather than size; the budget itself was not varied.
7. A floor fitted to the small models was 0.000; fitted to all eight, 0.86. On learning curves and on scaling ladders alike, the information that a curve bends lives only in the points that have already bent.
8. Four problems shrank with sixteen times the data: the standard error (2.8 to 0.7 points), tuning optimism (3.7 to 0.1), the selection leak (24.7 to 7.1) and the encoding leak (43 to 14).
9. Two did not: selective labels stayed 45 points wrong and row-level cross-validation with 20 people stayed 16 points optimistic. The BIC's count of Gaussians for one uniform square grew from 4 to 8.
10. Noise averages away; the design of the data and the assumptions of the model do not. With a large data set, spend your attention on who is in the data, how rows depend on each other and what the model assumes, not on error bars.

## Exercises

1. If you have a learning curve from a pilot study, refit it twice: once on all the sizes you have, once leaving out the largest. If the prediction at your target size moves by more than the improvement you are hoping for, the curve is not telling you what the extra data will buy. Then plot the local slope between neighbouring sizes: if it is still steepening or constant, you are in the part of the curve a power law describes; if it is shrinking, there is a floor ahead, and a two-parameter fit will overshoot it.
2. Before committing to a model, check the ranking at the largest size you can afford to test, not at the pilot size. Which of your candidate models is the most constrained, and would you expect it to lead or trail as the data grow?
3. Explain why, on a stretch of learning curve with no visible bend, the floor $c$ in $a\,n^{-b} + c$ is poorly determined by the data. Then explain why fitting $aN^{-b} + c$ to models that all lie on a straight line on log-log axes cannot recover the floor $c$, and what that implies for planning a model larger than any in the pilot ladder.
4. Take three or four sizes of a model you can train cheaply, each with the same data and the same number of steps, and fit $L = aN^{-b}$ to their validation loss. Before training the next size up, write down the prediction. Then train it, and a size beyond.
5. If the second prediction in Exercise 4 misses by more than the first, compute the training tokens per parameter for each model, and identify where the curve leaves the line. The point where it does is usually where that number stops being large.
6. For a model trained on a large data set, list the problems from this book you would have worried about with a small one. Mark each as noise (an estimate fitted to the sample's accidents) or design (who is in the sample, how rows relate, what the model assumes).
7. Retire the noise problems if your sample is large relative to your features and settings. For each design problem, name the data you would need to measure it: more people rather than more rows, outcomes for the refused, a period after the drift.
8. Apply the whole-population test to a target encoding of a category whose number of levels grows with the data. Explain whether its leak behaves like a noise problem.
