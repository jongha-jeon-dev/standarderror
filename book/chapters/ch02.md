# Cross-Validation Estimates the Error of a Model You Did Not Train

Chapter 1 ended where most advice about test-set noise ends: one split is noisy, so cross-validate. *K-fold cross-validation* (CV) divides the data into $K$ folds, fits the model $K$ times, each time holding one fold out and scoring the model on it, and averages the $K$ scores, so every row is used for testing exactly once. The result is described, in textbooks, library documentation and nearly every report that quotes it, with one sentence: *cross-validation estimates how well your model will do on new data.* A practitioner meets this sentence whenever a CV score is reported as the expected performance of the model about to be deployed. If it is false, that report describes a different model from the one being shipped, and its error bar describes neither.

The chapter first separates the two quantities the sentence could mean. It then measures which one CV estimates, on simulated least squares where the answer is known exactly, and checks the coverage of the usual CV interval. It repeats the question on the UCI digits, where the relationship turns out to run backwards. It closes by showing what does answer the question about a fitted model, a held-out set, and what that costs.

## Two quantities called "the error of your model"

The phrase "how well your model will do" can mean two different numbers.

The first is the error of the model you actually fitted, on new data from the same population. Write it $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$, because it depends on the particular training inputs $X$ and labels $y$ you were given. It is a *conditional* error: conditional on this training set.

The second is the average of that error over all the training sets of the same size you might have been given. Write it $\mathrm{Err}$, the *expected* error of the training procedure. A model fitted to an unlucky sample has $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ above $\mathrm{Err}$; one fitted to a lucky sample, below.

The sentence under test is about $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$: it speaks of *your* model. The question is which of the two CV estimates.

### A setting where the true error is exact

On real data this is hard to check, because $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ is never observed; it would take infinitely many new rows to measure. So the chapter starts where it can be computed exactly. Each dataset has 100 rows of 20 standard-normal features, a response that is linear in them with every coefficient 0.5, and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 1. A least-squares fit gives a coefficient vector $b$ in place of the true $\beta$.

On a fresh row $x$ with response $y = x^\top\beta + \varepsilon$, the prediction error is $y - x^\top b = \varepsilon - x^\top(b - \beta)$. Because the noise has mean zero and is independent of $x$, and the features are independent with mean zero and unit variance, the expected squared error is

$$
\mathrm{Err}_{XY} = 1 + \lVert b - \beta \rVert^2 ,
$$

the noise variance plus the squared distance of the fitted coefficients from the truth. No estimation is involved: every fitted model's true error is known. The code simulates 2,000 such datasets, computes the 10-fold CV estimate and the exact error for each, and reports their means, spreads and correlation.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import evaluation as ev

# 2,000 datasets: 100 rows, 20 features, least squares.
lin = ev.linear_cv_study(n=100, p=20, reps=2000)
cv, err_xy = lin["cv"], lin["err_xy"]
print(f"mean CV estimate      {cv.mean():.3f}   SD {cv.std():.3f}")
print(f"mean true error       {err_xy.mean():.3f}   SD {err_xy.std():.3f}")
print(f"correlation           {np.corrcoef(cv, err_xy)[0, 1]:+.3f}")
```

```text
mean CV estimate      1.294   SD 0.211
mean true error       1.255   SD 0.092
correlation           -0.012
```

The correlation between the CV estimate and the error of the model it accompanies is -0.012. Across 2,000 datasets, knowing the CV estimate tells you nothing about whether this particular fit is better or worse than usual. The figure below plots each dataset's CV estimate against its fitted model's true error.

## What cross-validation tracks instead

The figure shows the mechanism. The true errors vary from dataset to dataset with a standard deviation of 0.092; the CV estimates vary with a standard deviation of 0.211, 2.3 times as much. The CV estimate has noise of its own: which rows landed in which fold, and how hard those held-out rows happened to be. That noise swamps the comparatively small variation in $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ that the estimate would need to detect.

What survives the averaging over folds is the *level*. The CV estimates centre on 1.294, against an average true error of 1.255. They sit slightly high for a reason that is standard: each fold's model is trained on 90 rows rather than 100, and a least-squares fit on fewer rows has a larger expected error. CV therefore estimates the expected error of a slightly smaller training set, which is a small, predictable pessimistic bias.

So CV is an estimate of $\mathrm{Err}$, the average over training sets, and a reasonable one: its *root mean squared error* (RMSE, the square root of the average squared gap between estimate and target) is 0.215 against $\mathrm{Err}$ and 0.235 against $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$. That is useful. $\mathrm{Err}$ is the right quantity for choosing between *procedures*, since a procedure, not a fitted model, is what you would rerun on new data. But it is not what the sentence claims.

### The usual interval covers neither target

A CV score is often reported with an interval: the mean of the per-row losses plus or minus a normal quantile times their standard error. The next block checks how often the nominal 90% interval contains each target.

```python
print(f"90% CV interval covers the fitted model's error "
      f"{ev.coverage(cv, lin['cv_se'], err_xy):.1%}")
print(f"90% CV interval covers the average error        "
      f"{ev.coverage(cv, lin['cv_se'], lin['err']):.1%}")
```

```text
90% CV interval covers the fitted model's error 79.6%
90% CV interval covers the average error        83.3%
```

The interval's *coverage*, the fraction of datasets in which it contains the target, is 79.6% for $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ and 83.3% for $\mathrm{Err}$, both short of 90%. There are several reasons, and they compound.

First, the standard error treats the 100 per-row losses as independent, and they are not. Every fold's model was trained on most of the other folds' rows, so the models are similar and the losses on different folds are correlated. Correlated observations carry less information than the same number of independent ones, so the naive standard error is too small. That is one reason the interval is too narrow for $\mathrm{Err}$; for $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ it is also pointed at the wrong target.

Second, the centre is off: it sits 0.039 above $\mathrm{Err}$, for the training-size reason above.

Third, squared losses are skewed, with a long right tail, and a normal interval for the mean of skewed values undercovers at this sample size. Even the held-out intervals in the next sections, whose losses are independent given the fitted model, cover only 87% and 84%.

Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani prove the linear-model version of the correlation result and propose *nested cross-validation*, which runs an inner CV inside each outer fold to estimate the extra variance, as a way to get the interval's width right. The point of this chapter comes before that: it is about what the interval's centre estimates.

## On real digits the relationship runs backwards

A simulation is only as good as its assumptions, so the same question is asked of the UCI digits. The data are split once into a fixed test set of 797 images and a training pool. From the pool, 200 training sets of 300 images are drawn. For each, logistic regression is cross-validated, then fitted to all 300 images, and that fit is scored on the fixed test set. The test set is large enough to stand in for each fit's true accuracy, and disjoint from every training draw.

```python
dig = ev.digits_cv_study(train=300, test=797, reps=200)
print(f"CV accuracy    mean {dig['cv'].mean():.4f}  SD {dig['cv'].std():.4f}")
print(f"test accuracy  mean {dig['true'].mean():.4f}  SD {dig['true'].std():.4f}")
print(f"correlation    {dig['corr']:+.3f}")
```

```text
CV accuracy    mean 0.9396  SD 0.0128
test accuracy  mean 0.9376  SD 0.0085
correlation    -0.212
```

The correlation is -0.21, and it is negative: training sets that cross-validate well produce models that do slightly worse on new digits. As in the simulation, the CV scores (standard deviation 0.0128) vary more than the test accuracies they accompany (0.0085).

One explanation, not tested here, is the same averaging seen from the other side. A training set that happens to contain more awkward, atypical digits would score poorly in CV, because its held-out folds are full of them, and would also produce a model that has seen more awkward digits and handles the test set's better. If so, CV rewards the easy sample, not the good model. Measuring each draw's difficulty would be the way to check.

The design detail that matters is the fixed, disjoint test set. Had each fit been scored on all the digits it had not trained on, the test set would be the complement of the training set: a draw that took the hard digits would leave an easier test set behind, and the two effects would be confounded. With the test set fixed in advance and disjoint from every draw, that route is closed, and the correlation is still -0.21. The figure below shows the draws.

![Correlation -0.21. The training sets that cross-validate best produce models that do slightly worse on new digits.](ml102-f1-digits.png){alt="A cloud of dots with a gently falling fitted line."}

## What answers the question about a fitted model

If the question really is how well *this* model will do, the instrument is a held-out set the model never saw, scored once. In the simulation, scoring each fit on 100 fresh rows gives a correlation of +0.48 with its true error, and the naive interval covers 87% of the time.

That comparison is unfair to CV: it spends 100 extra rows. On the same budget, fitting on 80 of the 100 rows and scoring on the other 20, the code reports the held-out estimate's correlation with its own model's error, its spread, and that model's average true error.

```python
ho, ho_err = lin["split_holdout"], lin["split_err_xy"]
print(f"fit on 80, score on 20: correlation "
      f"{np.corrcoef(ho, ho_err)[0, 1]:+.3f}   SD {ho.std():.3f}   "
      f"mean true error {ho_err.mean():.3f}")
```

```text
fit on 80, score on 20: correlation +0.266   SD 0.443   mean true error 1.341
```

The held-out estimate still tracks its own model, at +0.27, and pays twice for doing so. The estimate is noisy, with a standard deviation of 0.443, because it averages only 20 losses. And the model being estimated was fitted on less data, so its average true error is 1.341 instead of 1.255. Holding out data buys an estimate of the right target at the price of a worse model and a noisier number.

That is the real choice. Cross-validation gives a stable estimate of how a *procedure* performs on data like yours, which is what you want when choosing between procedures. A held-out set gives a noisy estimate of how one *fitted model* performs, which is what you want when deciding whether to ship it. Most reports use the first and describe it as the second. The table below summarises the comparison.

![CV is closer to the average error than to the fitted model's error. A held-out set tracks the fitted model, noisily, and its interval covers closer to the nominal rate.](ml102-f2-table.png){alt="A four-row table comparing CV and held-out estimates by correlation with their target, error, and interval coverage."}

Whatever CV estimates, it can only estimate it honestly if nothing from the held-out fold reaches the fitted model. Chapter 3 measures what happens when preprocessing breaks that rule.

## In practice

- **Name the target.** When you report a CV score, describe it as the average performance of the training procedure on samples of this size, not as the performance of the deployed model.
- **Use CV to choose procedures.** Comparing algorithms, feature sets or hyperparameter settings is a question about $\mathrm{Err}$, and CV is the right tool; the pairing from Chapter 1 applies, since both procedures are scored on the same rows.
- **Hold data out to judge a model you will ship.** If the deployment decision depends on the specific fitted model being good, score it once on data it never saw, and accept that the estimate is noisy and the model slightly weaker.
- **Distrust the naive CV interval.** Its per-row standard error ignores the correlation between folds, and its nominal 90% covered neither target at 90% here. If the width matters, look at nested CV.
- **Do not read meaning into a good CV score for one particular sample.** Here, a better-than-usual CV score said nothing about the fitted model in simulation, and on the digits it went with slightly worse models.

## Limits of the evidence

- **The zero correlation is for this model and this size.** Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani show that it holds for linear models broadly. With very many rows per feature, the fitted model's error varies little from one training set to the next, so $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ and $\mathrm{Err}$ move closer together and the distinction matters less. Other model classes were not measured here. At 100 rows and 20 features it matters a lot.
- **The digits test set is a proxy for the truth.** It is a sample of 797 images, so each model's score on it is its true accuracy plus an error of order 0.008, shared in part between models because they face the same images, but not identical. The sign of the correlation survived the switch to a fixed test set; its size should be read loosely.
- **Squared error is skewed.** Part of the interval undercoverage, for every estimator in the table, comes from using a normal interval for a mean of skewed losses. The comparison between estimators is fair; the absolute coverages are a little worse than a better interval would give.

## Key points

1. "The error of your model" can mean the error of the model you fitted ($\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$) or the average error of models fitted to data like yours ($\mathrm{Err}$). They are different numbers.
2. On simulated least squares, the 10-fold CV estimate's correlation with $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ is -0.01. CV estimates $\mathrm{Err}$.
3. CV's naive 90% interval covers $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ 80% of the time and $\mathrm{Err}$ 83%: partly because the per-row losses are not independent, partly because they are skewed and CV's centre sits above $\mathrm{Err}$.
4. On real digits the correlation is -0.21: training samples that cross-validated well generalised slightly worse.
5. To evaluate a model you will ship, hold data out. To compare procedures, cross-validate. Say which one you did.
