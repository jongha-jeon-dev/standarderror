# What Cross-Validation Estimates

Cross-validation is the default way to score a model when data are scarce, and its output is quoted everywhere: in model-selection reports, in papers, in the documentation of the libraries that compute it. Chapter 1 left two questions open: how much a score depends on the particular training data, and how to compare models when there are too few rows to set a large test set aside. Cross-validation is the usual answer to both. This chapter asks what a cross-validated score is actually an estimate of, how far apart two such scores must be before the difference means something, and what the folds assume about the rows they are cut from.

Each section tests one sentence:

- Section 2.1: *cross-validation estimates how well your model will do on new data.*
- Section 2.2: *a paired t-test on the cross-validation fold scores tells you whether one model is better than another.*
- Section 2.3: *never shuffle a time series for cross-validation; always split it forward in time.*
- Section 2.4: *more rows means more data, and cross-validation over the rows measures how the model will do.*

Section 2.1 draws the distinction the rest of the chapter depends on. "How well your model will do" can mean the error of the model actually fitted, or the average error of the training procedure over all the data sets that might have been drawn. On simulated least squares, where both are known exactly, and on the UCI digits, cross-validation turns out to estimate the second and to be almost blind to the first. That makes it the right tool for comparing procedures and the wrong one for judging the model about to be shipped.

Section 2.2 follows the comparison of procedures to its usual end, a significance test. Fold scores share training rows, and repeated folds share the whole data set, so a paired t-test on them treats one data set seen many times as many independent pieces of evidence. On two algorithms that are equal by construction, the section counts how often five tests claim a difference, and identifies three that hold their stated error rate.

Sections 2.3 and 2.4 turn from the scores to the rows. Ordinary cross-validation shuffles rows into folds as if any row could stand in for any other. Section 2.3 shows that for a time series this is safe exactly when the model's errors, not its rows, are uncorrelated, and that forward splitting, the usual cure, has a bias of its own. Section 2.4 shows what happens when many rows come from the same people: row-level folds reward a model for recognising people it has already met, and extra rows from the same people add far less information than their count suggests.

The sections build in a line. The first fixes what a cross-validated score estimates; the second, how to tell whether two such estimates really differ; the last two, when the folds themselves fail to imitate the question the deployment will ask. The rule they arrive at is the same throughout: an estimate is only as good as the match between how it was computed and the question it is quoted to answer. Hold data out to judge a fitted model, and cut the folds by whatever the new data will be new in.

## Cross-Validation Estimates the Error of a Model You Did Not Train

<!-- source: ml-2-cv-target; hero: ml102-hero.png -->


Chapter 1 ended where most advice about test-set noise ends: one split is noisy, so cross-validate. *K-fold cross-validation* (CV) divides the data into $K$ folds, fits the model $K$ times, each time holding one fold out and scoring the model on it, and averages the $K$ scores, so every row is used for testing exactly once. The result is described, in textbooks, library documentation and nearly every report that quotes it, with one sentence: *cross-validation estimates how well your model will do on new data.* A practitioner meets this sentence whenever a CV score is reported as the expected performance of the model about to be deployed. If it is false, that report describes a different model from the one being shipped, and its error bar describes neither.

This section first separates the two quantities the sentence could mean. It then measures which one CV estimates, on simulated least squares where the answer is known exactly, and checks the coverage of the usual CV interval. It repeats the question on the UCI digits, where the relationship turns out to run backwards. It closes by showing what does answer the question about a fitted model, a held-out set, and what that costs.

### Two quantities called "the error of your model"


The phrase "how well your model will do" can mean two different numbers.

The first is the error of the model you actually fitted, on new data from the same population. Write it $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$, because it depends on the particular training inputs $X$ and labels $y$ you were given. It is a *conditional* error: conditional on this training set.

The second is the average of that error over all the training sets of the same size you might have been given. Write it $\mathrm{Err}$, the *expected* error of the training procedure. A model fitted to an unlucky sample has $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ above $\mathrm{Err}$; one fitted to a lucky sample, below.

The sentence under test is about $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$: it speaks of *your* model. The question is which of the two CV estimates.

#### A setting where the true error is exact

On real data this is hard to check, because $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ is never observed; it would take infinitely many new rows to measure. So the section starts where it can be computed exactly. Each dataset has 100 rows of 20 standard-normal features, a response that is linear in them with every coefficient 0.5, and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 1. A least-squares fit gives a coefficient vector $b$ in place of the true $\beta$.

On a fresh row $x$ with response $y = x^\top\beta + \varepsilon$, the prediction error is $y - x^\top b = \varepsilon - x^\top(b - \beta)$. Because the noise has mean zero and is independent of $x$, and the features are independent with mean zero and unit variance, the expected squared error is

$$
\mathrm{Err}_{XY} = 1 + \lVert b - \beta \rVert^2 ,
$$

the noise variance plus the squared distance of the fitted coefficients from the truth. No estimation is involved: every fitted model's true error is known. The code simulates 2,000 such datasets, computes the 10-fold CV estimate and the exact error for each, and reports their means, spreads and correlation.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import evaluation as ev

# 2,000 datasets: 100 rows, 20 features, least squares.
lin = ev.linear_cv_study(n=100, p=20, reps=2000)
cv, err_xy = lin["cv"], lin["err_xy"]
print(f"mean CV estimate      {cv.mean():.3f}   SD {cv.std():.3f}")
print(f"mean true error       {err_xy.mean():.3f}   SD {err_xy.std():.3f}")
print(f"correlation           {np.corrcoef(cv, err_xy)[0, 1]:+.3f}")
```

```text
mean CV estimate      1.294   SD 0.211
mean true error       1.255   SD 0.092
correlation           -0.012
```

The correlation between the CV estimate and the error of the model it accompanies is -0.012. Across 2,000 datasets, knowing the CV estimate tells you nothing about whether this particular fit is better or worse than usual. The figure below plots each dataset's CV estimate against its fitted model's true error.

![Correlation -0.01. The CV estimates spread 0.21 wide around a truth that varies by only 0.09: they track the horizontal line, the average, not each dot's own height.](ml102-f0-linear.png){alt="A wide horizontal cloud of dots with no slope, far from the dashed diagonal, centred on a horizontal line."}

### What cross-validation tracks instead


The figure shows the mechanism. The true errors vary from dataset to dataset with a standard deviation of 0.092; the CV estimates vary with a standard deviation of 0.211, 2.3 times as much. The CV estimate has noise of its own: which rows landed in which fold, and how hard those held-out rows happened to be. That noise swamps the comparatively small variation in $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ that the estimate would need to detect.

What survives the averaging over folds is the *level*. The CV estimates centre on 1.294, against an average true error of 1.255. They sit slightly high for a reason that is standard: each fold's model is trained on 90 rows rather than 100, and a least-squares fit on fewer rows has a larger expected error. CV therefore estimates the expected error of a slightly smaller training set, which is a small, predictable pessimistic bias.

So CV is an estimate of $\mathrm{Err}$, the average over training sets, and a reasonable one: its *root mean squared error* (RMSE, the square root of the average squared gap between estimate and target) is 0.215 against $\mathrm{Err}$ and 0.235 against $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$. That is useful. $\mathrm{Err}$ is the right quantity for choosing between *procedures*, since a procedure, not a fitted model, is what you would rerun on new data. But it is not what the sentence claims.

#### The usual interval covers neither target

A CV score is often reported with an interval: the mean of the per-row losses plus or minus a normal quantile times their standard error. The next block checks how often the nominal 90% interval contains each target.

```python
print(f"90% CV interval covers the fitted model's error "
      f"{ev.coverage(cv, lin['cv_se'], err_xy):.1%}")
print(f"90% CV interval covers the average error        "
      f"{ev.coverage(cv, lin['cv_se'], lin['err']):.1%}")
```

```text
90% CV interval covers the fitted model's error 79.6%
90% CV interval covers the average error        83.3%
```

The interval's *coverage*, the fraction of datasets in which it contains the target, is 79.6% for $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ and 83.3% for $\mathrm{Err}$, both short of 90%. There are several reasons, and they compound.

First, the standard error treats the 100 per-row losses as independent, and they are not. Every fold's model was trained on most of the other folds' rows, so the models are similar and the losses on different folds are correlated. Correlated observations carry less information than the same number of independent ones, so the naive standard error is too small. That is one reason the interval is too narrow for $\mathrm{Err}$; for $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ it is also pointed at the wrong target.

Second, the centre is off: it sits 0.039 above $\mathrm{Err}$, for the training-size reason above.

Third, squared losses are skewed, with a long right tail, and a normal interval for the mean of skewed values undercovers at this sample size. Even the held-out intervals later in this section, whose losses are independent given the fitted model, cover only 87% and 84%.

Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani prove the linear-model version of the correlation result and propose *nested cross-validation*, which runs an inner CV inside each outer fold to estimate the extra variance, as a way to get the interval's width right. The point of this section comes before that: it is about what the interval's centre estimates.

### On real digits the relationship runs backwards


A simulation is only as good as its assumptions, so the same question is asked of the UCI digits. The data are split once into a fixed test set of 797 images and a training pool. From the pool, 200 training sets of 300 images are drawn. For each, logistic regression is cross-validated, then fitted to all 300 images, and that fit is scored on the fixed test set. The test set is large enough to stand in for each fit's true accuracy, and disjoint from every training draw.

```python
dig = ev.digits_cv_study(train=300, test=797, reps=200)
print(f"CV accuracy    mean {dig['cv'].mean():.4f}  SD {dig['cv'].std():.4f}")
print(f"test accuracy  mean {dig['true'].mean():.4f}  SD {dig['true'].std():.4f}")
print(f"correlation    {dig['corr']:+.3f}")
```

```text
CV accuracy    mean 0.9396  SD 0.0128
test accuracy  mean 0.9376  SD 0.0085
correlation    -0.212
```

The correlation is -0.21, and it is negative: training sets that cross-validate well produce models that do slightly worse on new digits. As in the simulation, the CV scores (standard deviation 0.0128) vary more than the test accuracies they accompany (0.0085).

One explanation, not tested here, is the same averaging seen from the other side. A training set that happens to contain more awkward, atypical digits would score poorly in CV, because its held-out folds are full of them, and would also produce a model that has seen more awkward digits and handles the test set's better. If so, CV rewards the easy sample, not the good model. Measuring each draw's difficulty would be the way to check.

The design detail that matters is the fixed, disjoint test set. Had each fit been scored on all the digits it had not trained on, the test set would be the complement of the training set: a draw that took the hard digits would leave an easier test set behind, and the two effects would be confounded. With the test set fixed in advance and disjoint from every draw, that route is closed, and the correlation is still -0.21. The figure below shows the draws.

![Correlation -0.21. The training sets that cross-validate best produce models that do slightly worse on new digits.](ml102-f1-digits.png){alt="A cloud of dots with a gently falling fitted line."}

### What answers the question about a fitted model


If the question really is how well *this* model will do, the instrument is a held-out set the model never saw, scored once. In the simulation, scoring each fit on 100 fresh rows gives a correlation of +0.48 with its true error, and the naive interval covers 87% of the time.

That comparison is unfair to CV: it spends 100 extra rows. On the same budget, fitting on 80 of the 100 rows and scoring on the other 20, the code reports the held-out estimate's correlation with its own model's error, its spread, and that model's average true error.

```python
ho, ho_err = lin["split_holdout"], lin["split_err_xy"]
print(f"fit on 80, score on 20: correlation "
      f"{np.corrcoef(ho, ho_err)[0, 1]:+.3f}   SD {ho.std():.3f}   "
      f"mean true error {ho_err.mean():.3f}")
```

```text
fit on 80, score on 20: correlation +0.266   SD 0.443   mean true error 1.341
```

The held-out estimate still tracks its own model, at +0.27, and pays twice for doing so. The estimate is noisy, with a standard deviation of 0.443, because it averages only 20 losses. And the model being estimated was fitted on less data, so its average true error is 1.341 instead of 1.255. Holding out data buys an estimate of the right target at the price of a worse model and a noisier number.

That is the real choice. Cross-validation gives a stable estimate of how a *procedure* performs on data like yours, which is what you want when choosing between procedures. A held-out set gives a noisy estimate of how one *fitted model* performs, which is what you want when deciding whether to ship it. Most reports use the first and describe it as the second. The table below summarises the comparison.

![CV is closer to the average error than to the fitted model's error. A held-out set tracks the fitted model, noisily, and its interval covers closer to the nominal rate.](ml102-f2-table.png){alt="A four-row table comparing CV and held-out estimates by correlation with their target, error, and interval coverage."}

Knowing what a CV score estimates is half of reading it. The other half is knowing how uncertain a difference between two CV scores is, which Section 2.2 takes up.

## Cross-Validation Folds Are Not Independent Samples, and the t-Test Assumes They Are

<!-- source: ml-44-model-tests; hero: ml144-hero.png -->


Section 2.1 above established that a CV score estimates the expected error of a training procedure, which makes cross-validation the right tool for choosing between procedures. Such a choice usually ends with a statistical test. Section 1.1 compared two classifiers on one test set with McNemar's test. When data are scarce, the comparison is made with cross-validation instead: both models are scored on the same folds, the ten fold-by-fold differences are fed to a paired t-test, and a small p-value is reported as evidence that one model is better. The sentence under test is the justification for that recipe: *a paired t-test on the cross-validation fold scores tells you whether one model is better than another.* It appears in papers, model-selection reports and library tutorials. If it is false, the p-values attached to many reported improvements are too small, and some of those improvements do not exist.

This section builds a case where the true answer is known to be "no difference" and counts how often five tests claim otherwise. It then explains why the paired t-test fails, and why repeating the cross-validation makes it fail more often. It describes three tests that hold their stated error rate, and finally compares their ability to detect a difference that is real.

### A comparison with a known answer


#### Two algorithms equal by symmetry

Testing a test needs a case where the answer is known, and two real algorithms almost never have exactly equal accuracy. Symmetry provides one. The task is simulated in four dimensions: two classes centred at the origin, one with standard deviation 1 and the other with standard deviation 1.6. The classes differ only in spread, so the task looks the same from every direction; any rotation of the inputs leaves it unchanged.

Algorithm A is a decision tree of depth 4. Algorithm B is the same tree fitted after a fixed random rotation of the inputs. Trees split along the coordinate axes, so on any one data set the two trees carve the space differently, are genuinely different models, and either can be better. But averaged over data sets the rotation changes nothing about the task, so the two algorithms' expected accuracies are exactly equal at every training size.

#### Five tests, one level

The study draws 400 data sets of 300 rows. On each, five tests of the hypothesis "A and B are equally accurate" are run at the 5% level. Two are the naive recipe and three are designed to be honest; all five are described below. The code also reports the true difference between the two trees on each data set, measured on 50,000 fresh rows, and for each test its rejection rate with a standard error.

```python
from standarderror.ml import combine

r = combine.ttest_study()      # 400 data sets, equal algorithms
print(f"true difference: mean {r['true_mean']:+.4f}, "
      f"sd across data sets {r['true_sd']:.4f}")
for test, rate in r["reject"].items():
    print(f"{test:16s} rejects {rate:.3f} "
          f"(+/- {r['reject_se'][test]:.3f})")
```

```text
true difference: mean -0.0002, sd across data sets 0.0175
10-fold t        rejects 0.142 (+/- 0.017)
10x10 t          rejects 0.583 (+/- 0.025)
10x10 corrected  rejects 0.050 (+/- 0.011)
5x2cv            rejects 0.043 (+/- 0.010)
McNemar          rejects 0.048 (+/- 0.011)
```

The mean true difference is essentially zero, as the symmetry requires. A test's *size* is its rejection rate when there is nothing to find, and a test at the 5% level should have a size of about 5%. The paired t-test on ten folds rejected 14.2% of the time, nearly three times its nominal level. Repeating the ten-fold split ten times with different shuffles and testing all 100 differences, which looks more thorough, rejected 58.2% of the time (printed as 0.583 above: the rate falls exactly halfway between the two, and the two roundings go opposite ways). The figure below shows the five tests' rejection rates, here and with a real difference.

![With nothing to find, the paired t-test on ten folds found something in 14.2% of data sets; the corrected test in 5.0%.](ml144-f0-tests.png){alt="Paired bars for five tests. Under equal algorithms the 10-fold t-test rejects about 14% and the 10 x 10 t-test about 58%; the three other tests sit near the dotted 5% line. With a real difference, the 10 x 10 corrected test rejects most often among the honest tests."}

### Why the paired t-test on folds is too confident


#### The fold differences are not independent

With $J$ differences $d_1, \dots, d_J$ between the two models' fold scores, the paired t-test computes

$$
t = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{s^2 / J}},
$$

where $\bar d$ is the mean difference and $s^2$ the sample variance of the differences, and compares $t$ with a t distribution on $J - 1$ degrees of freedom. The denominator is the standard error of $\bar d$ *if the $d_j$ are independent draws.*

They are not. Any two training sets in ten-fold cross-validation share eight-ninths of their rows, so the ten models of each algorithm are close relatives and their differences move together. The spread of the ten differences therefore understates how much the mean difference would vary from one data set to another, and the t statistic, which divides by that spread, comes out too large. The problem cannot be fixed by a cleverer variance formula alone: Bengio and Grandvalet proved in 2004 that no unbiased estimator of that variance can be built from a single run of K-fold cross-validation.

#### Repeating the folds makes it worse

Repeating the split makes the problem worse rather than better. The hundred differences from ten repetitions all come from the same 300 rows. The repetitions average away the noise of the fold assignment, but they cannot average away the luck of the data set. The test treats a hundred echoes of one data set as a hundred independent observations, with 99 degrees of freedom, and its denominator shrinks with the square root of the number of echoes while the uncertainty it is meant to measure does not shrink at all.

#### Cross-validation answers a narrower question

There is a second reason, and it concerns the question rather than the arithmetic. On a particular data set the two trees are genuinely different: measured on 50,000 fresh rows, the tree trained on that data set's rows differed from the rotated tree by more than two points in 22% of the data sets. Cross-validation on a single data set can at best answer "which is better *here*?". The ten-fold test's rejections pointed the same way as that data set's true difference only 67% of the time, so they did not answer that narrower question well either.

Dietterich made the distinction in 1998: comparing *algorithms* means asking about their accuracy over the data sets one might have drawn, and only the variation between data sets can answer it. This is the same distinction Section 2.1 above drew for a single model, where cross-validation estimates the average error of the training procedure rather than the error of the model actually fitted.

### Three tests that hold their level


The other three tests are built around the variation between data sets, and all three held their level: 5.0% for Nadeau and Bengio's corrected resampled t-test, 4.2% for Dietterich's 5x2cv test (0.043 in the output, the same half-way rounding) and 4.8% for McNemar's test on a single two-thirds/one-third split, each within two standard errors of 5%.

**The corrected resampled t-test** keeps the hundred differences from ten repetitions of ten folds but inflates their variance by a term for the overlap between training and test sets:

$$
t = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{1}{J} + \dfrac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\right) s^2}} .
$$

The added ratio of test-fold size to training size does not shrink as $J$ grows, so piling on repetitions no longer manufactures confidence. Bouckaert and Frank recommended it with ten repetitions of ten folds for exactly this reason.

**The 5x2cv test** uses five repetitions of two-fold cross-validation. Within a repetition the two training sets do not overlap, which removes the training-set overlap that breaks the ordinary test, and the statistic is deliberately conservative: it compares one difference with a variance pooled over the five repetitions, on five degrees of freedom.

**McNemar's test**, described in Section 1.1, sidesteps fold dependence altogether. It uses one split and counts the test rows on which only one of the two models is right.

### Holding the level costs power


Holding the level is half of a test's job. The other half is *power*: the rejection rate when there is a real difference to find. The next block reruns the study with B's tree limited to depth 2, which makes it genuinely worse, by 0.043 on average.

```python
a = combine.ttest_study(depth_b=2)   # B genuinely worse
print(f"true difference: mean {a['true_mean']:+.4f}")
for test, rate in a["reject"].items():
    print(f"{test:16s} rejects {rate:.3f}")
```

```text
true difference: mean +0.0428
10-fold t        rejects 0.350
10x10 t          rejects 0.807
10x10 corrected  rejects 0.297
5x2cv            rejects 0.085
McNemar          rejects 0.098
```

The corrected test detected the difference in 30% of data sets; 5x2cv and McNemar in 8% and 10%. Both of the latter pay for their honesty by using less of the data per decision: 5x2cv trains on half the rows, and McNemar tests on a third. The naive tests reject more often here too, but that is not power. It is the same overconfidence that made them reject when there was nothing to find, and a rejection rate is only interpretable as power for a test whose size is correct. The table below sets size and power side by side.

![A test's size is its rejection rate when there is nothing to find; its power, when there is. The naive tests buy power with size they do not have.](ml144-f1-table.png){alt="A five-row table of rejection rates with standard errors, under equal and unequal algorithms."}

Every test here is applied once, to rows the analyst touches only to compute it; Section 3.3 asks what happens to a held-out score when the same rows are consulted again and again. All of them also take for granted that the rows are interchangeable, so that any row could have landed in any fold. Sections 2.3 and 2.4 test that assumption for rows that arrive in time order and for rows that come from the same people.

## Shuffling a Time Series Is Safe Until the Errors Remember

<!-- source: ml-36-time-series; hero: ml136-hero.png -->


Sections 2.1 and 2.2 above treated the rows as interchangeable: shuffled into folds, each fold a fair sample of the rest. The last two sections of this chapter turn to the rows themselves: where they came from, and what the process that produced them allows a model to learn and a score to report. Section 3.1 finds that duplicated rows straddling cross-validation folds can leak. The cases here are less obvious, starting with the most familiar rule about rows that arrive in order: *never shuffle a time series for cross-validation; always split it forward in time.* Anyone validating a model on logged data meets it. If it is too strict, practitioners throw away data for no benefit; if it is too loose, a shuffled score overstates how the deployed model will do.

This section tests both halves of the rule on simulated series where the future error can be measured directly. It shows that shuffled folds are accurate on a stationary autoregression with its lags as features, and why; then that they become optimistic when the errors have memory; and finally that forward splitting has a bias of its own, which contiguous blocks mostly avoid.

### The experiment: three ways to cross-validate one window


The reasoning behind the rule is that neighbouring observations in time resemble each other. A shuffled fold therefore puts a test row's neighbours in the training set, and the model is graded on rows it has nearly seen. Forward splitting, which trains on the past and tests on what follows, is meant to reproduce how the model will actually be used.

The experiment uses seven simulated settings, twenty series each. In each series the model is cross-validated on the first 1,000 steps in three ways, all with five folds:

- **Shuffled folds.** Ordinary *k*-fold cross-validation with the rows shuffled first, so each test fold is scattered across the window.
- **Contiguous blocks.** The same five folds without shuffling, so each test fold is one unbroken stretch of time. The model trains on the other four fifths, before or after the block.
- **Forward splits.** The window is cut into six stretches. The first fold trains on the first stretch and tests on the second; the next trains on the first two and tests on the third; and so on. Every test block lies strictly after its training data. This is the scheme scikit-learn implements as `TimeSeriesSplit`.

The model is then refitted on all 1,000 steps and scored on the 250 steps that follow. That last number, the *future error*, is what every cross-validated estimate is trying to predict. The code reports each estimate relative to the future error: the cross-validated mean squared error minus the future one, divided by the future one. Negative means *optimistic*, positive *pessimistic*.

The settings are built from two standard processes. An *autoregression of order p*, written AR(p), is a series in which each value is a weighted sum of the previous p values plus fresh noise. The main one here is

$$
y_t = 0.5\, y_{t-1} + 0.3\, y_{t-2} + e_t,
$$

with $e_t$ independent noise. An AR(1) process $u_t = \phi\, u_{t-1} + \eta_t$ has a single coefficient $\phi$, which this section calls its *persistence*: at $\phi = 0$ the series has no memory; near 1, each value is nearly a copy of the last and the series wanders slowly. A *random walk* is the case $\phi = 1$.

Three model families are used: linear regression, five-nearest-neighbour regression (5-NN), and a random forest of 50 trees with leaves of at least five rows.

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

for r in rows.time_series_table():   # 20 series per setting
    e = r["rel"]
    print(f"{r['setting']:28s} {r['model']:7s} shuffled "
          f"{e['shuffled']:+.1%}  blocked {e['blocked']:+.1%}  "
          f"forward {e['forward']:+.1%}")
```

```text

AR(2), two lags              5-NN    shuffled -3.0%  blocked -1.5%  forward -0.1%
AR(2), two lags              forest  shuffled -2.1%  blocked -1.1%  forward +1.0%
AR(2), one lag               linear  shuffled -1.4%  blocked -1.1%  forward -0.3%
residuals, persistence 0     5-NN    shuffled -2.2%  blocked -0.4%  forward +1.0%
residuals, persistence 0     forest  shuffled -2.1%  blocked -0.7%  forward -0.1%
residuals, persistence 0.9   5-NN    shuffled -12.0%  blocked -2.1%  forward +3.5%
residuals, persistence 0.9   forest  shuffled -13.1%  blocked -3.0%  forward +2.7%
residuals, persistence 0.99  5-NN    shuffled -19.5%  blocked +11.7%  forward +20.0%
residuals, persistence 0.99  forest  shuffled -22.3%  blocked +9.4%  forward +17.9%
```

Each row is the mean over twenty series of one setting and one model. The first rows show the result the rule says should not happen. On the stationary AR(2) process, with its last two values as the features, shuffled folds were within 1% of the future error for linear regression, 3% for 5-NN and 2% for the forest. With only one lag as a feature, so that the linear model misses part of the dependence, shuffled folds were still close, within a point and a half. The figure below shows all thirteen rows at once.

![Shuffled folds were within 3% of the future error on the autoregressions and 22% optimistic for a forest when the residuals had persistence 0.99.](ml136-f0-folds.png){alt="Dot plot, one row per setting and model, three dots per row. In the first six rows all dots sit near zero. In the most persistent and drifting rows the shuffled dot is far to the left; the forward dot is to the right for the forests."}

### Why shuffling was fine on the autoregression


Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo proved the reason in 2018. What matters is not whether the rows are independent, but whether their *errors* are. When the features are the series' own lags, each row carries the history it needs: the row for step $t$ contains the values at $t-1$ and $t-2$. In an AR(2) process, once those two values are known, what remains of $y_t$ is the fresh noise $e_t$, which is unrelated to the noise at any other step. A shuffled fold does put the rows for $t-1$ and $t+1$ in training, but their errors tell the model nothing about the error at $t$, which was drawn fresh.

Stated formally, cross-validation of a model with features $x_t$ requires the errors $\varepsilon_t = y_t - f(x_t)$ of different rows to be uncorrelated given the features,

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_s, \varepsilon_t \mid x_s, x_t) = 0 \quad \text{for } s \neq t,
$$

not the rows themselves to be independent. An autoregression with enough lags among its features satisfies this. The memory of the series is in the features, so it is not in the errors.

The rule is thus a heuristic for a narrower condition. When the condition holds, shuffling costs nothing, and it uses the data more evenly than any forward scheme can: every fold trains on four fifths of the window.

### When the errors have memory, shuffling is optimistic


The condition fails when something that affects the target changes slowly and the features do not record it. The residual settings make this explicit. The target depends on an observed input $x$ through a smooth curve, plus a residual $u$ that the model never sees:

$$
y_t = \sin(2 x_t) + x_t + 0.7\, u_t .
$$

Both $x$ and $u$ are independent AR(1) processes with unit variance; $x$ has persistence 0.95, and the persistence of $u$ is set to 0, 0.9 or 0.99. At persistence 0 the residual is fresh noise at each step, and shuffled folds were within 2% for the forest. At 0.9 they were 13% optimistic, and at 0.99, 22%.

#### A likely mechanism: accidental correlation within a window

A likely mechanism, which the measurement supports without isolating, is that over any single window of 1,000 steps two independent slow processes look correlated. The next block measures the typical correlation between $x$ and $u$ within a 1,000-step window, for each persistence of $u$.

```python
w = rows.window_correlation()   # x and u independent by construction
for phi, c in w.items():
    print(f"persistence {phi}: typical correlation of x and u "
          f"in a 1,000-step window {c:.3f}")
```

```text
persistence 0.0: typical correlation of x and u in a 1,000-step window 0.031
persistence 0.9: typical correlation of x and u in a 1,000-step window 0.113
persistence 0.99: typical correlation of x and u in a 1,000-step window 0.177
```

Over all time, $x$ and $u$ are unrelated by construction. Within one window, the typical correlation is 0.031 when $u$ has no memory and 0.177 when it has a lot. The reason is a standard result about sample correlations of autocorrelated series. For two independent stationary series observed for $n$ steps, the variance of their sample correlation is approximately

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \rho_x(k)\,\rho_u(k),
$$

where $\rho_x(k)$ and $\rho_u(k)$ are the autocorrelations at lag $k$. When both series are persistent, many terms are large, and the window behaves as though it held far fewer than $n$ independent observations.

A flexible model learns the window's accidental relationship between $x$ and the target as though it were part of the curve. Shuffled folds test it on rows from the same window, where the accident still holds, and reward it. The next 250 steps bring a different accident. This is the previous section's condition failing: the errors are correlated through the slow residual.

#### A drifting level

A drifting level does the same thing in a stronger form, because a random walk never forgets. In these settings the AR(2) process sits on a level that follows a random walk. A forest given the last two values was 19% optimistic under shuffled folds. Given a time index as well, which lets it learn the level at each moment and interpolate between neighbouring rows, it was 25% optimistic. Even linear regression, which cannot interpolate in this way, was 9% optimistic, because the future level is not where it was in the training window. The table below collects the mean squared errors behind every row.

![The right-hand column is the target. No scheme hit it in every row; shuffling missed it only where something the model could not see changed slowly.](ml136-f1-table.png){alt="A thirteen-row table of mean squared errors. In the autoregressive rows the four numbers agree to a few percent; in the persistent and drifting rows the shuffled column is the lowest."}

### Forward splitting has a bias of its own


The usual cure is forward splitting, and it has a cost the rule does not mention. Its first fold trains on one sixth of the window, its second on two sixths, and so on up to five sixths. On average, the models it scores are trained on half the data the final model will have. As Section 2.1 above showed, cross-validation estimates the error of the *procedure* at the training size it actually uses, not of the final model; wherever more data helps, a scheme that trains on less is pessimistic. The measurement shows this. With the drifting level and a time index, forward splitting overstated the forest's future error by 14%, against the 25% understatement from shuffling. With persistence 0.99 in the residuals it overstated by 18%.

Contiguous blocks, which train on four fifths of the window and test on the remaining block whether it lies in the past or the future, were the closest scheme in five of the seven hard rows. They were within 0% for the drifting forest with a time index, and within 2% for 5-NN at persistence 0.9, where forward splits were off by 4%. Forward splits were closer for the forest at persistence 0.9 and for linear regression on the drifting level.

Blocks were not perfect either. At persistence 0.99 every scheme missed: blocks by 9% high and forward splits by 18% high. The realised future error was not unusually low; scored 1,000 steps further on instead of immediately after the window, the same forests averaged 0.552 against 0.571. A likely reason, which this measurement does not isolate, is that a residual this persistent leaves only a few independent stretches in 1,000 steps. Every fold that trains on fewer rows than the final model therefore fits a noticeably worse curve. With that much memory, no split of one window estimates the future well.

Bergmeir and Benítez reached a similar conclusion on real series in 2012: blocked cross-validation was the most reliable of the schemes they compared, and the purely forward ones wasted data. The next section turns from rows ordered in time to rows grouped by the person they came from, where the same question, what the model can see of its test rows, takes a different form.

## Rows From the Same People Are Not New Data

<!-- source: ml-37-subjects; hero: ml137-hero.png -->


Section 2.3 above found that rows close in time are a problem for cross-validation only when they share something the model cannot see. People are the other common source of rows that share something: a patient measured at every visit, a customer with a year of transactions, a speaker with a hundred recordings, a writer with a page of digits. Section 3.1 measures exact duplicate rows straddling folds and notes that near-duplicates, such as the same patient twice, leak by the same mechanism with less force. This section measures that case, and the sentence that makes it easy to miss: *more rows means more data, and cross-validation over the rows measures how the model will do.* If the sentence is false, a team that collects many rows from a few people will overestimate its model, choose the data set that generalises worst, and believe it has far more evidence than it does.

This section proceeds in three steps. It first compares row-level and subject-level cross-validation with accuracy on new people, as the same 2,000 rows are drawn from fewer and fewer people. It then explains what each kind of fold measures. Finally it asks what extra rows from the same people are worth, and connects the answer to the design effect from survey sampling.

### The same number of rows from fewer people


The simulation gives each simulated person, or *subject*, two things that belong to them alone. One is a *signature*: a random offset, normal with standard deviation 2, added to all ten of their features, so that their rows sit together in feature space. The other is a *propensity*: a random offset, also with standard deviation 2, added to the log-odds of all their outcomes, so that some people are simply positive more often than others. Neither tells the model anything about a new person. What does carry over is a relationship between two features and the outcome that is the same for everyone: the shared part of the log-odds is $1.5x_1 + x_2$.

Each design has 2,000 rows; what changes is how many people they come from, from 2,000 people with one row each down to 20 people with 100 rows each. Two kinds of five-fold cross-validation are compared:

- **Row-level cross-validation** shuffles the rows and deals them into folds, ignoring who they came from. A person's rows are spread across the folds.
- **Subject-level cross-validation** keeps all of a person's rows in a single fold, so each held-out row belongs to someone the model never saw in training. In scikit-learn this is `GroupKFold`.

The target both are trying to estimate is accuracy on new people: the model is fitted on all 2,000 rows and scored on 2,000 new subjects with five rows each. Every figure is an average over six simulated data sets per design. The first block prints the results for five-nearest-neighbours (5-NN).

```python
from standarderror.ml import rows

for r in rows.subject_cv():   # 2,000 rows each time
    if r["model"] == "5-NN":
        print(f"{r['subjects']:5d} subjects x {r['per']:3d} rows: "
              f"row CV {r['row']:.3f}, subject CV {r['subject']:.3f}, "
              f"new people {r['new']:.3f}")
```

```text
 2000 subjects x   1 rows: row CV 0.737, subject CV 0.741, new people 0.741
  400 subjects x   5 rows: row CV 0.782, subject CV 0.747, new people 0.735
  100 subjects x  20 rows: row CV 0.827, subject CV 0.719, new people 0.718
   40 subjects x  50 rows: row CV 0.818, subject CV 0.674, new people 0.704
   20 subjects x 100 rows: row CV 0.825, subject CV 0.662, new people 0.685
```

With one row from each of 2,000 people, the two kinds of fold are the same thing, and row-level cross-validation agrees with the truth: 0.737 against 0.741. As the same 2,000 rows come from fewer people, the estimate and the truth move in opposite directions. Row-level cross-validation rises, to above 0.81 from 100 people down and to 0.825 with 20, while accuracy on new people falls at every step, to 0.685.

The direction matters as much as the size. A practitioner choosing between data sets by their row-level scores would pick the ones built from 100 people or fewer, which look like the best of the five, and the one from 20 people scores the forest highest of all. Row-level folds reward exactly the data sets that generalise worst. The figure below shows the opposing trends for all three models.

![From 20 subjects, row-level cross-validation scored five-nearest-neighbours at 0.825; on new people it scored 0.685.](ml137-f0-cv.png){alt="Dashed lines for row-level cross-validation climb as the number of subjects falls from 2,000 to 20; solid lines for accuracy on new subjects fall; dotted subject-level lines follow the solid ones."}

### What each kind of fold measures


#### Row-level folds reward recognising people

A row-level fold holds out one fifth of each person's rows and trains on the other four fifths. Consider a held-out row from one of 20 people. Because the signature shifts all of that person's features together, the row's nearest neighbours in the training set are almost all rows from the same person: 96.6% of the five nearest training rows, in this simulation. Those rows also carry the same propensity, so their labels lean the same way as the held-out row's label. Five-nearest-neighbours therefore answers by asking the person's other rows what usually happens to this person. That is an excellent way to predict a person the model has already met, and no way at all to predict a new one, whose signature places them in an unfamiliar region and whose propensity is unknown.

The forest finds the same shortcut through its splits: 0.847 under row-level folds, against 0.770 on new people. A tree can isolate the region of feature space a person's signature occupies and assign it that person's usual outcome. Logistic regression, which can only draw one plane through the feature space, has less room to memorise people, and its gap is smaller but not zero: 0.824 against 0.783. A plausible reason, not isolated here, is that with 20 people a few of them can by chance have signatures that line up with their propensities, and a linear model fits that accident as though it were part of the shared relationship.

#### Subject-level folds measure new people

The repair is to make the folds match the question. Subject-level cross-validation scored the nearest-neighbour model at 0.662 and the forest at 0.751 with 20 subjects, slightly below the truth. The pessimism is likely due in part to training size: each fold trains on 16 people instead of the 20 the final model sees, and with so few people, losing four is a noticeable loss of information about what generalises. It is a feature of very few groups. With 400 subjects the same folds were a little optimistic, by up to 0.012. The table below collects all fifteen combinations of design and model.

![Row-level cross-validation measures how well the model knows the people it has met. Subject-level cross-validation measures how well it does on new ones.](ml137-f2-table.png){alt="A fifteen-row table. With one row per subject the three accuracy columns agree; with 20 subjects row-level CV is 4 to 14 points above accuracy on new subjects."}

### What more rows from the same people buy


The second half of the sentence, that more rows mean more data, can be measured directly by growing the training set in two ways: one row from each of N new people, or N/20 rows from each of the same 20 people. Both training sets are scored on new people.

One row per person keeps improving: logistic regression goes from 0.785 at 100 rows to 0.810 at 6,400. Twenty people with more and more rows each barely move, from 0.772 to 0.788. #### The design effect

Survey statisticians have a name for this: the *design effect* of cluster sampling. Suppose $n$ rows come in clusters of $m$ rows each, and the *intra-cluster correlation* $\rho$ is the correlation between two rows from the same cluster, equivalently the share of the outcome's variance that lies between clusters rather than within them. Kish's formula gives the *effective sample size*, the number of independent rows that would estimate a mean with the same precision:

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{1 + (m - 1)\rho}.
$$

The formula is about survey means, not fitted models, but it describes the pattern measured here. When $\rho = 0$, every row counts fully. When $\rho$ is large and $m$ is large, the denominator grows with $m$, and adding rows to existing clusters barely increases $n_{\text{eff}}$. Here the differences between people are large compared with what varies within a person, so extra rows from the same person add almost nothing about what is true of people in general. They add a great deal about that person, and that is precisely what row-level folds reward.

### Which question is being asked


Row-level cross-validation is not wrong in itself; it answers a different question. If the model will only ever be used on the people it was trained on, such as predicting a known customer's next purchase or a known patient's next reading, then held-out rows from the same person are exactly the relevant test, and subject-level folds would be too pessimistic. Saeb and colleagues made this point for clinical models built from smartphone data: the right split is the one that imitates the use. The mistake is to answer one question and report the answer as though it were the other.

The same logic covers any grouping the deployment will not share: hospitals when a model moves to a new hospital, schools, devices, plots of land in ecology, and the writers of handwritten digits. Roberts and colleagues reviewed it for temporal, spatial and phylogenetic groupings and reached the same rule: block the folds by whatever the new data will be new in.

Every section of this chapter has assumed one thing more: whatever cross-validation estimates, it estimates honestly only if nothing from the held-out fold reaches the fitted model. Chapter 3 measures what happens when preprocessing breaks that rule.

## In practice

### Reading a cross-validated score

- **Name the target.** When you report a CV score, describe it as the average performance of the training procedure on samples of this size, not as the performance of the deployed model.
- **Use CV to choose procedures.** Comparing algorithms, feature sets or hyperparameter settings is a question about $\mathrm{Err}$, and CV is the right tool; the pairing from Section 1.1 applies, since both procedures are scored on the same rows.
- **Hold data out to judge a model you will ship.** If the deployment decision depends on the specific fitted model being good, score it once on data it never saw, and accept that the estimate is noisy and the model slightly weaker.
- **Distrust the naive CV interval.** Its per-row standard error ignores the correlation between folds, and its nominal 90% covered neither target at 90% in Section 2.1. If the width matters, look at nested CV.
- **Do not read meaning into a good CV score for one particular sample.** In Section 2.1, a better-than-usual CV score said nothing about the fitted model in simulation, and on the digits it went with slightly worse models.

### Comparing two models

- **Stop running a plain paired t-test on cross-validation fold scores,** and never on repeated folds with $J - 1$ degrees of freedom. Its p-values are too small.
- **Use the corrected resampled t-test on repeated cross-validation** when data are scarce: multiply the variance of the differences by $1/J + n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$. Of the honest tests in Section 2.2 it was the most powerful.
- **Use McNemar's test on a large held-out set** when there are enough rows to set one aside.
- **Know which question you are answering.** A test on one data set says, at best, which model is better on that data set. Claims about algorithms in general need variation between data sets.
- **Report the size of the difference with its p-value.** A significant difference can be too small to matter; Section 1.1's error bars show directly how large a gap the data can resolve.

### Rows in time order

- **Choose the scheme from the residuals.** Compute the autocorrelation of the out-of-fold residuals, in time order, at a short, a medium and a long lag. If it is near zero, the features carry the memory and shuffled folds are valid.
- **When the residuals are autocorrelated, use contiguous blocks.** Their gap from shuffled folds measures how much a slow unseen component flatters the shuffled score.
- **Do not treat forward splitting as unbiased.** Its folds train on half the data the final model sees, on average, so it tends to be pessimistic wherever more data helps.
- **Expect rough estimates when the residuals barely move.** With very persistent residuals, no split of one window predicted the future error well; widen the reported uncertainty rather than trusting any one scheme.

### Rows from the same sources

- **Name the unit of deployment before choosing folds.** Ask what the new data will be new in (people, accounts, devices, sites, hospitals) and group the folds by that identifier, for example with `GroupKFold`.
- **Run both kinds of cross-validation and report the gap.** The difference between row-level and grouped scores is the part of the model's apparent skill that comes from recognising sources it has already seen.
- **Count sources, not rows.** Compute the intra-cluster correlation of the target within each source and apply Kish's formula. Quote the effective sample size alongside the row count.
- **Do not choose between data sets by row-level scores.** In Section 2.4, row-level scores rose as the data came from fewer people while true accuracy fell.
- **Spend collection effort on new sources.** When between-source variation is large, a row from a new person is worth far more than another row from a known one.
- **With very few sources, use leave-one-source-out folds** and state the uncertainty plainly.

## Limits of the evidence

### What cross-validation estimates (Section 2.1)

- **The zero correlation is for this model and this size.** Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani show that it holds for linear models broadly. With very many rows per feature, the fitted model's error varies little from one training set to the next, so $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ and $\mathrm{Err}$ move closer together and the distinction matters less. Other model classes were not measured here. At 100 rows and 20 features it matters a lot.
- **The digits test set is a proxy for the truth.** It is a sample of 797 images, so each model's score on it is its true accuracy plus an error of order 0.008, shared in part between models because they face the same images, but not identical. The sign of the correlation survived the switch to a fixed test set; its size should be read loosely.
- **Squared error is skewed.** Part of the interval undercoverage, for every estimator in the table, comes from using a normal interval for a mean of skewed losses. The comparison between estimators is fair; the absolute coverages are a little worse than a better interval would give.

### Tests on cross-validation folds (Section 2.2)

- **One task, small data.** Three hundred rows and shallow trees, where variation from one data set to another is large compared with the differences between algorithms. With many more rows the variation between data sets shrinks; whether the naive tests' excess shrinks with it was not measured here.
- **Unstable learners.** Decision trees change a lot when the data change, which makes the dependence between folds matter. Stable learners such as regularised linear models change less from fold to fold, and the naive tests' excess may be smaller for them; that was not measured here.
- **One comparison.** Comparing many models at once, or one model across many data sets, raises further problems: multiple testing, and, for many data sets, the rank-based tests Demšar recommended in 2006.
- **Significance is not size.** None of these tests says whether a difference is large enough to matter, which Section 1.1's error bars address more directly.

### Time series (Section 2.3)

- **Simulated series with known structure.** Real series have seasonality, structural breaks and regime changes, each a slow component of the kind that makes shuffling optimistic. The settings here isolate the mechanism; they do not say how much of it a given real series has.
- **One horizon.** The future error was measured over the 250 steps after training, refitting once. A model refitted every step, or scored at a longer horizon, has a different target, and the comparison between schemes would shift.
- **No gaps between folds.** Blocked schemes are often run with a gap of a few steps on each side of the test block, to stop lagged features from reaching across the boundary. With two lags the boundary effect is small; with long lag windows it is not.
- **Means over twenty series.** Every estimate here is an average. On a single series the spread of each scheme is wide, and a single forward split, the most common choice in practice, is the noisiest of all.

### Grouped rows (Section 2.4)

- **Strong subject effects, by construction.** The signature and the propensity both have a standard deviation of 2, large enough that a person's rows are easy to recognise. Where differences between people are small, the gap between the two kinds of cross-validation shrinks towards the one-row-per-person case, where it vanishes.
- **Effects that do not transfer at all.** Here nothing about a person helps with a new one. In practice some person-level traits are measured, and a model that uses them generalises better; the problem is the part that is not measured.
- **Twenty subjects is few.** Subject-level cross-validation with 20 groups trains on 16 of them per fold, and its estimates are noisy from data set to data set. With very few subjects, leave-one-subject-out folds and an honest statement of the uncertainty are the best available.
- **Balanced subjects.** Every subject contributed the same number of rows. With a few heavy contributors, row-level scores are dominated by them, and the distortion can be worse.

## Key points

1. "The error of your model" can mean the error of the model you fitted ($\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$) or the average error of models fitted to data like yours ($\mathrm{Err}$). They are different numbers, and cross-validation estimates $\mathrm{Err}$: on simulated least squares, the 10-fold CV estimate's correlation with $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ is -0.01.
2. CV's naive 90% interval covers $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ 80% of the time and $\mathrm{Err}$ 83%: partly because the per-row losses are not independent, partly because they are skewed and CV's centre sits above $\mathrm{Err}$.
3. On real digits the correlation is -0.21: training samples that cross-validated well generalised slightly worse. To evaluate a model you will ship, hold data out. To compare procedures, cross-validate. Say which one you did.
4. With two algorithms of exactly equal expected accuracy, the paired t-test on ten folds declared a difference in 14.2% of data sets, and on ten repetitions of ten folds in 58.2%. Fold scores share training rows, and repeated folds share the data set; neither carries the variation between data sets that the comparison is about.
5. The corrected resampled t-test, 5x2cv and McNemar's test held their 5% level; of the three, the corrected test detected a genuine difference of 0.043 most often (30%). A naive test's high rejection rate under a real difference is not power; it is the same overconfidence.
6. What cross-validation needs is uncorrelated errors given the features, not independent rows. On a stationary autoregression with its lags as features, shuffled folds were within 3% of the future error for all three models.
7. Shuffled folds are optimistic when something unseen changes slowly: 13% at residual persistence 0.9, 22% at 0.99, and 25% with a drifting level and a time index. Check the residuals of a shuffled-fold model for autocorrelation before trusting it.
8. Forward splitting trains on less data and was 11% to 20% pessimistic for the drifting forests and at persistence 0.99. Contiguous blocks were the closest scheme in five of the seven hard rows.
9. Rows from the same person share what makes that person different. With 2,000 rows from 20 people, row-level cross-validation scored five-nearest-neighbours at 0.825; on new people it scored 0.685. As the same rows came from fewer people, row-level scores went up while true accuracy fell, so row-level folds favoured exactly the data sets that generalise worst.
10. With 20 subjects, subject-level folds matched the truth to within 0.023, slightly pessimistic. 6,400 rows from 20 people trained logistic regression to 0.788, about what 100 rows from 100 people gave: extra rows from the same people added far less than their count, the pattern Kish's design effect describes for survey means.
11. Count people, not rows, and split the folds by whatever the deployment will be new in.

- Alpaydin and Kaynak, UCI Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits, as bundled with scikit-learn: the real dataset used in Section 2.1.
- Dietterich, "Approximate statistical tests for comparing supervised classification learning algorithms", *Neural Computation* (1998): the 5x2cv test, McNemar's test for classifiers, and the distinction between comparing models and comparing algorithms.
- Nadeau and Bengio, "Inference for the generalization error", *Machine Learning* (2003): the corrected resampled t-test.
- Bouckaert and Frank, "Evaluating the replicability of significance tests for comparing learning algorithms", *PAKDD* (2004): why ten repetitions of ten folds with the correction is a sound default.
- Bengio and Grandvalet, "No unbiased estimator of the variance of K-fold cross-validation", *JMLR* (2004): the impossibility result behind the failure of the naive test.
- Bergmeir and Benítez, "On the use of cross-validation for time series predictor evaluation", *Information Sciences* (2012): an empirical comparison of cross-validation schemes on real series, favouring blocked folds.
- Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo, "A note on the validity of cross-validation for evaluating autoregressive time series prediction", *Computational Statistics & Data Analysis* (2018): the proof that shuffled folds are valid for autoregressions with uncorrelated errors.
- Roberts et al., "Cross-validation strategies for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical, or phylogenetic structure", *Ecography* (2017): a broad treatment of blocked cross-validation for dependent data of every kind, temporal and grouped alike.
- Kish, *Survey Sampling* (1965): the design effect and effective sample size for clustered samples.
- Saeb et al., "The need to approximate the use-case in clinical machine learning", *GigaScience* (2017): why subject-wise and record-wise splits answer different questions, with clinical examples.
- Section 3.1 of this book: leakage from duplicated rows that straddle folds.
