# Perfect Training Accuracy Was Not the End of Learning

Chapter 26 trained a network until it fitted every training label, and watched its test accuracy fall as it memorised noise; the lesson there was to stop early. This chapter tests the belief that makes stopping at a perfect training score feel natural: *once training accuracy reaches 100%, learning is over*. Every training example is classified correctly, so the gradient has nothing left to correct except confidence, and nothing of substance should change. Practitioners act on this whenever they end training, or set an early-stopping rule, at the point where the training set is fitted. If it is false, a run stopped there may discard a model that would have generalised far better given more steps.

The chapter uses a task with an exact rule and no label noise, addition modulo 97. It first shows a network fitting its training pairs early with no ability to answer unseen ones, and then, thousands of steps later, answering almost all of them. It then shows that weight decay drives the change, by following the size of the network's weights and by switching weight decay off. Finally it varies the amount of training data, and closes with why the result does not license training past 100% in general.

## A task with a rule and nothing else

*Modular addition* takes two whole numbers and returns the remainder of their sum after division by a fixed modulus; with modulus 97, $(a + b) \bmod 97$ is $a + b$ if that is below 97 and $a + b - 97$ otherwise. Every pair $(a, b)$ of numbers from 0 to 96 has a label, 9,409 pairs in all. A small network is trained on a random 3,763 of them, 40%, and asked about the other 60%. Because the labels follow an exact rule, a model that learned the rule would answer every unseen pair correctly, and there is no noise in the training labels to memorise.

The network knows nothing about numbers. Each of $a$ and $b$ is represented by a learned *embedding*, a vector of 128 parameters looked up by index and adjusted during training, so that the number 5 is at first just a row of random values. The two embeddings are concatenated and passed through one hidden layer of 256 ReLU units, and the output is one of 97 classes. Training is full-batch, with every training pair used at every step, for 10,000 steps.

The optimiser is AdamW, a variant of Adam with *decoupled weight decay*. At every step, besides the Adam update driven by the loss, each parameter $\theta$ is shrunk towards zero in proportion to its own value,

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\lambda\,\theta,
$$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate (here 1e-3) and $\lambda$ the weight decay (here 1, or 0 for a control). Weight decay is a steady pressure towards small parameters, which the loss gradient opposes only where the training loss needs larger ones.

The full runs take long enough that their training curves are stored and read back. The first block reads them and reports, for four settings of training fraction and weight decay, when training accuracy first reached 100%, when test accuracy first passed 99%, and the test accuracy after 10,000 steps. The second block reruns the first 200 steps of the first setting from scratch and prints its accuracies beside the stored ones, as a check that the stored curves are what the code produces.

```python
from standarderror.ml import nets

runs = nets.grokking_runs()   # written by scripts/train_grokking.py
for r in runs:
    print(f"{r['frac']:.0%} of pairs, weight decay {r['weight_decay']:g}: "
          f"100% training at step {r['train_100']}")
    reach = f"at step {r['test_99']}" if r["test_99"] else "not reached"
    print(f"    99% test {reach}; test after 10,000 steps "
          f"{r['final_test']:.3f}")
```

The rerun matches the stored curve to four decimals at both checkpoints. The first setting, 40% of pairs with weight decay 1, is the one to follow. Its training accuracy reaches 100% at step 200. At that moment test accuracy is 0.000: not chance, which would be about 0.010 with 97 classes, but below it. The network has learned a table of the 3,763 answers it was shown and nothing that transfers to any other pair.

## The network memorises first and generalises later

By the sentence under test, training is over at step 200. It continues anyway, and for a long time nothing visible happens, as the figure above shows. Training accuracy stays at 100% at every checkpoint. Test accuracy stays under 10% until step 2,300, 12 times as long as it took to fit the training set. Then it climbs steeply and passes 99% at step 4,900. By the end of the run the network answers every one of the 5,646 pairs it never saw.

Power and colleagues, who first reported this behaviour on small algorithmic tasks, named it *grokking*: generalisation that arrives long after the training set has been fitted. Nanda and colleagues later examined the internals of a small transformer trained on modular addition (modulo 113 rather than 97) and found what it had learned. Its embeddings place the numbers on circles, and it adds angles, which is modular addition done with trigonometry. Whether this chapter's network found the same solution was not checked; what is measured is that it found one that answers the unseen pairs.

The important point is that a memorised table and a rule both fit the training pairs perfectly. Only the rule answers the rest. "100% training accuracy" therefore does not identify what the network has learned: two quite different functions score identically on the training set, and training moved the network from the first to the second while its training score never changed. Training accuracy is a measure of fit to the training set, not a description of the function that achieves it.

## Weight decay moves the network from the table to the rule

Once every training pair is classified correctly, the labels have no errors left to correct, only confidence to add; if they were not what moved the network from the table to the rule, something else was. With weight decay, every step shrinks every parameter slightly, and the gradient pushes back only where the training loss needs it. The network's *parameter norm*, the length of the vector of all its parameters, summarises the result. With weight decay it falls steadily, from 112 when the training set is first fitted to 76 when the rule is found, and on to 65 at the end of the run.

The reading Liu, Michaud and Tegmark give, and that this curve fits, is that the memorised table is expensive in weight norm and the rule is cheap. A table must store a separate answer for each of thousands of pairs, which takes many large weights; a rule that works for every pair can be represented compactly. Both fit the training pairs. Weight decay keeps charging for size, so the network drifts, step by step, towards the solution that fits the data with the smallest weights, and that solution is the rule.

The control is to switch weight decay off and change nothing else, which is the second run. It also fits the training set at step 200. Its parameter norm grows instead of shrinking, from 131 at that step to 248, and after 10,000 steps its test accuracy is 0.1%. Without the pressure on size, the memorised table is as good a resting place as any, and nothing moves the network away from it. The figure below shows the two norm curves.

![With weight decay the parameter norm falls from 112 when the training set is fitted to 76 when the rule is found. Without it, the norm ends at 248.](ml127-f1-norm.png){alt="The weight-decay curve falls steadily from about 110 to about 65; the curve without weight decay rises instead."}

## The less data, the longer the rule takes

The last two runs keep weight decay and change the share of pairs in the training set. With 60% of the pairs the rule arrives sooner, test accuracy passing 99% at step 1,500 against 4,900 with 40%. With 30% it does not arrive within 10,000 steps, and test accuracy ends at 0.056.

That is the shape Power and colleagues reported: the delay between memorising and generalising grows quickly as the training fraction shrinks. The account above offers a reason. The fewer pairs there are to fit, the cheaper a table that fits them, and the longer weight decay takes to make the rule the cheaper of the two. The table below collects all four runs.

![Every run fits its training pairs within a few hundred steps. When, and whether, it then finds the rule depends on weight decay and on how many pairs it sees.](ml127-f2-table.png){alt="A four-row table of when each run reaches 100% training accuracy and when its test accuracy passes 10% and 99%."}

## In practice

- **Treat 100% training accuracy as a statement about fit, not about learning.** It says the network fits the training set, not which function it fits; only held-out data say which.
- **Do not stop a run because the training set is fitted.** Base stopping on a validation curve, and when the task plausibly has a simple underlying rule, look at what happens well past the point of fit before deciding.
- **Track the parameter norm alongside validation accuracy.** Here the norm kept falling under weight decay after the training set was fitted, while the network moved from the table to the rule; a norm that is still changing is a hint that the run may not be finished.
- **Set early-stopping patience with this in mind.** A validation score flat at its floor for a long stretch is not proof that nothing more will happen; here it stayed there for over a thousand steps before rising.

## Limits of the evidence

- **A task with an exact rule.** Modular addition has a compact rule that fits every pair, including the unseen ones. Most real data have no such rule, only a noisy relationship, and Chapter 26's network, trained on real digits with noisy labels, got worse after fitting, not better. Grokking shows the sentence is false; it is not evidence that training past 100% is generally wise.
- **Strong weight decay, full batches.** A weight decay of 1 with AdamW is far stronger than usual, and full-batch training removes the noise of minibatches. Both make the slow drift easy to see. Power and colleagues report a longer delay with weaker decay; without any, here, the rule did not arrive at all.
- **Early stopping would have failed.** A validation set drawn from the unseen pairs would have shown accuracy at 0 and stayed there for over a thousand steps after the training set was fitted. Any reasonable patience setting would have stopped the run long before the rule arrived. The same tool that rescued Chapter 26's network discards this one.
- **One seed per setting.** Each row of the table is one run, and the timings would move with the seed. The pattern, fitting first and generalising much later under weight decay, is the one reported across many seeds in the literature.

## Key points

1. 100% training accuracy says the network fits the training set, not how. Here a memorised table and the rule both fitted it perfectly.
2. Learning continued after the training set was fitted. Training accuracy reached 100% at step 200 with test accuracy 0.000, and test accuracy passed 99% at step 4,900.
3. In these runs weight decay drove the switch, the parameter norm falling from 112 to 76 on the way. Without it, in the one control run, test accuracy was 0.1% after 10,000 steps.
4. More training data brought the rule sooner: step 1,500 with 60% of the pairs.
5. Neither "stop at 100%" nor "train past it" is a rule. What happens after the fit depends on whether there is a simpler solution to find and whether anything pushes the network towards it.
