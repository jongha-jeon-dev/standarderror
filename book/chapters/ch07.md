# Trees and Forests

Chapter 6 pushed linear models past the conditions their textbooks assume. This chapter does the same for decision trees and the random forests built by averaging them. Trees reach a practitioner in two different roles. A single shallow tree is chosen when someone needs to read the model: its rules can be put on a slide or handed to a clinician. A forest of hundreds of trees is chosen when someone needs accuracy without having to specify the shape of the relationship, and it comes with an error estimate that costs nothing extra. Each role rests on a sentence that is usually taken on trust, and each sentence fails in a specific, measurable way.

The chapter tests three sentences, one per section:

- Section 7.1: *a decision tree is interpretable.*
- Section 7.2: *out-of-bag error is free cross-validation.*
- Section 7.3: *tree ensembles can approximate any function.*

Section 7.1 refits a shallow tree on bootstrap resamples of a breast-cancer training set. The accuracy barely moves, but the root feature, the thresholds, the features in the top levels and the diagnoses near the decision boundary all do, because a greedy split search settles a close contest between correlated features according to which rows were drawn. The natural response, averaging many trees grown on bootstrap resamples, is what a random forest does, and the bootstrap gives the forest something extra: every training row has trees that never saw it. Section 7.2 tests the error estimate built from those trees against cross-validation and a held-out set. It finds the estimate accurate for large forests, pessimistic for small ones for two separable reasons, and blind when rows are copies of one another. Section 7.3 turns from what a forest reports about itself to what it can predict. Because every tree predicts with averages of training labels and places its thresholds inside the training data, a forest, and boosted trees too, go flat beyond the range of inputs they were trained on. The section measures the cost on a simulation with a trend and on real data, and shows how fitting the trend first removes it.

The three sections share one thread. What a tree or a forest tells you, whether in its printed structure, in its own error estimate or in the range of its predictions, is a property of the sample it was fitted to, and in each case a check that uses only the training data shows how far that property reaches: refitting on resamples, looking for copies among the rows, comparing the ranges of the data to be scored with those of the data seen. Section 7.1 introduces the bootstrap, on which Section 7.2 builds directly; Section 7.3 can be read on its own. Chapter 8 continues with the other ways trees and models are combined: boosting, averaging and stacking.

## Resample the Rows and the Tree Tells a Different Story

<!-- source: ml-11-tree-stability; hero: ml111-hero.png -->


The chapter starts with the reason a single tree is chosen at all: *a decision tree is interpretable.* A practitioner meets this sentence whenever a shallow tree is preferred to a more accurate model because its rules can be shown on a slide or handed to a clinician. A depth-3 tree is at most eight rules that a person could check by hand: *if the largest nuclei have a perimeter above about 106, call it suspicious and look further down the tree.* That readability is often the whole argument for the tree. If its rules are a property of the rows that happened to be drawn rather than of the population, reading them is reading noise with confidence.

This section refits a depth-3 tree on 200 bootstrap resamples of a breast-cancer training set and compares each with the tree fitted to all the rows. It then explains why the root moves, follows the instability down the tree and into the predictions, and asks which statements about the data survive resampling.

### A stable score from an unstable structure


#### What would make a tree a property of the data

Reading a tree as an account of the data assumes that another sample from the same population would give roughly the same tree. That is checkable without new data, by the *bootstrap*. A bootstrap resample of a training set with $n$ rows is a set of $n$ rows drawn from it at random *with replacement*, so some rows appear more than once and others not at all. The variation of a statistic across resamples approximates its variation across samples from the population; here the statistic is the tree itself.

The data are the Wisconsin breast-cancer measurements as bundled with scikit-learn: 30 features describing the cell nuclei in images of each tumour, and a benign or malignant diagnosis. One stratified 70/30 split leaves 398 training tumours and 171 held out. The tree is scikit-learn's `DecisionTreeClassifier` limited to depth 3, the size people actually draw on a slide. The code fits one such tree to each of 200 bootstrap resamples of the 398 training rows and reports the full-data tree's root, how often each feature is the root, and the resampled trees' accuracy on the 171 held-out tumours.

```python
from standarderror.ml import trees as tr

b = tr.tree_bootstrap(boots=200, depth=3)
print(f"full-data root: {b['full_root']}")
for name, k in b["roots"]:
    print(f"  root in {k:3d} of 200 resamples: {name}")
acc = b["accuracy"]
print(f"test accuracy {sum(acc) / len(acc):.3f}, sd {b['accuracy_sd']:.3f}")
```

```text
full-data root: worst perimeter
  root in 100 of 200 resamples: worst perimeter
  root in  59 of 200 resamples: worst concave points
  root in  17 of 200 resamples: mean concave points
  root in  15 of 200 resamples: worst radius
  root in   9 of 200 resamples: worst area
test accuracy 0.914, sd 0.015
```

#### The accuracy holds and the root does not

The last line is what a model-selection report would show, and it is steady: 0.914, with a standard deviation of 0.015.

The root is a different matter. The tree fitted to all 398 rows splits first on *worst perimeter*, the perimeter of the largest nuclei in the image. A resampled tree agrees in 100 of the 200 resamples, 50% of the time. *Worst concave points* (the number of concave portions of the contour of the most irregular nuclei) wins 59 times, and three more features take the rest. The figure below shows the distribution.

![The full-data tree splits on worst perimeter first, and a resample agrees 50% of the time. The five contenders' best splits are within 5% of one another, so which one wins is decided by which rows were drawn.](ml111-f0-roots.png){alt="Horizontal bars: worst perimeter is the root in half the resamples, worst concave points in about 30%, and three other size and shape features in the rest."}

### Why the root moves: a close contest decided greedily


#### How a tree chooses a split

A classification tree is grown *greedily*. At each node it tries every feature and every threshold on that feature, and for each candidate split it computes how much the split reduces the *impurity* of the node, a measure of how mixed the classes are. scikit-learn's default is the *Gini impurity*: for a node in which a fraction $p_k$ of rows belong to class $k$,

$$
G = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2 ,
$$

which is zero for a pure node and largest when the classes are evenly mixed. The *gain* of a split is the parent's impurity minus the impurities of the two children, each weighted by the share of rows it receives. The tree keeps the single split with the largest gain, and then repeats the search separately within each child. It never revisits a decision, and it discards the runners-up however close they were. When several splits are nearly as good, the winner is decided by the details of the sample, and nothing in the printed tree records that the contest was close.

#### How close the contest is at the root

The next block lists the five features whose best splits on the full training set come nearest the top, with each one's gain and that gain as a share of the best.

```python
for c in b["candidates"]:
    print(f"{c['feature']:22s} best split gain {c['gain']:.4f}"
          f"  ({c['relative']:.1%} of the best)")
```

```text
worst perimeter        best split gain 0.3435  (100.0% of the best)
worst radius           best split gain 0.3365  (97.9% of the best)
worst area             best split gain 0.3328  (96.9% of the best)
worst concave points   best split gain 0.3325  (96.8% of the best)
mean concave points    best split gain 0.3278  (95.4% of the best)
```

All five are within 5% of the best. That is not a coincidence of this dataset; the five features measure overlapping things. The perimeter, radius and area of the largest nuclei are almost deterministic functions of one another: across all 569 tumours, worst perimeter correlates with worst radius and with worst area at 0.98 or more. Concavity goes with size, less tightly: worst perimeter and worst concave points correlate at 0.82.

With contenders that close, a resample that shifts a handful of borderline tumours can plausibly change which one edges ahead. This situation, many models of similar accuracy that tell different stories about the data, is what Breiman called the *Rashomon effect*.

#### The instability propagates down the tree

The root is only the start. Every split below it is chosen on the rows the root sent down, so a different root means different subsets of rows at the second level, and a different contest at every node beneath. Even when two trees share a root feature, they need not share its cut point: across resamples whose root is worst perimeter, the threshold ranges from 104 to 115 between the 5th and 95th percentiles. The next block measures how far the variation reaches.

```python
print(f"distinct top-3-level feature sets: {b['distinct_sets']} of 200")
print(f"same set as the full-data tree: {b['full_set_same']} of 200")
print(f"test rows where some tree disagrees: {b['any_disagree']:.1%}")
print(f"two trees disagree on, averaged over pairs: "
      f"{b['pair_disagreement']:.1%}")
```

```text
distinct top-3-level feature sets: 194 of 200
same set as the full-data tree: 0 of 200
test rows where some tree disagrees: 36.3%
two trees disagree on, averaged over pairs: 7.6%
```

The 200 resamples put 194 different sets of features in the first three levels. The most common set turns up only 2 times, and the full-data tree's own set, the one a reader would be shown, turns up 0 times. (Section 22.2 reruns this measurement on another split and finds the full-data tree's set in 3 of 200 resamples; either way it is rare.) A reader who took any one of these trees as *the* structure of the data would be reading one draw from a wide distribution.

### Different rules give different diagnoses


#### The disagreement reaches the predictions

Structure could move while predictions stayed put: two trees can encode the same decision with different rules. They do not. Averaged over every pair among the first sixty trees, two trees disagree on 7.6% of the held-out tumours, about the size of their own error rate (8.6%). Across all two hundred, 36% of tumours get a different diagnosis from at least one tree. The figure below sorts the held-out tumours by the share of trees calling them malignant.

![36% of test tumours get a different diagnosis from at least one of the trees, and any two trees disagree on 7.6% of them. Each tree, read on its own, explains its answer with confidence.](ml111-f1-rows.png){alt="Dots sorted from 0 to 1: grey runs at each end where all trees agree and a coloured middle third where they do not."}

The tumours the trees disagree on are not a random selection. They are the ones near the decision boundary, where several rules of similar quality draw the line in slightly different places. Far from it, a clearly benign or malignant tumour falls on the same side of every candidate rule. That is also where a diagnosis matters most, and where a tree's printed path, "malignant because worst perimeter > 106", is least an account of the data and most an account of the sample.

#### Why the accuracy hides it

The accuracy hides all this because it is an average over tumours, and most tumours are easy. Every tree gets the easy ones right; the trees differ on the borderline ones. The score is stable because it averages over exactly the cases where the model is not. This is the lesson of Section 1.1, which showed that a test score carries an error bar wider than the gap between the classifiers it was used to rank, applied one level down: a single summary number can be steady while the thing it summarises varies. The table below sets the two side by side.

![Everything a reader would read off the tree moves; the number a model selection would look at does not.](ml111-f2-table.png){alt="A six-row table contrasting a stable test accuracy with an unstable root feature, threshold, top-level feature set and per-row prediction."}

### What survives resampling


#### Statements at the level of the feature family

Not everything moves. Every one of the 200 roots is one of 5 features, and all of them measure either the size or the concavity of the nuclei: four describe the largest, most irregular nuclei, and the fifth, mean concave points, the concavity averaged over all of them. A statement at that level, *the first thing that separates these tumours is how large and how concave their nuclei are*, holds in every resample. A statement at the level of the printed tree, *worst perimeter, cut at 106*, holds as far as the feature goes in about half the resamples, and less often to the unit, since the cut itself ranges from 104 to 115.

#### How to present a tree honestly

That suggests a way to present a tree. Report the set of features that compete for each of the top splits and how often each wins, not the single winner. Quote thresholds as ranges across resamples, not as one number. Say which statements about the data are stable and which are a property of one fit. This is more work than printing the tree, and it is what the tree's readability was supposed to buy in the first place: a claim about the data that a reader can check.

It also says what not to conclude. A tree is not evidence that its root feature is *the* most important variable, since in half these resamples worst perimeter is not the root. And a feature absent from the tree is not evidence that the feature is irrelevant: in 26% of resamples worst perimeter does not appear anywhere in the top three levels, although it is the root of the full-data tree.

The natural response to an unstable tree is to average many of them, which is what bagging and random forests do. That should steady the predictions (it was not measured here), but it gives up the single readable tree, and the importance measures that take its place have problems of their own, which Section 14.1 examines. A forest also brings something no single tree has: an estimate of its own error, computed from the trees that did not see each row. Section 7.2 tests it.

## Out-of-Bag Error Is Free, Pessimistic When Small and Blind to Copies

<!-- source: ml-13-oob; hero: ml113-hero.png -->


Averaging trees grown on bootstrap resamples, the response to instability with which Section 7.1 ended, is how a random forest is built, and the bootstrap gives the forest an error estimate built in. It is usually introduced with one sentence: *out-of-bag error is free cross-validation.* A practitioner meets this sentence whenever `oob_score=True` is set and the resulting number is reported in place of a cross-validated or held-out error, or used to choose the number of trees or other settings. The appeal is real: the estimate needs no held-out data and no refitting. If the sentence is false in some regimes, a small forest will be compared unfairly against other models, and, worse, a forest trained on data with repeated rows will report an error that describes its memory rather than its performance.

This section first explains where the out-of-bag estimate comes from and checks it against five-fold cross-validation and a held-out half of the data on two datasets, for forests from 5 to 500 trees. It then takes apart the large pessimism of small forests and separates its two causes, one a property of voting with part of a forest and one a bookkeeping choice in scikit-learn. Next it breaks the estimate's central assumption by duplicating rows. It closes with how to use the estimate well.

### Where the out-of-bag estimate comes from


#### Every row has trees that never saw it

A random forest grows each tree on a bootstrap sample of the training rows, $n$ rows drawn with replacement from the $n$ available, as in Section 7.1. Any one row is missed by each of the $n$ draws with probability $1 - 1/n$, so it is left out of the whole sample with probability

$$
\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n} \approx e^{-1},
$$

about 37% for any training set of realistic size. So every training row has a set of trees that never saw it, roughly a third of the forest, and those trees can predict it as if it were new. Collect those predictions for every row, let the trees that did not see the row vote on its class, and compare the vote with the true label. The resulting error rate is the *out-of-bag* (OOB) error: an estimate of test error that needs no held-out data and no refitting. Breiman called it free, and in scikit-learn it is one argument, `oob_score=True`.

#### Checking the claim against cross-validation and a held-out set

"Free cross-validation" is a claim that can be checked directly. The breast-cancer data of Section 7.1 above, used again in Section 14.1, are split in half, stratified by class, ten times with different seeds. On each split, three estimates are computed for forests of increasing size, all with scikit-learn's default settings otherwise: the OOB error of a forest fitted to the training half; *five-fold cross-validation* on the same training half, which fits five more forests, each on four-fifths of the half, and scores each on the remaining fifth; and the error on the untouched other half, the held-out error that both estimates are trying to approximate. The block prints the averages over the ten splits.

```python
from standarderror.ml import trees as tr

# breast cancer, 10 half splits; OOB and CV use the training half only
for r in tr.oob_study(data="breast cancer", trees=(5, 10, 25, 100, 500)):
    print(f"{r['trees']:>3} trees  out-of-bag {r['oob']:.3f}"
          f"  5-fold CV {r['cv']:.3f}  held-out {r['test']:.3f}")
```

```text
  5 trees  out-of-bag 0.137  5-fold CV 0.064  held-out 0.051
 10 trees  out-of-bag 0.071  5-fold CV 0.054  held-out 0.048
 25 trees  out-of-bag 0.052  5-fold CV 0.048  held-out 0.047
100 trees  out-of-bag 0.045  5-fold CV 0.044  held-out 0.047
500 trees  out-of-bag 0.042  5-fold CV 0.045  held-out 0.046
```

At 500 trees the claim holds: OOB 0.042, cross-validation 0.045, held-out 0.046. Both estimates are within half a point of the held-out number. Cross-validation is a little closer here; on the UCI handwritten digits, run the same way over five half splits, out-of-bag is closer, at 0.030 against cross-validation's 0.034 and a held-out 0.028. And cross-validation took five extra forests to get there.

At 5 trees the claim fails badly: OOB error is 0.137 against a held-out 0.051, 2.7 times too high. Cross-validation, at 0.064, is also pessimistic, but by far less; part of that is presumably because each of its forests is trained on four-fifths of the rows. The figure below shows the three curves on both datasets.

![At 5 trees the out-of-bag error is 2.7 times the held-out error on breast cancer and 2.8 times on digits. At 500 trees it is within 0.004 and 0.002.](ml113-f0-curves.png){alt="Solid out-of-bag lines start far above the dashed held-out and dotted cross-validation lines at 5 trees, close in on them by 100 trees and meet them by 500."}

### Why small forests look worse than they are


#### Each row is judged by about a third of the forest

The OOB prediction for a row is a vote among the trees that did not see it, not among all of them. The next block computes the expected share of a forest's trees for which a given row is out of bag.

```python
# share of all trees for which a given row is out of bag
print(" ".join(f"{T} trees: {tr.oob_coverage(T):.3f}" for T in (5, 100)))
```

```text
5 trees: 0.367 100 trees: 0.368
```

A row's OOB verdict comes from about 37% of the forest, whatever its size. With 500 trees that is around 185 trees, plenty for a stable vote, so the estimate describes a forest almost as good as the real one. With 5 trees it is one or two trees on average, and a vote of one or two trees can be expected to be worse than a forest of five, because averaging over more trees is what reduces a forest's variance. In that sense the OOB error at $T$ trees describes a forest of roughly $T/e$ trees. That distinction is negligible for a large forest and matters exactly when the forest is small enough for the difference in size to change its accuracy.

#### Rows with no vote are scored as the first class

Some rows are worse off still. The chance that a row was drawn into every one of $T$ bootstrap samples, and so is out of bag for no tree, is about $(1 - 1/e)^T$: 10% of rows at 5 trees, 1.0% at 10. Those rows have no OOB prediction at all, and scikit-learn does not drop them. It issues a warning, gives them an all-zero vote, and the predicted class becomes the first one in the label order: malignant in the breast-cancer data, the digit 0 in the digits. Every such row from another class is counted as an error. This is a bookkeeping choice, not a property of the estimate, and how much of the 5-tree number it accounts for can be measured directly. The block below splits the 5-tree OOB error into the part contributed by rows with no vote and the error rate among rows that did get one.

```python
# 5 trees: how much OOB error comes from rows that got no OOB vote
for data, reps in (("breast cancer", 10), ("digits", 5)):
    v = tr.oob_novote(data=data, trees=5, reps=reps)
    print(f"{data:13s} OOB {v['oob']:.3f}  no-vote rows "
          f"{v['from_novote']:.3f} ({v['novote_share']:.0%} of rows)"
          f"  voted rows' error {v['voted_error']:.3f}")
```

```text
breast cancer OOB 0.137  no-vote rows 0.063 (10% of rows)  voted rows' error 0.082
digits        OOB 0.301  no-vote rows 0.101 (11% of rows)  voted rows' error 0.225
```

On breast cancer, 0.063 of the 0.137, 46% of the out-of-bag error, comes from rows that received no vote. Among the rows that did get one the error is 0.082, 1.6 times the held-out 0.051. On digits the no-vote rows supply 33% of the 0.301, and the voted rows still err at 0.225, 2.1 times the held-out 0.109.

So the 5-tree pessimism is part artefact and part a genuinely smaller voting forest. The artefact disappears once every row has a vote: by 25 trees the expected share of rows without one is far below one row in a thousand. The smaller-forest effect fades more slowly, because it shrinks only as the out-of-bag third of the forest itself becomes large enough to vote stably.

#### When the pessimism matters

Pessimism is the safe direction for an error estimate, and it fades as trees are added. At 100 trees out-of-bag error is 0.045 against a held-out 0.047 on breast cancer, still 0.039 against 0.028 on digits, and by 500 trees it is gone on both. It still matters in two places. When OOB error is used to choose the number of trees, it argues for more trees than are needed, since part of its improvement with size is the estimate catching up rather than the forest improving. And when it is used to compare a forest against a model scored by honest cross-validation, a small forest is handicapped. Janitza and Hornung (2018) document a further, subtler pessimism in OOB error for small samples, which these runs were not designed to isolate. The table below collects all the runs.

![At 500 trees, out-of-bag error and five-fold cross-validation are both within about half a point of the held-out error on both datasets; at 100 trees that holds on breast cancer, while on digits both are still about a point high. Out-of-bag error costs a sixth of the fitting.](ml113-f2-table.png){alt="A ten-row table: out-of-bag error far above the others at 5 and 10 trees and close to them from 100 trees on."}

### When rows are not independent, the estimate breaks


#### The assumption behind "unseen"

The out-of-bag argument rests on one assumption: a tree that did not see a row has learned nothing specific to it. That is true when rows are independent draws from the population. It is false when the training data contain copies or near-copies: augmented images, repeated measurements of one patient, the same customer on many rows, or a careless merge that duplicated a table.

The test is to duplicate every digit image in the training half, so that each appears twice, and refit. The block prints the OOB and held-out errors with independent rows and with duplicated ones; the held-out half is not duplicated.

```python
d = tr.oob_duplicates()     # digits; each training image twice
print(f"independent rows: OOB {d['oob']:.3f}, held-out {d['test']:.3f}")
print(f"duplicated rows:  OOB {d['oob_dup']:.3f}, "
      f"held-out {d['test_dup']:.3f}")
```

```text
independent rows: OOB 0.029, held-out 0.027
duplicated rows:  OOB 0.000, held-out 0.027
```

#### Why the out-of-bag error falls to zero

Out-of-bag error is exactly 0. When one copy of an image is out of a tree's bootstrap sample, the other copy is a separate row, drawn or missed almost independently, and it is in that sample about 63% of the time, $1 - 1/e$. So most of the trees voting on a row have seen its twin, and a fully grown tree that has seen a row's twin predicts that row correctly, because the twin sits in a leaf of its own class with identical feature values. The "unseen" row was seen. The held-out error is unchanged at 0.027: the model is no better; only its own estimate of itself is broken. The figure below sets the two cases side by side.

![Whenever a row is out of bag its copy is usually in the bag, so the forest is scored on what it memorised: out-of-bag error 0.000, held-out 0.027. The model did not get better; the estimate broke.](ml113-f1-dup.png){alt="Paired bars: with independent rows both errors are near 0.03; with duplicated rows the out-of-bag bar is zero and the held-out bar is unchanged."}

This is the same failure that Section 3.1 measured for cross-validation with duplicated rows, where copies of a row landing on both sides of a fold boundary made the held-out fold partly a training fold. It has the same remedy, with a cost: the rows must be grouped and the groups held out together. A forest's built-in OOB estimate has no notion of groups. With exact copies, as here, it says nothing at all; with looser groups, such as repeated visits or related images, it should be optimistic to a lesser degree, which was not measured here. Either way, grouped cross-validation, or a held-out set split by group, has to replace it. Section 2.4 measured the cost of treating rows from the same people as new data.

### Using the out-of-bag estimate well


None of this makes out-of-bag error less useful; it says when to use it. The cost argument is real: for a forest of a few hundred trees, OOB error comes with the fit, while five-fold cross-validation fits five more forests. On large data that is the difference between an estimate and no estimate.

Three habits keep it honest. First, grow the forest first and read the OOB error last: the curves in the first figure settle by 100 trees on breast cancer and only by 500 on digits, and before they settle the number describes a weaker model than the one you have. Second, keep a held-out set anyway for the final, reported number, because the OOB error of a model chosen after looking at several OOB errors is no longer an independent estimate. Third, before trusting the estimate at all, ask whether two rows of the training data can be the same thing in disguise. If they can, the bootstrap's notion of "unseen" is wrong, and so is every number built on it, as the duplicated digits show with an error of exactly 0.

Out-of-bag error is a property of bagging. Boosting, which builds its trees in sequence rather than independently, has no equivalent, and Section 8.1 asks a different question of it: whether running it longer eventually overfits. Before that, Section 7.3 turns from what a forest reports about its own error to what it can predict at all.

## A Forest Cannot Predict Beyond the Largest Value It Has Seen

<!-- source: ml-15-extrapolation; hero: ml115-hero.png -->


Sections 7.1 and 7.2 examined what a tree and a forest report about themselves: their rules and their error. This section examines the range of what they can predict. Trees and the ensembles built from them come with a reassuring theorem attached: with enough trees and enough depth they can approximate any reasonable function. In practice that becomes the sentence under test in this section: *tree ensembles can approximate any function.* A practitioner leans on it whenever a random forest or a gradient-boosted model is chosen as a safe default because it needs no assumption about the shape of the relationship. The theorem is true, but it has a domain: it concerns the region the training data cover. If a model is deployed on inputs beyond that region, such as next year's dates, larger loans or heavier patients, a tree ensemble will not follow the trend there, and its errors will be large and all in one direction.

This section explains why a forest's predictions are bounded by its training labels, measures the consequence on a simulation with a linear trend inside and beyond the training range, shows why the predictions stop at a ceiling and how fitting the trend first removes it, repeats the comparison on real data, and gives three checks that detect the problem before deployment.

### A forest's predictions are averages of training labels


#### The averaging bound

A regression tree predicts, in each leaf, the mean of the training labels that landed there. A random forest's prediction is an average of its trees' predictions, so it too is an average, with non-negative weights, of training labels. No average of numbers can be larger than the largest of them or smaller than the smallest. Whatever a forest predicts, it predicts within the range of the labels it was trained on.

Inside the region the training data cover, that bound costs nothing: the labels there span the values the model needs to produce. Outside it, the bound is decisive. If the outcome keeps rising beyond the largest training input, it eventually passes the largest training label, and no forest can follow it there. As the next sections show, the forest in fact stops well short of that bound.

#### A simulation with a trend

The simulation that measures this has an outcome that rises as $2x$ plus a nonlinear effect of a second input,

$$
y = 2x + \sin(2z) + 0.3\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim N(0, 1),
$$

where $z$ is a standard-normal input, and two further standard-normal inputs carry nothing. There are 500 training rows with $x$ uniform between 0 and 1, and 2,000 test rows in each of four bands of $x$: the training range itself, and three bands beyond it, up to $x = 2$. Four models are compared. Two are tree ensembles from scikit-learn: a random forest, and *histogram gradient boosting* (`HistGradientBoostingRegressor`), a fast form of gradient boosting that bins each input into a histogram before searching for splits. The third is linear regression on all inputs. The fourth is a hybrid, *trend plus forest*, described below. The block prints each model's test error in each band.

```python
from standarderror.ml import trees as tr

# train: 500 rows, x in [0, 1], y = 2x + sin(2z) + noise
ex = tr.extrapolation()
for r in ex["rows"]:
    lo, hi = r["band"]
    print(f"x in [{lo:4.2f}, {hi:4.2f}]  " + "  ".join(
        f"{k} {r[k]:.3f}" for k in
        ("forest", "boosting", "linear", "trend + forest")))
```

```text

x in [1.00, 1.25]  forest 0.263  boosting 0.248  linear 0.508  trend + forest 0.119
x in [1.25, 1.50]  forest 0.841  boosting 0.798  linear 0.508  trend + forest 0.119
x in [1.50, 2.00]  forest 2.713  boosting 2.625  linear 0.508  trend + forest 0.119
```

#### Best inside the range, worst beyond it

Inside the training range the trees are the best of the single models, second only to the hybrid: 0.122 for the forest and 0.123 for boosting, against 0.508 for a linear regression that cannot represent the sine term.

Beyond the range the picture reverses. In the first band past the edge the forest's error is already 2.2 times larger than inside. Between 1.5 and 2 it is 2.71, 22 times its error inside, and the linear model, unchanged at 0.508 in every band because its misfit of the sine term does not depend on $x$, is 5.3 times better. Boosting follows the forest closely in every band. The figure below plots the predictions along $x$.

At x between 1.5 and 2 the truth averages 3.51.](ml115-f0-curves.png){alt="A dashed rising line for the truth; the forest and boosting curves follow it to x = 1 and then run flat; the linear and trend-plus-forest curves keep rising with it."}

### A ceiling, not a mistake


#### Where the predictions stop

The figure makes the mechanism plain. Along $x$, the forest and the boosted trees follow the trend to the edge of the training data and then run flat. The next block prints the largest training label and the forest's largest prediction in each band.

```python
print(f"largest training label {ex['train_y_max']:.2f}")
for r in ex["rows"]:
    lo, hi = r["band"]
    print(f"largest forest prediction at x in [{lo:4.2f}, {hi:4.2f}]: "
          f"{r['forest_max']:.2f}")
```

```text
largest training label 3.58
largest forest prediction at x in [0.00, 1.00]: 3.07
largest forest prediction at x in [1.00, 1.25]: 2.87
largest forest prediction at x in [1.25, 1.50]: 2.87
largest forest prediction at x in [1.50, 2.00]: 2.87
```

Past the edge the forest's largest prediction is 2.87 in every band, the same number whether $x$ is 1.1 or 1.9. That is below both the largest training label, 3.58, and the forest's own largest prediction inside the range, 3.07. The ceiling that binds is lower than the one the averaging argument guarantees. The averaging bound says only that predictions cannot exceed the largest label; the ceiling actually reached past the edge is set by the particular leaves that the training rows with the largest $x$ fall into.

#### Why the forest goes flat

Nothing went wrong in fitting. Every split threshold on $x$ lies inside $[0, 1]$, because thresholds are placed between observed training values. So every test row with $x$ above 1 goes the same way at every split on $x$ as the training rows with the largest $x$, falls into the same leaves, and gets their average. Its prediction can still vary with the other inputs, but not with $x$. The model has no representation of "and it keeps rising"; it has a step function that ends.

Boosting is no exception, despite being a sum of trees rather than an average. Each of its trees is still piecewise constant in $x$ with thresholds inside the training range, so each tree is constant in $x$ beyond the range, and so is their sum. A sum is not bounded by the largest label the way an average is, but it is just as flat past the last threshold.

#### Fitting the trend first

The fix keeps the trees' strengths and removes the ceiling. Fit the trend with a model that can extrapolate it, here a linear regression on $x$ alone, and fit the forest to what is left, the *residuals*: the differences between the outcomes and the fitted trend. The prediction is the sum of the two. The residuals have no trend in $x$, so the forest's flat continuation beyond the range is the right continuation for them, and the linear part carries the rise. That hybrid has error 0.110 inside the range and 0.119 between 1.5 and 2: best everywhere, as the figure below shows.

![Inside the range the forest's error is 0.12 and the linear model's 0.51. Between 1.5 and 2 the forest's is 2.71. The hybrid stays at 0.12.](ml115-f1-bands.png){alt="Grouped bars on a log scale: forest and boosting lowest in the training band and rising steeply in each band beyond; linear constant; trend plus forest lowest everywhere."}

The hybrid works because the shape of the trend was known. When it is not, the honest statement is that no model has evidence about what happens past the data, and the trees at least make that visible by refusing to guess.

### On real data the effect is smaller and still there


#### Training below a threshold and testing above it

Real extrapolation is rarely so clean. The diabetes data of Efron and colleagues, as bundled with scikit-learn, record ten baseline measurements of each patient, including body-mass index (bmi), and a measure of disease progression a year later. The model is trained on the patients with bmi at or below its 90th percentile and tested on the 45 above it, so every test patient has a higher bmi than every training patient. As a control, the same models are fitted to a random split of the same sizes. The block prints, for a forest and a linear regression on each split, the test *mean squared error* (MSE) and the mean error, the average of prediction minus outcome, whose sign shows whether the model under- or over-predicts.

```python
d = tr.diabetes_extrapolation(quantile=0.9)
for split in ("random", "beyond"):
    print(split, "  ".join(f"{m} MSE {d[split][m]['mse']:,.0f} "
                           f"(mean error {d[split][m]['bias']:+.1f})"
                           for m in ("forest", "linear")))
```

```text
random forest MSE 3,158 (mean error -11.4)  linear MSE 3,371 (mean error -12.6)
beyond forest MSE 5,136 (mean error -33.7)  linear MSE 3,660 (mean error -18.3)
```

#### Reading the result

On the random split the forest comes out ahead, 3,158 against 3,371, a gap of 7% on 45 patients, which one split cannot distinguish from a tie. On the high-bmi patients it loses by 40%, and its errors lean one way: it under-predicts their disease progression by 34 on average, against 18 for the linear model.

The likely reading, by analogy with the simulation, is that progression keeps rising with bmi past the training range and the forest stops. The linear model under-predicts too, so the outcome on these patients is also higher than either model's account of it.

The effect is much smaller than in the simulation, plausibly because bmi is one of ten inputs and the outcome is noisy, though neither was varied here. That is the realistic version: not a dramatic failure but a quiet, systematic bias on exactly the cases at the edge, such as the heaviest patients, the largest loans or the newest dates, which are often the ones a model is deployed to judge. The table below sets the two splits side by side.

![The forest edges the random split, within the noise of 45 patients, and clearly loses the extrapolation; its misses on the high-bmi patients are systematically low.](ml115-f2-table.png){alt="A four-row table: on a random split the forest has the lower error; on the highest-bmi patients the linear model does, and the forest under-predicts by more."}

### How to see the ceiling coming


The ceiling is easy to detect before it costs anything, because it is a property of the training data, not of the model's internals. Three checks cover most cases.

First, compare each input's range in the data the model will score with its range in the training data. A row whose value of an input is beyond the largest training value is a row the trees will treat as if it sat at the edge. In the simulation, every row in the last band is such a row.

Second, compare the model's predictions with the training labels. A forest's predictions are confined to the label range by construction. If the outcome you expect is outside it, as with next year's sales, a larger loan or an older patient, the model cannot produce it, and its predictions will pile up below the old maximum. Here, past the edge, they stop at 2.87, under a training maximum of 3.58.

Third, score the model on a split by the drifting variable, not only on a random one. The diabetes comparison is that check: the random split put the forest slightly ahead, and the split by bmi reversed the ranking by a wide margin.

These checks close the chapter. Its three sections have measured what a tree's structure, a forest's built-in error estimate and a tree ensemble's range can and cannot tell you. Chapter 8 turns to the other ways of combining models: boosting, averaging and stacking.

## In practice

### Before reading a tree

- **Refit before you read.** Before presenting a tree as an explanation, refit it on a few dozen bootstrap resamples of its training data and count how often its root feature and its top-level features recur.
- **Check the margin at the root.** List the best split on each feature at the root and the gain of each as a share of the best. If several are within a few per cent of one another, the root is decided by the sample, and the printed tree should say so.
- **Report competing features and threshold ranges.** For each of the top splits, give the features that win across resamples and how often, and quote thresholds as ranges rather than single values.
- **Measure prediction disagreement on held-out rows.** Compute the share of held-out rows on which some resampled tree disagrees with the original. Those rows are where the tree's explanation is weakest; flag them rather than explaining them confidently.
- **Do not read importance or irrelevance off one tree.** A root feature is not thereby the most important variable, and an absent feature is not thereby irrelevant; a correlated feature may have taken its place.

### Before trusting out-of-bag error

- **Use OOB error only once the forest has a few hundred trees.** Below that, it describes a smaller forest than the one you fitted, and in scikit-learn it also counts rows with no vote as the first class.
- **Check the warning.** If scikit-learn warns that some rows have no out-of-bag prediction, the OOB error is inflated by bookkeeping; add trees before reading it.
- **Do not compare a small forest's OOB error with another model's cross-validated error.** The forest is handicapped. Score every candidate the same way.
- **Do not tune on OOB error and report the winner's OOB error.** Keep a held-out set for the final number.
- **Look for copies before trusting it.** Count exact duplicate rows, and identify any grouping variable such as a patient, customer or device. With copies or groups, replace OOB error with grouped cross-validation or a held-out set split by group.

### Before deploying a tree ensemble

- **Check the ranges.** For each input, compare the range in the scoring data with the range in the training data, and count the rows that fall outside it. Pay most attention to inputs that drift, such as dates, sizes and prices.
- **Check the predictions against the label range.** If the outcome you expect is above the largest training label, a forest cannot produce it. Plot predictions against the drifting input and look for a flat line.
- **Validate on a split by the drifting variable.** Train on the lower part of its range, or the earlier dates, and test on the rest, alongside a random split of the same sizes. Prefer the model that does well on the split that resembles deployment.
- **Detrend before fitting trees when the trend's shape is known.** Fit the trend with a model that can extrapolate it and fit the ensemble to the residuals; add the two predictions back together. For time series, differencing or detrending does the same job.

## Limits of the evidence

### Tree stability (Section 7.1)

- **Deeper trees are unlikely to be better.** Depth 3 is the readable case. Grown without a depth limit, a tree on these 398 rows has 16 leaves and depth 6. Deeper trees were not refitted on the resamples here, but every split below the top is chosen on the rows the splits above it selected, so they can be expected to inherit the instability above them.
- **Some data have one dominant split.** When one feature separates the classes far better than any other, the root is stable and the instability starts further down. The size of the gap between the best and second-best split at the root is the thing to check, and on data with many correlated measurements it is usually small.
- **Bagging is the fix for prediction, not for reading.** *Bagging* (bootstrap aggregating) fits a tree to each of many bootstrap resamples and averages their predictions. Averaging many such trees should give predictions that move far less between resamples than any one tree's; that was Breiman's point in 1996, and it was not measured here. Bagging also gives up the single readable tree. Sections 14.1 and 14.2 measure what is left of the reading once trees are averaged.
- **Unbiased split selection is unlikely to settle the contest.** *Conditional inference trees* choose the split variable by a significance test of association with the outcome, rather than by the raw impurity decrease. That corrects a bias that Section 14.1 measures, the tendency of impurity-based selection to favour features with many possible split points. But with five candidates within 5% of one another, the resample would still be expected to decide. Conditional inference trees were not fitted here.

### Out-of-bag error (Section 7.2)

- **OOB error is not free for tuning.** Each OOB error is an honest estimate for one forest. Choosing the best of many hyperparameter settings by their OOB errors reuses the same rows to select and to score, and the winner's OOB error is optimistic, as any validation score reused for selection is.
- **Gradient boosting has no equivalent.** Its trees are fitted in sequence, each to the residuals of all the earlier ones, so no tree is independent of a row it did not sample. Subsampled boosting reports an "out-of-bag improvement", which is a different and much rougher quantity.
- **Two datasets, one model setting.** Both datasets are easy and both forests use scikit-learn's defaults. The crossover to an honest estimate came between 100 and 500 trees here; it plausibly depends on how much a single tree's error exceeds the forest's.
- **Other libraries treat the no-vote rows differently.** The artefact measured in Section 7.2 is scikit-learn's. A library that drops rows without an OOB vote, or reports them separately, would show less pessimism at 5 trees, and the same smaller-forest effect.
- **Duplicates are the extreme case.** Near-duplicates, such as augmented copies with small shifts or repeated visits by one patient, should leak less than exact copies but in the same direction; only exact copies were measured here.

### Extrapolation (Section 7.3)

- **Time is the common case.** A forest given a date or a time index as a feature is extrapolating on every future prediction. Trends, inflation and growth all live past the last training date, and a forest on raw levels will predict last year's level. Differencing or detrending first is the same fix as the trend-plus-forest hybrid.
- **Extrapolation in one feature is not extrapolation in all.** A row can be inside the range of every feature separately and still far from any training row, in a corner of the joint space that no one sampled. Trees are flat there too, and no per-feature range check will catch it.
- **Linear models extrapolate confidently, not correctly.** The linear model here was right beyond the range because the truth was linear in $x$. Had the trend bent, it would have extrapolated the wrong line with equal conviction. The trees' ceiling is a known, bounded failure; the line's is not. Section 15.3 measures what a misspecified linear model costs when its inputs shift.
- **One simulation, one real split.** The size of the ratio between errors beyond and inside the range depends on how far past the edge one tests and how steep the trend is. The ceiling does not.

## Key points

1. Two hundred bootstrap refits of a depth-3 tree on breast cancer scored 0.914 ± 0.015 on the same held-out tumours: the accuracy is a property of the data. The root feature matched the full-data tree's in 50% of the refits, and the top three levels came out as 194 different feature sets in 200 resamples, none of them the full-data tree's: the structure is a property of the sample.
2. The root moves because the candidates are close. Here the best splits on five correlated size and concavity features were within 5% of one another, and greedy selection keeps only the winner.
3. 36% of held-out tumours got a different diagnosis from at least one tree. Instability in the rules is instability in the predictions near the boundary, where the diagnosis matters most. Before reading a tree, refit it on a few dozen bootstrap resamples and report how often its root and its top-level features recur.
4. With 500 trees, out-of-bag error was within 0.004 of the held-out error on both datasets, about as close as five-fold cross-validation, for one fit instead of six.
5. With 5 trees it was 2.7 and 2.8 times the held-out error. Each row's OOB vote comes from about 37% of the forest, so a small forest is judged as an even smaller one; and scikit-learn scores rows with no vote as the first class, which supplied 46% and 33% of those errors. Use OOB error once the forest has a few hundred trees, and do not tune on it and report the winner's OOB error.
6. With duplicated rows, OOB error was 0 and the held-out error 0.027. With exact copies OOB error is not an estimate of anything; on grouped data, expect it to be optimistic.
7. A tree's prediction is an average of training labels, so a forest cannot predict beyond the range of the labels it saw; past the edge of its training range this forest went flat at 2.87, under a largest label of 3.58. Boosted trees go flat too, because each tree is constant in an input beyond its last threshold.
8. Inside the training range the trees were the best single models (0.12 against 0.51 for linear regression); between 1.5 and 2 the forest's error was 22 times larger. Fitting the trend first and the forest to the residuals was best everywhere: 0.11 inside, 0.12 beyond.
9. On diabetes, a forest that edged a random split lost by 40% on the highest-bmi patients and under-predicted them systematically. Before deploying a tree ensemble, check where its inputs will fall relative to the training data, especially any input that drifts, such as time.
