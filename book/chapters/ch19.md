# Grading a Language Model

Every earlier chapter evaluated a model the reader had trained, on a test set the reader had drawn, with a scoring rule the reader had chosen. Language models are mostly evaluated under none of those conditions. The model was trained by somebody else, on data nobody outside can inspect; the benchmark was written by somebody else and has been on the web for years; the score depends on a prompt and a scoring rule fixed before any model is run; and, increasingly, the grader is another model rather than a person. A practitioner meets all of this when choosing between two hosted models, deciding whether a fine-tune beats its base, reading a leaderboard or a vendor's contamination scan, tuning an evaluation prompt, or setting up an automated grader to decide which version ships. This chapter asks whether the habits of the earlier parts of the book survive the move, and finds that each step hides a choice that can decide which of two models looks better.

The chapter works throughout with small arithmetic transformers trained in this book's repository. They are far smaller than the models these questions are usually asked about, and that is their use: every answer can be checked exactly, so each evaluation habit can be set against the quantity it is meant to estimate rather than against another score. Section 19.1 describes the models in full; the later sections recall only what they need.

Each of the five sections tests one sentence that evaluations of language models commonly rest on:

- Section 19.1: *the model with the higher benchmark score is the better model.*
- Section 19.2: *if a model has memorised a benchmark, its likelihood on the benchmark's items will show it.*
- Section 19.3: *a benchmark score measures the model.*
- Section 19.4: *scoring a model by which answer option it finds likeliest measures the same thing as asking it for the answer.*
- Section 19.5: *a model can grade other models' answers in place of people.*

The sections move outward from the questions to the grader. Section 19.1 treats a benchmark as a random sample of questions and sets up the tools the rest of the chapter leans on: the error bar of a score, the paired comparison of two models on the same questions, and clustering rows by question. Section 19.2 drops the assumption that the questions are fresh, leaks a benchmark into training with a known dose, and shows that the leak is visible when the benchmark is compared as a set with problems that cannot have leaked, and hard to see item by item; it is the test-set discipline of Section 3.3 applied to somebody else's model. Sections 19.3 and 19.4 hold the questions fixed and vary how they are asked and scored: first the text in front of the question, then the choice between asking for the answer and offering options. Both find that a decision made before any model is run moves not only the scores but the gap between two models, and Section 19.3 meets the optimism of search from Section 4.1 again when a prompt is chosen on the items it is reported on. Section 19.5 removes the known answer altogether and lets the models grade one another, then repairs the grader with a small random sample of checked answers through prediction-powered inference. The last part of Section 19.5 draws the five results together, and the closing sections merge their practical guidance.

## A Benchmark Is a Sample of Questions

<!-- source: ml-51-benchmark-sample; hero: ml151-hero.png -->


Language models are compared by their benchmark scores, and the comparison rests on one sentence: *the model with the higher benchmark score is the better model.* A practitioner meets it when choosing between two hosted models, when deciding whether a fine-tuned version beats its base, and whenever a leaderboard is read from the top. If it fails, models are switched, fine-tunes shipped and claims published on the strength of which questions happened to be in the benchmark. Section 1.1 showed that a test score on 360 digits is an estimate with an error bar. This section asks what that error bar does and does not license when the test is a benchmark that somebody else wrote and the comparison is a leaderboard of models that most readers did not train.

This section first sets up a family of models whose true scores are known exactly. It then shows that each model's error bar is right while the leaderboard built from the scores is mostly noise; that comparing two models on the same questions recovers a real difference an unpaired test almost never finds; and that asking each question several ways multiplies the rows without multiplying the evidence.

### A family of models whose true scores are known


The models are small arithmetic transformers trained in this book's repository, each with 804,096 parameters, trained on generated arithmetic lines with held-out test problems. Tiny models suit the question: every answer can be checked exactly, so grading involves no judgement, and every model can be graded on thousands of questions in a minute. Each evaluation habit can therefore be compared with the quantity it is trying to estimate.

There are ten models: the *main model*; its *leaky twin*, trained on the same lines in a different order; five copies of the main model, each trained for 600 more steps under a different seed, the way several teams might each fine-tune one base model (*fine-tuning* is continuing to train an already trained model, usually briefly); and three snapshots of a separate, much shorter run of 6,000 steps.

The truth is a *pool* of 2,500 problems, 500 from each of five tasks (multiplication, order of operations, linear equations, subtraction and a probe of three-digit carries). Each model's *pool score*, the fraction of the pool it answers correctly, is treated as its true score. A benchmark is a sample of questions from this pool, as a real benchmark is a sample from the questions that could have been written. The code builds the ten-by-2,500 matrix of right-or-wrong results and prints each pool score.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import llmeval as le

names, m, tasks = le.item_matrix()     # 10 models x 2,500 questions
for name, row in zip(names, m):
    print(f"{name:8s} {row.mean():.4f}")
```

```text

cont0    0.6504
cont1    0.6636
cont2    0.6584
cont3    0.6464
cont4    0.6516
leaky    0.6752
s1_6000  0.2284
s1_4500  0.1136
s1_3000  0.0548
```

`clean` is the main model, `cont0` to `cont4` its fine-tuned copies, `leaky` the twin, and the last three rows the short run. The seven strong models are close: the main model leads the next best by 0.014, and the copies sit between 0.646 and 0.664. Extra training made every copy slightly worse than its starting point, probably because the main run had ended its learning-rate schedule near zero and the copies restarted at a constant rate. That matters here only because it leaves the copies close to one another and a little below the main model, the situation in which leaderboards are hardest to read.

### Each score's error bar is right; the ranking is not


#### The interval for one model's score

A benchmark score is a proportion. If $n$ questions are drawn independently, each is a Bernoulli trial succeeding with probability $p$, the pool score, and the observed score $\hat p$ has the binomial standard error of Section 1.1. The textbook 95% interval, the *Wald interval*, is

$$
\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}.
$$

Its *coverage* is the fraction of repeated benchmarks on which it contains the true value; a correct 95% interval covers 0.95 of the time.

Across 2,000 benchmarks of 100 questions, the ten models' intervals covered their pool scores 94.2% of the time on average, and 91.3% to 95.3% for individual models. The low end belongs to the weakest models: the Wald interval undercovers for proportions near zero, where its normal approximation is poor. At every size from 50 to 2,500 questions the average stayed between 94.2% and 95.4%. One score's error bar is, near enough, right.

#### The leaderboard built from those scores

The next block draws four benchmarks of 100 questions and prints the top four models on each.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for _ in range(4):                     # four benchmarks of 100 questions
    idx = rng.integers(0, m.shape[1], 100)
    s = m[:, idx].mean(1)
    print(" > ".join(names[i] for i in np.argsort(-s)[:4]))
```

```text
clean > cont2 > cont0 > cont3
leaky > cont0 > cont1 > cont2
clean > cont1 > cont2 > leaky
leaky > clean > cont1 > cont4
```

Four draws give four leaderboards, and on two of them the model with the highest pool score is not first. Over many draws, on 100 questions the best model came first in **50%** of benchmarks. Among the seven close models, 27% of pairs came out in the wrong order and the full order was right in 0.4% of benchmarks. Even on a resample the size of the pool, 2,500 questions drawn with replacement, the best model came first 94% of the time and the seven were in true order in 14%.

Nothing is wrong with the intervals; a leaderboard asks a different question. An interval answers "where is this model's score?" A leaderboard answers "which of these models is higher?" for every pair at once. Whether a pair comes out in the right order depends on the gap between their true scores relative to the noise in the difference of their observed scores, and for close models that noise is large compared with the gap, so the two intervals overlap almost entirely. A correct full ranking needs every pairwise comparison to land the right way at once, which is why it is so much rarer than a correct winner. Reading a ranking off overlapping intervals is reading noise. The figure below shows the three quantities against benchmark size.

![On 100 questions the models' intervals covered their true scores 94% of the time on average, and the best model came first in 50% of benchmarks.](ml151-f0-board.png){alt="Four lines against benchmark size. Interval coverage is flat near 0.95. The best model ranked first rises from about 0.44 at 50 questions to 0.95 at 2,500; the seven close models in true order stays at or below about 0.14."}

### Comparing two models on the same questions


#### The difference has its own standard error

The right question for a pair of models is about their difference. The usual mistake, one of the first that Miller's 2024 guide to error bars for language-model evaluations warns against, is to compute its standard error as if the two scores came from different questions, adding the variances: $\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_a^2 + \mathrm{SE}_b^2}$. That is the *unpaired* standard error, correct only for independent scores. Here both models answered the same questions and failed on the same hard ones: the correlation between the main model's right-or-wrong column and its fine-tuned copy's was 0.79.

A *paired* comparison uses that correlation. Let $D_i$ be $+1$ if only the first model answers question $i$ correctly, $-1$ if only the second does, and $0$ if they agree. The paired standard error is the standard error of the mean of the $D_i$, $\mathrm{sd}(D)/\sqrt{n}$. Because $\mathrm{Var}(a_i - b_i) = \mathrm{Var}(a_i) + \mathrm{Var}(b_i) - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(a_i, b_i)$, a strong positive correlation removes most of the variance the unpaired formula counts, and only questions where the models disagree contribute. *McNemar's test* (1947), met in Section 1.1, is the same idea as a test: in its exact form, a binomial test of whether the disagreements split evenly.

The block below draws one benchmark of 300 questions and compares the main model with its first fine-tuned copy both ways.

```python
a, b = m[names.index("clean")], m[names.index("cont0")]
idx = np.random.default_rng(7).integers(0, len(a), 300)
r = le.paired_difference(a[idx], b[idx])
print(f"gap {r['diff']:+.3f}  unpaired SE {r['se_unpaired']:.3f}  "
      f"paired SE {r['se_paired']:.3f}")
print(f"right only for main {r['only_a']}, only for copy {r['only_b']}"
      f", McNemar p = {r['mcnemar_p']:.4f}")
```

```text
gap +0.063  unpaired SE 0.038  paired SE 0.019
right only for main 27, only for copy 8, McNemar p = 0.0019
```

On this draw the paired standard error is half the unpaired one. The main model alone was right on 27 questions and the copy alone on 8; on all the others they agreed, and McNemar's test gives p = 0.0019.

#### How often each test finds a real gap

On the pool the main model beat its fine-tuned copy by 0.0384, a real difference by construction. Across 4,000 benchmarks of 300 questions the measured gap varied with a standard deviation of 0.0176. The paired standard error at that size is 0.0178, about right; the unpaired one is 0.0384, more than twice too large. A test that overstates the noise that much rarely declares a difference: the unpaired test found the real gap in 2.0% of benchmarks and the paired test in **60%**, as the figure below shows.

![Against the fine-tuned copy, the paired standard error matches the actual spread and the paired test detects the gap far more often than the unpaired one. Standard errors are scaled from the pool to 300 questions; 'error corr.' is the correlation between the two models' right-or-wrong columns.](ml151-f1-paired.png){alt="Two rows. Against the fine-tuned copy, the paired standard error matches the actual spread and the paired test detects the gap far more often than the unpaired one."}

Pairing is not magic. The leaky twin is 0.0136 behind the main model on the pool, so here it is known to be behind; but had the 2,500 questions been a sample from a larger population, McNemar's test on all of them would have given p = 0.12, and a 300-question benchmark put the twin ahead in 26% of draws. Section 2.2 met the opposite failure, a t-test on cross-validation folds that found differences between equal algorithms because the folds share training data and are not independent. Here the questions are independent, the paired test is the right one, and it can still correctly say "not enough evidence".

### Five prompts per question are not five questions


Language-model evaluations often ask each question several ways: several prompt templates, several samples at a nonzero temperature, several orderings of the options. The rows multiply, and an error bar computed as if each row were independent shrinks. To measure what that buys, the main model answered 600 questions, 200 each from multiplication, mixed operations and three-digit carries, five times, each with a different context of preceding lines.

The answers barely depended on the context: 42% of questions were answered right all five times and 45% wrong all five times, leaving 12% that the context could change. The five rows from one question are nearly copies of one another. The *cluster-robust standard error* accounts for this by treating each question's rows as one *cluster* and measuring variation between clusters rather than between rows; with equal rows per question, it amounts to averaging each question's rows and taking the ordinary standard error over questions. When rows within a cluster are highly correlated, extra rows add little information, and this standard error stays close to the one-row-per-question value.

A benchmark of 100 such questions has 500 rows. Treating them as independent gave a standard error of 0.0223; the actual spread of the score across 4,000 redrawn benchmarks was 0.0462, **2.1 times** larger. The cluster-robust standard error gave 0.0475, matching the spread. With 20 questions the three numbers were 0.0488, 0.1062 and 0.1048. This is Section 2.4's lesson, where rows from the same people were not new people, applied to questions: rows from the same question are not new questions.

![Of 600 questions, 42% were answered right in all five contexts and 45% in none, so the naive standard error is about half the actual spread and the cluster-robust one matches it.](ml151-f2-cluster.png){alt="Two groups of three bars. In each, the naive bar is about half the height of the other two, which match."}

A benchmark is a sample of questions, and its score inherits that sample's noise. Section 19.2 asks what happens when the questions are not a fresh sample at all, because the model saw them in training.

## A Leaked Benchmark Shows in the Set, Not in the Item

<!-- source: ml-52-contamination; hero: ml152-hero.png -->


Section 19.1 treated a benchmark as a fresh random sample of questions. That assumption is the first to fail for language models. They are trained on large scrapes of the web, and benchmarks live on the web, so a model may have seen some test questions, with their answers, during training; this is *benchmark contamination*. Nobody evaluating the model can rerun its training without them, but they can examine the model, and most contamination checks rest on one sentence: *if a model has memorised a benchmark, its likelihood on the benchmark's items will show it.* A practitioner meets the sentence when a vendor reports a contamination scan that found nothing, or when deciding whether a strong benchmark result can be trusted. If it is false, a clean scan says little, and an inflated score passes for ability.

This section manufactures contamination with a known dose and measures what it does. It first shows that leaking a benchmark into training raises the benchmark score without raising the ability the benchmark is meant to measure. It then shows that the model's likelihood cannot say which individual items leaked, and explains why. Finally it shows that the same likelihoods, compared as whole sets against fresh problems, detect the leak in every run from two insertions on.

### Leaking a benchmark on purpose, with a known dose


The model is the main arithmetic transformer of Section 19.1. The benchmark is 300 problems from its test split, 150 multiplications and 150 mixed-operation (order of operations) problems, on which the starting model gets 0.74 and 0.46 right, so there is room to improve. A *fresh set* of 600 problems of the same kinds is drawn alongside and never leaked.

The model is then trained for 600 more steps, roughly one more pass over its training data, with each benchmark line inserted into the training stream $k$ times at random positions, for $k$ from 0 to 32. The number of insertions $k$ is the *dose*. The runs with $k = 0$ matter most. They receive the same extra training with nothing leaked, so they separate the effect of the leak from the effect of training further, and five seeds of them show how much a run varies by chance.

Training reads random 64-character windows from the stream rather than whole lines in order, so an insertion is an opportunity for the model to see a line, not a guaranteed reading. At one insertion, a fair share of the leaked lines is probably never seen whole.

The code loads the two sets and prints three problems from each. The comment records the call that ran each fine-tuning job.

```python
from standarderror.ml import llmeval as le

bench, fresh = le.leak_sets()          # 300 to leak, 600 never seen
print(len(bench), "leaked, e.g.", [p.line.strip() for p in bench[:3]])
print(len(fresh), "fresh,  e.g.", [p.line.strip() for p in fresh[:3]])
# le.finetune(model, [p.line for p in bench], repeats=k, seed=s) is
# what scripts/llm_eval_runs.py ran for every k and seed below.
```

```text
300 leaked, e.g. ['368*6=2208', '82*61=5002', '617*3=1851']
600 fresh,  e.g. ['362*8=2896', '64*56=3584', '137*8=1096']
```

Each problem is a single line: the question, an equals sign and the answer. The training stream has 168,856 lines, so at two insertions the leaked lines are 0.35% of it, and even at 32 they are 5.4%. The small doses are the realistic case: a benchmark is a speck in a training corpus, seen a few times through copies, not drilled.

### The benchmark score rose; the ability did not


The next block reads the committed per-item results of every fine-tuning run and prints, for each dose and seed, accuracy on the leaked benchmark, accuracy on the fresh set, and an item-level detection score that the next section explains.

```python
c = le.contamination()                 # the committed per-item results
for key, v in c["runs"].items():
    if key != "base":
        print(f"k={v['dose']:2d} seed {v['seed']}  leaked "
              f"{v['bench']:.3f}  fresh {v['fresh']:.3f}  "
              f"item AUC {v['auc_loss']:.3f}")
```

Inserted once, the leaked problems made no measurable difference: the score was 0.540, inside the controls' range of 0.503 to 0.577. From two insertions on, every run scored above every control, and at 32 insertions the benchmark score was 0.800, +0.249 over the controls' mean. The fresh problems, meanwhile, stayed between 0.513 and 0.542 in every run, leaked or not. The benchmark reported an improvement that existed only on the benchmark.

#### What an outside evaluator would see

One comparison is less flattering to the leak. Every fine-tuned run, leaked or not, lost ground against the starting model, which scored 0.600 on the benchmark and 0.562 on the fresh set. Measured against the starting model rather than against the controls, the two-insertion run gained only +0.030 on the benchmark while losing 0.030 on fresh problems. The controls make the two effects separable; an outside evaluator comparing a model with its predecessor has no controls and sees only their net.

#### A training run is a sample too

The spread of the controls deserves attention in its own right. Five runs that differed only in their random seed scored between 0.503 and 0.577 on the same 300 problems, a range of 0.073, and the three runs at $k = 8$ spanned 0.630 to 0.647. At one insertion, a comparison of one contaminated run with one clean run would have been a coin toss. This is Section 19.1's lesson in the training direction: there a benchmark was a sample of questions, here a training run is a sample of possible runs.

Fresh-problem accuracy stays between 0.51 and 0.55 at every dose. Leaked-problem accuracy is 0.50 to 0.58 at dose 0 and rises above the controls from dose 2."}

### Item by item, the likelihood cannot say which leaked


#### Membership inference by loss

The usual detector asks whether the model finds a particular item suspiciously likely. In privacy research this is *membership inference*: deciding, from a model's behaviour, whether a given example was in its training data. Its simplest version, analysed by Yeom and colleagues in 2018, thresholds the *loss*, here the negative log-likelihood the model assigns to the correct answer, on the principle that a model's loss on its training examples tends to be lower than on others. Items below the threshold are flagged as members.

Its quality as a detector is summarised by the *AUC*, the probability that a randomly chosen leaked item is ranked as more suspicious than a randomly chosen fresh one; 0.5 is a coin and 1 is perfect separation. A second summary is the share of leaked items caught when the threshold is set so that a fixed share of fresh items, the *false-flag rate*, is flagged.

Ranking the 900 problems by the contaminated model's log-likelihood of the right answer separated leaked from fresh with an AUC of 0.621 at $k = 32$, and 0.545 at $k = 2$. In the output above the controls, where nothing leaked, sit near 0.5, as they should. Set to flag 5% of the fresh problems, the detector flagged 3.3% of the leaked ones at $k = 32$: no better than flagging at random.

#### Why the trace is lost among the items

The leak did leave a trace. The difficulty is that problems differ from one another in how likely their answers are by far more than the leak changed them. At $k = 32$ the per-problem log-likelihoods had a standard deviation of 1.22, while the leak raised the leaked set's average by 0.51 relative to the fresh set. An easy multiplication's answer is likely whether or not it leaked, and a hard mixed-operation answer is unlikely whether or not it leaked. A threshold on the raw likelihood therefore mostly sorts problems by difficulty, and the leak shifts the leaked problems, on average, only a fraction of the way across that spread.

#### Comparing with a reference model

Carlini and colleagues' remedy, in 2022, was to remove the item-to-item variation by comparing each item's likelihood under the model with its likelihood under *reference models* that did not see it. An item that is easy for every model stops looking suspicious; what remains is how much more likely the tested model finds it than a comparable model would. Here the uncontaminated starting model served as the reference, and the problems whose likelihood rose most were flagged. The AUC was 0.745 at $k = 32$, and at the same 5% false-flag rate the detector caught 24% of the leaked problems.

That is better, but still far from naming the leaked items, because extra training moves each problem's likelihood by its own amount whether or not the problem leaked. Even in the controls the change from the starting model had a standard deviation of 0.65 per problem, against 0.67 at $k = 32$. And a reference model of the same lineage that is known not to have seen the benchmark is exactly what an outside evaluator does not have. Methods built for large models, such as the *Min-K% probability* of Shi and colleagues, which scores an item by the average log-probability of its least likely tokens, refine the per-item score but face the same item-to-item variation.

![At 32 insertions the per-item AUC was 0.621 with the model alone and 0.745 with the uncontaminated model as a reference.](ml152-f1-detect.png){alt="Two lines against dose, both starting at 0.5. The model-alone line rises slowly; the reference line rises faster."}

### As a set, the leak is visible


The question an evaluator usually needs answered is not "which items leaked?" but "is this benchmark's score inflated?", and that question has much more data behind it. It can be answered by comparing the benchmark as a whole with a fresh set from the same source: the same generator and tasks, with problems that cannot have leaked because they were written afterwards or never published.

The comparison here is on the mean log-likelihood per problem, leaked set minus fresh set, scaled by its standard error to give a *Welch t-statistic*,

$$
t = \frac{\bar x_{\text{leaked}} - \bar x_{\text{fresh}}}{\sqrt{s_{\text{leaked}}^2/n_{\text{leaked}} + s_{\text{fresh}}^2/n_{\text{fresh}}}},
$$

where $\bar x$, $s$ and $n$ are each set's mean, standard deviation and size. Welch's form does not assume the two sets have equal variances. The mechanism that defeated the item-level detector works in this test's favour. The per-item spread is large, but the standard error of a mean over hundreds of items is that spread divided by the square root of the number of items, so a shift that is small relative to one item's variation can be large relative to the uncertainty in a set's average.

The five controls gave t-statistics between -0.25 and 0.71. Every contaminated run from $k = 2$ on gave more than 2, outside the controls' range though not far outside it at $k = 2$ and 4 (2.22 and 2.31), and at $k = 32$ it was 7.4. Accuracy told the same story with more noise: the gap between the leaked and fresh sets was -0.018 to +0.060 in the controls and +0.268 on average at $k = 32$. Neither test says which problems leaked. Both say that the score on this benchmark is not the score on the task. The table below lists every run.

![k = 0 rows are the controls: the same extra training, with nothing leaked. The five k = 0 rows have t between -0.3 and 0.8; rows from k = 2 on have t above 2.](ml152-f2-table.png){alt="A table of every fine-tuning run. The five k = 0 rows have t between -0.3 and 0.8; rows from k = 2 on have t above 2."}

This is what Zhang and colleagues did for grade-school arithmetic in 2024. They wrote new problems in the style of a popular benchmark, GSM8k, and compared models' accuracy on the two sets, finding clear drops for some model families and little for others. The method needs no access to training data and no reference model, only the discipline to keep some problems unpublished, which Section 3.3 asked of test sets in general when it showed that a test set answers honestly only the first time it is used.

A leaked benchmark inflates the score, and so, as Section 19.3 shows, can something much more ordinary: the text placed in front of the question.

## The Prompt Is Part of the Score

<!-- source: ml-53-prompt-format; hero: ml153-hero.png -->


Sections 19.1 and 19.2 asked which questions a benchmark contains. This section asks how they are put to the model. A language model never sees a question alone. It sees a window of text that ends with the question: a system prompt, instructions, worked examples, earlier turns of a conversation. A benchmark fixes one such window per question, reports the result as the model's score, and a leaderboard compares models under that one window. The comparison assumes the window is a neutral container, which is the sentence this section tests: *a benchmark score measures the model.* A practitioner relies on it whenever they quote a published score, compare two models under their own evaluation prompt, or tune a prompt and report the result. If it is false, a score is partly a measurement of the prompt, a ranking can depend on which prompt was used, and a tuned prompt can report its own luck.

This section first measures how much the context in front of a problem moves one model's score, then shows that it moves the gap between two models. Next it tests, with examples whose answers are wrong, whether the models read the examples at all. Finally it imitates trying several prompts and keeping the best, and shows when that choice holds up on new problems.

### Six ways to ask the same question


The models are two of the arithmetic transformers of Section 19.1: the main model and its leaky twin, trained on the same lines in a different order. Their answers are numbers that can be checked exactly, and the context in front of a problem is easy to vary, because it is simply other lines of the same kind of text.

Every earlier number for these models, including those in Sections 19.1 and 19.2, was measured with twelve random training lines in front of each problem, drawn afresh per problem. Here the same 200 problems per task are asked in six kinds of context:

- twelve random training lines (the standard context);
- nothing at all;
- two random lines;
- twelve lines of the same task;
- twelve lines of a different task;
- twelve lines of the same task with the last digit of every answer changed, the arithmetic version of *few-shot examples* (worked examples placed in the prompt) with wrong labels.

Each kind of context is drawn three times, so its spread from one draw to the next can be set against the differences between kinds. A difference between two kinds of context means something only if it is larger than the variation among draws of the same kind.

The code loads the main model, takes 200 multiplication problems, and scores them under four of the six kinds. For each kind it prints the accuracy and the last few characters of the first problem's context, so the reader can see what the model was shown.

```python

    ctx = le.contexts(kind, "mul", len(probs), seed=100)
```

#### How much of the score is context

The main model multiplied correctly 0.735 of the time with twelve random lines in front and 0.500 with nothing: about a third of its score was the context. An empty context cost it 0.035 to 0.137 on the other three tasks too.

One reading is positional: the model was trained on a packed stream of lines, so every problem it saw in training had other lines in front of it. For multiplication the reading fits: two random lines recovered almost all of the score (0.725, again an average over three draws, not the code's single twelve-line draw), as if what mattered was having something in front of the problem. But on mixed operations and linear equations two lines did slightly worse than none, so position is not the whole story.

Across three draws of the same kind of context no score moved by more than 0.060. The large differences between kinds, such as the empty context's, are far outside that range and are real effects of the kind of context. The small ones are not distinguishable from the draw-to-draw variation. The table below gives every model, task and context.

### The gap between two models moves with the context


A shift that moved every model by the same amount would leave a leaderboard's rankings and gaps intact. This one did not. On three-digit carries the leaky twin beat the main model by 0.127 under the standard twelve lines and by 0.012 under two: a clear win and a tie, from the same two models on the same problems. On multiplication the main model's lead ran from 0.050 to 0.168 across contexts, and on mixed operations from 0.002 to 0.055. The figure below shows each task's gap under each of the six contexts.

![On three-digit carries the twin led by 0.127 with twelve lines of context and by 0.012 with two.](ml153-f0-gaps.png){alt="Four rows of six dots. Three-digit carries span -0.13 to -0.01; multiplication 0.05 to 0.17; order of operations 0.00 to 0.06; linear equations 0.02 to 0.13."}

The most extreme case is the task that separates the two models by design. The twin learned reversed sums by copying from the line above, which its training order made possible, so it scored 0.005 on them after twelve lines of context and 0.435 with none, while the main model scored 0.995 or 1.0 under every context. Whether the twin "can do" reversed sums depends entirely on how it is asked.

Sclar and colleagues found the same thing at scale in 2024. Changing only the formatting of a prompt (separators, spacing, casing) moved one model's few-shot accuracy on a task by up to 76 points, and which formats worked well correlated only weakly from one model to another. They argued that this makes a comparison of models under one fixed format unreliable, since the format may favour one model over the other. Their remedy, reporting a range over plausible formats rather than a single point, is the one the numbers in this section support.

### Examples with wrong answers changed nothing


If the context mattered because the model read the examples and used their answers, examples with wrong answers should hurt. They did not. Replacing the last digit of every example answer changed no model's score on any task by more than 0.005 relative to correct examples of the same task. What the examples taught was not their answers.

Which task the examples came from did matter, for the twin. On three-digit carries it scored 0.412 after lines of the same task and 0.473 after lines of a different one, a difference outside the spread of either kind's draws. So the context acted on the twin through what kind of lines it contained, not through whether their answers were right.

This is a finding about these models, which are far too small to learn from examples in context, and it should not be read as a finding about large ones. It does echo the result of Min and colleagues in 2022 for larger models: on many classification tasks, randomly reassigning the labels of the demonstrations barely hurt, and the format and the distribution of the inputs mattered more than whether the labels were right. Either way, the fact that a context changes a score is not evidence that the model understood the context.

### Choosing the prompt on the items you report


#### Selecting the best of sixteen prompts

In practice the window is chosen, not drawn. Zhao and colleagues showed in 2021 that a large model's few-shot accuracy swung widely with which examples were in the prompt and in what order, and the natural response is to write several prompts, try them, and keep the best. To imitate that, sixteen fixed prompts were built. Each is twelve training lines shared by every problem, the way a *prompt template* puts the same worked examples in front of every question. Each prompt was scored on one set of 150 multiplication problems, set A, and on a disjoint second set, set B. The code shows two of the sixteen.

```python
import numpy as np
sets = le.items("test", "mul", 150), le.items("test", "mul", 150, skip=150)
pool = [p.line for p in le._train_by_task()["*"]]
for seed in (500, 506):          # two of the sixteen fixed prompts
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    prompt = "".join(pool[i] for i in rng.integers(0, len(pool), 12))
    a, b = (le.ok(main, s, fills=[prompt] * 150).mean() for s in sets)
    print(f"prompt {seed}: set A {a:.3f}, set B {b:.3f}")
```

```text

prompt 506: set A 0.727, set B 0.693
```

The prompt that looks better on set A, 0.753 against 0.727, is slightly worse on set B. Across all sixteen, for the main model on multiplication, the prompts scored between 0.707 and 0.793 on set A. The best one, at 0.793, scored 0.667 on set B, below the 0.680 average of all sixteen there. The correlation between the prompts' scores on the two sets was -0.02: a prompt's score on one set said nothing about its score on the other. The search had found the prompt that suited set A's particular problems.

This is the result of Section 4.1, with prompts in place of hyperparameters. When several candidates are compared by noisy estimates, the one with the highest estimate is disproportionately likely to be one whose noise happened to be favourable, so its estimate is biased upward, and the bias is largest when the true differences between candidates are small compared with the noise. There the best cross-validated score of a hyperparameter search reported the search's best luck; the best prompt score does the same.

#### When the choice holds

It is not always luck. For the twin on three-digit carries the same sixteen prompts ranged from 0.287 to 0.487, the two sets agreed (correlation +0.92), and the best prompt on set A was also the best on set B. Here the prompts really differed in their effect, and the differences were large compared with the noise. Presumably some prompts gave the twin more to copy from; that was not checked. Without the second set there is no way to tell these two situations apart, since in both the search returns a best prompt with a high score.

The choice of prompt can also decide a comparison. On mixed operations the twin beat the main model under 3 of the sixteen prompts on set A and under none on set B. The true order is probably the main model first, and a comparison reported under one of those three prompts, chosen because it looked good on set A, would have stated the opposite. ## Multiple Choice Grades the Wrong Answers You Offered

<!-- source: ml-54-multiple-choice; hero: ml154-hero.png -->


Section 19.3 held the scoring rule fixed and varied the text in front of the question. This section holds the question and its context fixed and varies the rule. There are two common ways to score a language model on a question with one right answer. The first asks for the answer: the model generates a continuation, and the item counts as right if the continuation matches. The second offers options: it computes how likely the model finds each candidate answer, and counts the item right if the true answer is the likeliest. The second is cheap, needs no parsing of free text, and is how many multiple-choice benchmarks are commonly scored. It is usually treated as the same measurement in a more convenient form, which is the sentence this section tests: *scoring a model by which answer option it finds likeliest measures the same thing as asking it for the answer.* A practitioner relies on it whenever a multiple-choice score is quoted as evidence that a model can do a task, or used to rank models that will be deployed to generate. If it is false, the score depends on the wrong answers the benchmark happened to offer, and a model can be credited with an ability it does not have.

This section scores the same models on the same problems both ways. It first shows models that cannot generate a single right answer to a set of problems but pick the right answer from four options almost every time, and explains how. It then shows that the multiple-choice score is set by the kind of wrong options offered, and that the choice of options can erase a real difference between two models. Finally it examines length normalisation, the usual correction for options of different lengths.

### Two scoring rules for the same question


#### Generation and likelihood scoring

Under *generation scoring* (also called *exact match*), the model is given the question and produces its answer one character at a time until it ends the line. The item is right if the produced string equals the true answer.

Under *likelihood scoring*, the model produces nothing. For each option $a$, made of characters $a_1, \dots, a_L$, it computes the log-likelihood of that option following the question $q$,

$$
\log P(a \mid q) = \sum_{t=1}^{L} \log P(a_t \mid q, a_1, \dots, a_{t-1}),
$$

and the item is right if the true answer has the largest log-likelihood. The wrong options are called *distractors*. Evaluation harnesses such as the widely used lm-evaluation-harness of Gao and colleagues report this accuracy both as it stands and with *length normalisation*, where each option's log-likelihood is divided by its length in characters or bytes before the comparison.

#### The models and the length probe

The models are again the arithmetic transformers of Section 19.1 (four blocks, 804,096 parameters), whose training data leave out some kinds of problem as held-out probes. Because every answer can be checked exactly, each scoring rule can be compared with the truth rather than with another score. Three are used here: the main model; its leaky twin, trained on the same data unshuffled; and the 6,000-step snapshot of the separate, shorter run, which is much weaker. Each problem has one context of twelve random training lines in front of it, shared by both scoring rules.

The first test set is the *length probe*: four-digit sums, whereas the models saw operands of up to three digits in training. The code generates the main model's answers to 200 of them and prints four, then the exact-match accuracy and the share of answers shorter than the truth.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import llmeval as le
from standarderror.schoolmath import arithmetic as ar
from standarderror.schoolmath import model as sm

model = sm.load()                      # the main arithmetic model
sums = le.items("probe_length", "add", 200)     # four-digit operands
fills = ar.fillers(len(sums), seed=9)  # one fresh context per problem
rows = ar.graded(model, sums, filler=fills)
for r in rows[:4]:
    print(f"{r['prompt']:12s} want {r['want']:6s} got {r['got']}")
print("exact:", np.mean([r["ok"] for r in rows]),
      " shorter than the answer:",
      np.mean([len(r["got"]) < len(r["want"]) for r in rows]))
```

```text
2216+1490=   want 3706   got 769
2669+5296=   want 7965   got 795
8625+8412=   want 17037  got 1603
exact: 0.0  shorter than the answer: 0.995
```

None of the three models generated a single right answer to the 200 four-digit sums. The main model's failures have a pattern: 99.5% of its answers were shorter than the truth, typically a truncated version of roughly the right number. The first digit was right in 90% of them, the first two in 61%, and 25.5% were an exact prefix of the answer. The model starts four-digit answers well and stops too soon.

### Picking an answer the model cannot produce


Offer the main model the same sums as four options, the right answer and three random numbers with as many digits, and it picks the right one 0.955 of the time. Its twin scores 0.980, and the short run, which generates far fewer answers correctly than either (0.205 on multiplication), scores 0.790. Nothing about the models changed between the two scoring rules, only the question asked of them. The figure below compares the rules across four tasks.

![On four-digit sums the main model generated a correct answer 0.00 of the time, but picked it from random options 0.955, from near misses 0.395 and from options of different lengths 0.315.](ml154-f0-scores.png){alt="Grouped bars for four tasks. On four-digit sums generation is zero, multiple choice against random numbers is near one, against near misses about 0.4, against different lengths about 0.3."}

#### Why a truncating model wins against random options

The reason is visible in the generations. A model that writes a truncated version of nearly the right number puts most of its probability on strings that start with the right digits. An option's log-likelihood is the sum of per-character terms in the formula above, and the first characters, where the model is most often right, weigh heavily in the comparison. A random number of the same length almost never shares the true answer's leading digits, so each of its early characters is unlikely and its total log-likelihood is far below the true answer's. The true answer wins without the model ever being able to finish it.

Multiple choice therefore measures whether the model prefers the right answer to the wrong ones offered. That is a real ability, closer to recognition than to production, and it is a different one from generating the answer.

#### The distractors set the score

The next block takes the first sum and offers it with three kinds of distractor: random numbers of the same length; *near misses*, which differ from the answer by a one-digit slip; and options of different lengths, made by dropping, appending or prepending a digit. It prints which option the model finds likeliest.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
p = sums[0]
for kind in ("random", "near", "length"):
    options = [p.answer] + le.distractors(p.answer, kind, rng)
    lp = le.answer_logp(model, [p.prompt] * 4, options, [fills[0]] * 4)
    pick = options[int(np.argmax(lp))]
    print(f"{kind:6s} options {options}  picked {pick}")
```

```text
random options ['10411', '19837', '33545', '85381']  picked 10411
near   options ['10411', '10410', '10421', '19411']  picked 10411
length options ['10411', '1041', '110411', '910411']  picked 1041
```

Against random options and near misses the model picks 10411 for this sum; against options of different lengths it picks 1041, the truncated answer it would have generated.

Over all 200 sums, the score is set by the wrong answers. Against one-digit slips, the errors a person or a model actually makes, the main model's score fell to 0.395. Near misses share most of the answer's digits, so the leading digits no longer decide the comparison. Against options that differ in length the score fell to 0.315, barely above the 0.25 of guessing among four options, and in 68% of items the option the model preferred was shorter than the answer: the same truncation it generates. That gives three numbers anyone could publish, 0.955, 0.395 and 0.315, for one model on one set of questions.

### Which model is better depends on the options


Within the training lengths the gap between the two scoring rules is smaller, but still not a constant that cancels when models are compared. On multiplication the main model generated 0.755 of answers correctly and its leaky twin 0.650, a clear lead. Against near-miss options the twin scored 0.895 and the main model 0.880: a lead of 0.105, more than two standard errors, became a difference well inside one. On problems mixing addition and multiplication, generation put the two models level (0.410 and 0.400) and multiple choice put the twin ahead by 0.050, about one standard error. On three-digit carries both rules agreed that the twin was better. Neither rule's ranking is free of noise on 200 problems; what is not noise is the size of the multiplication gap under one rule and its absence under the other.

Against random distractors every model scored 0.99 or more on multiplication and mixed operations (the short run scored 0.97 on three-digit carries), including the short run, which generates 0.205 of multiplications correctly. A benchmark scored that way cannot tell the three models apart. The table below gives every model, task and rule.

![Each block of three rows is one task; MC is multiple choice among four options. Generation ranges from 0 to 0.755; multiple choice against random numbers is 0.79 or higher everywhere.](ml154-f1-table.png){alt="A twelve-row table. Generation ranges from 0 to 0.755; multiple choice against random numbers is 0.79 or higher everywhere."}

Schaeffer and colleagues made a related point about apparently "emergent" abilities of large models: a discontinuous metric such as exact match, which gives no credit for a nearly right answer, can make smooth improvement look sudden. Here the arrow runs the other way: a lenient metric made a large real gap look like none. Section 12.2 found the same pattern for classifiers, where the model with the best AUC was never the cheapest once a miss cost ten times a false alarm. A summary that does not match the use can rank models in a different order from the decision they serve.

### Length normalisation did nothing here


The usual worry about likelihood scoring is length. Because an option's log-likelihood is a sum of one negative term per character, a longer option accumulates more terms and tends to look less likely regardless of whether it is right. Harnesses therefore offer a per-character or per-byte average to correct for it.

Here the correction moved no score by more than 0.040, and every score but one by 0.010 or less. Most option sets had a single length, so there was nothing to correct. In the length-varied set, a likely reason is that each option is scored together with its closing newline, which charges a short option for stopping early; that was not tested separately.

That is a result about these options, not a general reassurance. Benchmarks whose options are sentences of different lengths are exactly where the two versions are known to disagree, which is why harnesses report both. The point that does generalise is that the choice between them is one more scoring decision, made before any model is run, on which the headline number depends.

The scoring rules in this section still compare an answer with a known truth. Section 19.5 removes the truth, and asks whether a model can grade other models' answers in its place.

## Every Judge Ranked Itself First

<!-- source: ml-55-judge; hero: ml155-hero.png -->


Sections 19.1 to 19.4 graded every answer against a known truth, which arithmetic supplies for free. Most of what language models are now asked to produce does not: summaries, explanations, code that should be readable. Checking such outputs by hand is slow, and the common substitute is to ask a model to grade them, either one answer at a time or by choosing the better of two. This is the *LLM-as-a-judge* approach, and it rests on the sentence this section tests: *a model can grade other models' answers in place of people.* A practitioner relies on it whenever an automated grader decides which prompt, fine-tune or vendor wins. If it fails, the grader's ranking reflects the grader as much as the contestants, and a model can be promoted because it resembles the model that judged it.

Zheng and colleagues reported in 2023 that strong judges agreed with human preferences about as often as humans agreed with one another. They also documented biases for the position of an answer and for its length, and saw some signs of judges favouring their own answers that their data were too limited to confirm. This section measures that last bias with judges whose verdicts can be checked exactly. It first builds the simplest possible judge and shows that every judge ranked its own answers first. It then explains why a model's own mistakes look right to it, and what the judges nonetheless did well. Next it shows how a small set of human labels, used through prediction-powered inference, removes the bias, and when that repair costs precision. A last subsection draws together what the chapter has measured.

### A judge in its simplest form


The contestants are the three arithmetic models of Section 19.4: the main model, its leaky twin, and the much weaker short run. Each answers 300 problems on each of three tasks (three-digit carries, multiplication, and problems mixing addition and multiplication), generating each answer *greedily*, that is, by taking the most likely next character at every step.

Each model then acts as a judge. It reads every answer, its own and the other two models', after the question, in a context different from the one the answer was written in, and reports its average log-probability per character. A high score means "this looks right to me". A real judge is prompted for a verdict instead of read for a likelihood, but its verdict plausibly draws on the same sense of what is likely. Panickssery and colleagues found that large models' preference for their own outputs rose with their ability to recognise them, though they did not test a likelihood mechanism.

To turn a score into a verdict, each judge needs a pass threshold. To give the judge every advantage, the threshold is set after the fact, so that over all 900 answers on a task it passes as many as are actually right, up to ties in its scores. The judge's overall pass rate is therefore correct by construction. Any bias that survives this is not a matter of the threshold being in the wrong place; it is a matter of which answers the judge passes.

The code shows the mechanics on six three-digit carries. The short run answers them; then the short run and the main model each judge those answers, and the per-character scores are printed beside the true answers.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import llmeval as le
from standarderror.schoolmath import arithmetic as ar
from standarderror.schoolmath import model as sm

main, short = sm.load(), sm.load_member("s1_6000")
sums = le.items("probe_carry", "add", 300)[:6]    # three-digit carries
gen = ar.fillers(6, seed=9)                       # contexts to answer in
ctx = ar.fillers(6, seed=21)                      # contexts to judge in
a = [r["got"] for r in ar.graded(short, sums, filler=gen)]
for judge, name in ((short, "short"), (main, "main")):
    lp = le.answer_logp(judge, [p.prompt for p in sums], a, ctx)
    print(f"{name:5s} judges short's answers:",
          np.round(lp / [len(x) + 1 for x in a], 2))
print("right answers:", [p.answer for p in sums])
print("short wrote:  ", a)
```

```text

main  judges short's answers: [-0.   -4.19 -0.87 -4.7  -0.15 -4.25]
right answers: ['1712', '1220', '907', '972', '1314', '721']
short wrote:   ['1692', '1191', '897', '960', '1294', '722']
```

All six of the short run's answers are wrong. Judging them itself, the short run gives several of them scores close to zero, the highest possible, such as -0.02 and -0.01. The main model gives most of the same answers far lower scores, down to -4.7. The same wrong answers look plausible to the model that wrote them and implausible to a stronger one.

### Nine judges, nine self-rankings


Across the full experiment, on every task each of the three judges passed a higher share of its own answers than of either other model's: **9 of 9** judge-and-task pairs ranked the judge first.

For the main model and its twin, which are close in ability, that self-preference reversed the true order whenever the judge was the weaker of the two on a task. On three-digit carries the main model is right 0.357 of the time and the twin 0.513. Judging, the main model passed 0.477 of its own answers and 0.353 of the twin's: it raised its own score, lowered the twin's, and put itself first.

The short run is the extreme case. Its answers to three-digit carries were right 1.3% of the time, and judging them itself it passed **73%**. A model that is the worst on every task by a wide margin was, in its own judgement, the best on every task. The figure below shows all nine judge-and-task pairs.

### A model's own mistakes look right to it


#### The mechanism

The likely mechanism is simple. A greedy answer is built from the characters the model finds most likely at each step, so the same model, asked how likely that answer is, tends to find it likely, whether it is right or wrong. Because the judging context differs from the answering one, this is an expectation rather than an identity, and the scores bear it out. On problems mixing addition and multiplication the main model passed 29% of its own wrong answers and 6% of the twin's. On three-digit carries its average score for its own *wrong* answers, -0.098, was higher than its average score for the twin's *right* ones, -0.353.

#### What the judges did well

What the judges did well deserves an equally precise statement. Within any one model's answers, the main model and the twin as judges separated right answers from wrong ones with AUCs from 0.59 on three-digit carries to 0.99 on multiplication. The AUC, as in Section 12.2, is the probability that a randomly chosen right answer receives a higher score than a randomly chosen wrong one. So the bias was not mainly an inability to tell right from wrong. Each judge ranked answers sensibly within a model but put its own answers on a higher scale, and a single threshold applied across models then favours the judge.

Judging two models that were neither of them, the main model and the twin ordered the remaining pair correctly in all six comparisons. That was the easy case: each such pair set a strong model against the short run, a gap of 0.31 to 0.53 in true accuracy. The only close comparison made by a judge not involved in it was the short run judging the main model against the twin, and it got their order wrong on three-digit carries, passing 0.100 of the main model's answers and 0.053 of the twin's against true rates of 0.357 and 0.513.

The usual advice is to use a judge from a different model family. These three models share their training data, so they cannot test that advice. What they do show is that a judge's verdict on itself is the least trustworthy thing it produces, and that a judge outside the contest is not automatically a good one. The table below gives each judge's verdicts on each model.

### Correcting a judge with forty labels


#### Prediction-powered inference

The repair that does not depend on finding an unrelated judge is a small set of answers checked by people. *Prediction-powered inference* (PPI), introduced by Angelopoulos and colleagues in 2023, uses the judge on every answer and the labels on a few. Let $f_i$ be the judge's verdict on answer $i$ (1 for pass, 0 for fail) over all $N$ answers, and let $Y_j$ be the true label on a random subset of $n$ answers. The estimate of the true accuracy is the judge's pass rate plus the judge's average error on the labelled answers,

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{PP}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \;+\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(Y_j - f_j\right).
$$

The second term, the *rectifier*, estimates how far the judge's pass rate is from the truth, and subtracting the bias in this way makes the estimate unbiased whatever the judge does. Its variance is roughly $\mathrm{Var}(f)/N + \mathrm{Var}(Y - f)/n$. When the judge agrees with the labels, $Y - f$ is mostly zero, the second variance is small, and the judge's large sample keeps the interval narrow. When the judge disagrees with the labels often, $Y - f$ can vary more than $Y$ itself, and the interval can be wider than one computed from the labels alone.

The *tuned* version, from the PPI++ paper of Angelopoulos, Duchi and Zrnic, multiplies the judge's terms by a weight $\lambda$ estimated from the labels. A weight of one gives plain PPI; a weight of zero ignores the judge and uses the labels alone. Estimating $\lambda$ lets the method lean on the judge only as far as the judge deserves.

#### Coverage and width

The block below runs the study for one case: the main model judging its twin on three-digit carries, with 40 labelled answers drawn at random 1,000 times. For four estimators it prints the bias, the width of the nominal 95% interval, and its coverage: the judge alone, the 40 labels alone, plain PPI, and tuned PPI.

```python
v = le.judged(sc, "probe_carry/add", "clean", ["clean", "leaky"])
d = sc["probe_carry/add/clean/leaky"]     # the main model judges the twin
r = le.ppi_study(np.array(d["score"]) > v["threshold"],

print("true accuracy", round(r["target"], 3))
for k in ("judge", "labels", "ppi", "tuned"):
```

### What the chapter measured


This chapter took the habits of the earlier parts of this book to models somebody else built and benchmarks somebody else wrote. A benchmark score was a sample of questions, and a question asked five ways was still close to one question (Section 19.1). Leaked answers raised the score on the leaked items while a loss-based detector barely noticed (Section 19.2). The prompt was part of the measurement, and the gap between two models depended on it (Section 19.3). Multiple choice measured the distractors as much as the model (Section 19.4). And a model asked to grade others graded itself highest (Section 19.5).

None of these needed a large model to show. Each is an older problem in new clothes: sampling error, leakage, a choice made on the test set, a metric that does not match the use, and labels that come from the system being judged. The tools that answered them in earlier parts answer them here: paired comparisons, fresh data, held-out selection, evaluation at the point of use, and a small random sample checked by people. Chapter 20 follows a model past its evaluation, into the period after it ships, when the outcomes that would grade it are not yet in.

## In practice

Every result in this chapter comes down to a choice made before any model is run: which questions, which prompt, which scoring rule, which grader. Each of those choices belongs in the report alongside the number, and each can be checked with data the evaluator controls.

### Comparing models on a benchmark (Section 19.1)

- **Report a comparison as a difference with a paired interval.** Export per-question results for both models on the same questions and report the mean difference with its paired standard error, not two headline scores with separate error bars.
- **Count the disagreements.** The questions only one model got right, in each direction, carry all the evidence; McNemar's exact test is the test on them.
- **Treat leaderboard positions inside each other's intervals as ties.** A correct interval for each score says nothing about the correctness of the ranks.
- **Cluster by question.** When a question is asked under several prompts, samples or option orders, compute the standard error from per-question averages or with a cluster-robust estimator. Do the same for families of near-duplicate questions written from one template.

### Testing for contamination (Section 19.2)

- **Keep a fresh set.** For any benchmark you rely on, write or generate problems in its style that have never been published, and hold them back. They are the only contamination test that needs nothing from the model's developer.
- **Compare whole sets, not items.** Test whether the benchmark's mean score or mean log-likelihood differs from the fresh set's, with a standard error, rather than scanning items for suspicious ones.
- **Do not read a clean per-item scan as evidence that nothing leaked.** At two insertions the model-alone detector in Section 19.2 scored an AUC of 0.545, close to chance, although the benchmark score was already above every control's.
- **Check that the fresh set matches.** Confirm on a model that cannot have seen either set that the fresh problems are as hard as the benchmark's; otherwise a difference in difficulty will masquerade as contamination, or hide it.

### Choosing and reporting the prompt (Section 19.3)

- **Report the prompt with the score.** A score without its context, examples and format is not reproducible, and on these models the context alone moved a score by about a third.
- **Report a range over reasonable prompts.** Score under several variants a colleague would call equivalent, and report the spread alongside the headline number.
- **Compare models under the same prompts, and check the ranking under each.** If the ranking flips between equivalent prompts, the comparison is a comparison of prompts.
- **Choose a prompt on items you will not report.** Split the problems; select on one set and report on the other. The correlation of prompts' scores across the two sets says whether the selection found something real.
- **Do not read a context effect as understanding.** A score that changes with the examples does not show that the model used their content.

### Choosing the scoring rule (Section 19.4)

- **Treat a multiple-choice score as accuracy against those particular distractors.** State what the distractors are, and how they were made, whenever the number is reported.
- **Report generation where the use is generation.** If the model will be asked to produce answers, measure it producing answers; a high multiple-choice score does not show it can.
- **Test the distractors.** Rescore a sample of items with plausible near-miss options. A large drop means the original score was measuring how easy the wrong answers were to reject.
- **Check rankings under both rules.** If two models' order changes between generation and multiple choice, the comparison depends on the rule, and the rule should match the use.
- **State the normalisation.** Report whether option log-likelihoods were divided by length, and in what units.

### Using a model as a judge (Section 19.5)

- **Do not let a model grade itself or a close relative.** Self-preference survived a threshold calibrated on the truth in every case measured in Section 19.5.
- **Check any judge against a random sample graded by people,** and compute the judge's pass rate and the people's separately for each system being compared. A gap that differs between systems means the judge's ranking is partly a ranking of resemblance to the judge.
- **Use the labelled sample to correct the estimate, not only to decide whether to trust the judge.** Report the prediction-powered estimate, with its interval, alongside the judge's raw pass rate.
- **Prefer the tuned form of PPI.** It uses the judge only as far as the labels support; with a weak judge, its interval was no wider than the labels' alone, where plain PPI's was.
- **Distrust a narrow interval from a judge alone.** Its width reflects the number of judged answers, not the judge's error.

## Limits of the evidence

The same broad limitation runs through every section: the models are tiny, trained on the same arithmetic lines, and graded by exact match. That is what made the truth available, and it is also why the sizes of the effects here should not be carried over to large models from different developers. The specific limits of each section follow.

### A benchmark as a sample (Section 19.1)

- **A pool, not a population.** The truth there is a fixed pool of 2,500 problems from five tasks. A real benchmark is a sample from a population of questions nobody has written down, and its questions are often chosen rather than drawn, which adds a bias that no error bar describes.
- **Exact answers.** Grading was by exact match, so every error bar in Section 19.1 is pure question sampling. Graders that disagree with one another add their own variance.
- **Models of one family.** All ten models were trained on the same lines, and six are the main model or copies of it, so their errors are more correlated than those of models from different developers. The more correlated the errors, the more pairing gains; for unrelated models the gain is smaller, but the paired test is still the right one.
- **Questions drawn independently.** Benchmarks often contain families of near-duplicate questions written from one template, which cluster in the same way as the repeated prompts there.

### Contamination (Section 19.2)

- **Verbatim contamination.** Every leaked line was the exact problem and answer. Real contamination includes paraphrases, translations and solutions without the question, which can raise scores while leaving even less trace in the likelihood of the exact item.
- **A small model and a short exposure.** A large model memorises single examples more readily, so its per-item signal may be stronger, and a benchmark seen across many training epochs leaves more trace than one inserted into a single extra pass.
- **Arithmetic answers.** The answers are a few characters long, which gives a per-item likelihood little to work with. Longer answers carry more evidence per item, which helps per-item detectors.
- **A fresh set that matches.** The set-level test is only as good as the match between the fresh problems and the benchmark. Before any training, the leaked set there was already slightly easier than the fresh set, which the controls absorb; an evaluator without controls needs the fresh set to be drawn the way the benchmark was.

### Prompts (Section 19.3)

- **Context, not instructions.** These models cannot follow instructions, so the prompt variations were positions and examples. For instruction-following models, wording and format are larger levers, and the variations multiply.
- **Two models, one family.** The two models share their training data and architecture. Models from different families respond to the same prompt change differently, which makes ranking changes more likely, not less.
- **Small sets.** The prompt search used 150 problems per set, so a difference of 0.04 between two prompts' scores is about one standard error of either score, and less than two standard errors of the paired difference. That is the condition under which selection does the most damage, and also the usual one.
- **Greedy decoding.** The models answered with their most likely output. Sampling with a temperature adds another source of run-to-run variation, which was not measured.

### Scoring rules (Section 19.4)

- **Arithmetic, not language.** Real multiple-choice benchmarks such as MMLU (Hendrycks and colleagues, 2021) have options written by people, and are often scored by the letter the model picks rather than by the likelihood of the answer text. Scoring the letter is a third rule, with its own failure modes, and it was not measured.
- **Small models.** Large models can be asked to choose explicitly and may reason before answering, and the gap between recognising and producing may be smaller for them. The mechanism shown in Section 19.4, a partial answer that still beats unrelated options, is not specific to small models, though how often it operates in a large one was not measured.
- **Distractors of three invented kinds.** A benchmark's distractors are usually chosen to be plausible to people, which is closer to the near-miss column than to the random one; how close is a property of each benchmark.
- **200 problems per cell.** Differences of a few points between models under near-miss multiple choice sit within about one standard error; the large gaps between scoring rules do not.

### Model judges (Section 19.5)

- **A likelihood judge.** Real judges are prompted, are often asked to compare two answers, and can reason before answering. Their biases include position and length as well as self-preference, and their sizes differ from these.
- **Exact answers.** Arithmetic made the truth free, which is what let every judge be checked. For open-ended outputs the "truth" is itself a human judgement with its own noise, and the labelled set in prediction-powered inference inherits that noise.
- **Three tiny models trained on the same data.** All three were trained on the same lines, and the main model and the twin differ only in their order, which is the case in which a judge is most likely to recognise a contestant. A judge from a different family might be biased differently or less, and that was not measured.
- **Fixed threshold.** A judge that outputs a score rather than a pass could be corrected by a calibration per model; that needs labels for each model, which is the prediction-powered recipe again.

## Key points

1. A single benchmark score's 95% interval covered the true score about 95% of the time at every benchmark size, but the ranking built from those scores did not hold: on 100 questions the best of ten models ranked first in only 50% of benchmarks, and seven close models came out in true order in 0.4%.
2. On 300 shared questions the paired test detected a real 0.038 gap in 60% of benchmarks and the unpaired one in 2.0%, and the paired standard error matched the actual spread. Five prompts per question gave a true standard error 2.1 times the naive one; the cluster-robust standard error matched it.
3. With each benchmark problem inserted into training 32 times, the benchmark score rose from a control mean of 0.551 to 0.800, while accuracy on fresh problems of the same kinds stayed within 0.513 to 0.542. Runs differing only in seed scored 0.503 to 0.577 on the same benchmark; a single training run is a sample too.
4. Item by item, the contaminated model's likelihood identified leaked problems with an AUC of 0.621 at 32 insertions, catching 3% of them at 5% false flags; with the clean model as a reference, 0.745 and 24%. Compared as sets with fresh problems, every contaminated run from two insertions on stood out from all five controls.
5. With nothing in front of each problem the main model multiplied correctly 0.500 of the time; with twelve ordinary lines, 0.735. The context moved the gap between models too: the twin's lead on three-digit carries was 0.127 under one context and 0.012 under another. Examples with wrong answers changed no score by more than 0.005.
6. The best of sixteen prompts on one set of 150 multiplications scored below the prompts' average on a second set; where the prompts really differed, the choice survived the second set.
7. Three models that generated no four-digit sum correctly picked it from four options 0.955, 0.980 and 0.790 of the time against random distractors, because the right leading digits are enough to beat unrelated options. Multiple choice measures something closer to recognition than to production.
8. The main model's multiple-choice score on the same sums was 0.955, 0.395 or 0.315 depending only on the kind of wrong options offered, and its 0.105 lead over its twin in generating multiplications disappeared under near-miss multiple choice.
9. Each of three models, judging all three models' answers by its own likelihood with a threshold calibrated on the truth, ranked itself first on every task, 9 of 9; the weakest, right on 1.3% of three-digit carries, passed 73% of its own answers.
10. With 40 labelled answers, prediction-powered inference removed the judge's bias and covered the truth in about 94% of draws; with a weak judge the plain version was wider than labels alone, and the tuned version was not.
11. Report comparisons as paired differences, cluster rows by question, keep a fresh set, report the prompt, the distractors and the normalisation with the score, choose prompts on items you will not report, and never let a model grade itself or a close relative.
