# Target the Effect, Not the Risk

When an intervention is available but cannot be given to everyone, someone has to decide who receives it, and the model asked to decide is usually a risk model: who is most likely to churn, default or be readmitted. The policy that follows looks obvious, and it is the sentence this chapter tests: *give the intervention to the people the model says are most at risk*. Practitioners meet it in retention campaigns, collections, care management and fraud review, wherever a budget limits how many people can be contacted. If it is wrong, the budget is spent on people the intervention does not help, and possibly on people it harms, while the risk model itself scores well on every standard metric.

Chapter 48 found that the features a model leans on need not be levers. This chapter assumes the lever is known, a retention offer, and asks only whom to apply it to. It sets up a simulated customer base with a randomised trial of the offer, compares targeting by predicted risk with targeting at random and with targeting by the predicted effect of the offer, explains why risk is the wrong ranking here, and measures how the answer changes with the size of the budget.

## The value of an intervention is a difference

For a customer with features $x$, the offer's value is the reduction it makes in that customer's probability of churning:

$$
\tau(x) = P(\text{churn} \mid x, \text{no offer}) - P(\text{churn} \mid x, \text{offer}).
$$

This quantity is called the *uplift*, or in causal-inference terms the *conditional average treatment effect*. A risk model estimates only the first term. Ranking by risk targets the people with the largest uplift only if the offer works best for the people most likely to leave, which is a fact about the intervention, not about the model.

Radcliffe and Surry named four kinds of customer by how the offer acts on them. *Persuadables* stay if they receive the offer and leave otherwise. *Sure things* stay either way. *Lost causes* leave either way. *Do-not-disturbs*, also called *sleeping dogs*, are made more likely to leave by being contacted. Only the persuadables are worth the budget, and the do-not-disturbs cost more than nothing.

## A simulated retention offer

The simulation has five standard-normal features. Without the offer, 34.5% of customers churn, and the log-odds of churning are $-1 + 1.5\,x_1 + x_2$, so risk rises with the first two features. The offer adds

$$
-1.6\,\exp(-x_1^2)
$$

to the log-odds, plus $0.8$ for customers with $x_3 > 1$. The first term is largest in magnitude when $x_1$ is near zero, that is, roughly for customers of middling risk: the offer does little for those almost certain to stay, who will stay anyway, and little for those almost certain to leave, whom one offer will not keep. For customers with a high value of the third feature whose risk is far from the middle, the positive term dominates and the offer backfires; they are about one customer in fifteen.

A randomised trial offers the deal to half of either 2,000 or 20,000 customers, chosen at random. From the trial, models choose which 20% of a new population of 100,000 to offer it to, and the churn prevented by each choice is computed from the simulation's true probabilities. Four kinds of targeting rule are compared:

- **random**: a random 20%;
- **true effect**: the 20% with the largest true uplift, the best possible;
- **highest risk**: the 20% with the highest predicted churn, from a model fitted to the trial's untreated arm;
- **largest effect**: the 20% with the largest predicted uplift, described below.

The last two rules are each run with boosted trees and with logistic regression.

```python
from standarderror.ml import decide

u = decide.uplift_targeting()   # budget: 20% of customers
for row in u["rows"]:
    print(f"trial of {row['n']:,} customers:")
    for k, v in row.items():
        if k not in ("n", "sd"):
            print(f"   {k:26s} {v:5.1f} churns prevented per 1,000")
```

```text
trial of 2,000 customers:
   random                      21.9 churns prevented per 1,000
   true effect                 55.5 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (boosted)      22.0 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (boosted)    39.0 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (logistic)     20.3 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (logistic)   46.5 churns prevented per 1,000
trial of 20,000 customers:
   random                      21.9 churns prevented per 1,000
   true effect                 55.5 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (boosted)      21.4 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (boosted)    47.9 churns prevented per 1,000
   highest risk (logistic)     20.1 churns prevented per 1,000
   largest effect (logistic)   50.4 churns prevented per 1,000
```

Offering the deal to the fifth with the highest predicted churn prevented between 20.1 and 22.0 churns per 1,000 customers. Choosing the fifth at random prevented 21.9. With the logistic risk model and the large trial, the risk-targeted offer did slightly worse than random. Boosted and logistic risk models did about equally badly, which points away from the quality of the risk model: it estimates the first term of the uplift, and that term is the wrong thing to rank by here. The figure below compares the rules for the large trial.

## Why risk is the wrong ranking here

The next block measures who is in the highest-risk fifth: the share of customers the offer makes more likely to churn, overall and in that fifth, and the mean reduction in churn probability in each group.

```python
print("made more likely to churn by the offer:")
print(f"   {u['harmed_share']:.1%} of all customers, "
      f"{u['top_risk_harmed']:.1%} of the highest-risk fifth")
print(f"mean reduction in churn probability: all customers "
      f"{u['mean_gain']:.3f}, highest-risk fifth {u['top_risk_mean_gain']:.3f}")
```

```text
made more likely to churn by the offer:
   6.4% of all customers, 11.2% of the highest-risk fifth
mean reduction in churn probability: all customers 0.109, highest-risk fifth 0.100
```

The highest-risk fifth holds more of the customers the offer harms. On average the offer reduced their churn probability by 0.100, less than its average over all customers, 0.109, and 11.2% of them were made more likely to leave, against 6.4% overall. Ranking by risk spends the budget on customers who are moved slightly less than average and harmed more often. The highest-risk customers are disproportionately lost causes and do-not-disturbs; the persuadables sit further down the risk scale. ## Ranking by the estimated effect

The randomised trial makes a better ranking possible. Fit one model to the customers who were offered the deal and another to those who were not, and rank new customers by the difference between their two predictions. Künzel and colleagues call this the *T-learner* ("T" for two models), the simplest of several *meta-learners*, general recipes that turn any supervised learner into an estimator of heterogeneous treatment effects. Because assignment in the trial was random, the two arms differ systematically only in the offer, so the difference between the arms' models estimates the offer's effect rather than the reasons people were offered it.

The T-learner prevented 50.4 churns per 1,000 with logistic models and the large trial, more than twice the risk ranking, against 55.5 for ranking by the true effect.

### Estimating a difference is harder than estimating a level

Each arm's model has its own estimation error, and the errors of two separate fits add up in their difference. Since the uplift is often much smaller than the churn probabilities themselves, the noise can be large relative to the signal. With the smaller trial, the effect ranking fell to 46.5 with logistic models, varying from trial to trial with a standard deviation of 3.7, and to 39.0 with boosted trees.

The logistic T-learner beat the boosted one at both trial sizes (50.4 against 47.9 with the large trial), even though the true effect is not linear in the features. A plausible reason, not isolated here, is that two simpler models leave less noise in their difference than two flexible ones. Neither came closer than 5.1 churns per 1,000 to ranking by the true effect. Methods that model the difference directly rather than as the gap between two fits, such as Athey and Imbens's causal trees, exist for this reason.

The whole approach rests on the trial. Without random assignment, the difference between offered and not-offered customers would mix the offer's effect with the reasons they were offered it. That combines two problems met earlier: Chapter 39's outcomes that exist only for those an earlier decision selected, and Chapter 48's confounding of a lever with the causes of its use.

## How the budget changes the answer

All of this was at one budget, a fifth of the customers. The ranking matters most when the budget is small and stops mattering when the budget covers everyone. The same comparison was run at five budgets, with logistic models and the large trial.

```python
for b, v in decide.uplift_budgets().items():   # trial of 20,000
    print(f"offer to {b:4.0%}: by risk {v['risk']:5.1f}, by effect "
          f"{v['effect']:5.1f}, at random {v['random']:5.1f}, "
          f"best {v['true']:5.1f}")
```

```text
offer to   5%: by risk   0.9, by effect  14.7, at random   5.5, best  17.1
offer to  10%: by risk   4.7, by effect  27.9, at random  10.9, best  31.7
offer to  20%: by risk  20.1, by effect  50.4, at random  21.9, best  55.5
offer to  50%: by risk  79.5, by effect  92.4, at random  54.7, best  98.1
offer to 100%: by risk 109.4, by effect 109.4, at random 109.4, best 109.4
```

With only 5% of customers reachable, offering the deal to the highest predicted risk prevented 0.9 churns per 1,000, against 5.5 for a random 5% and 14.7 for the effect ranking. The riskiest twentieth gained on average only 0.018 in churn probability from the offer, against 0.109 across all customers, and the risk ranking did several times worse than chance. At half the customers the risk ranking finally beat random targeting (79.5 against 54.7), because a list that long reaches far enough down the risk scale to include the middling customers the offer helps. Offering it to everyone prevents 109.4 whatever the ranking.

So the cost of ranking by risk is largest exactly when targeting matters most, under a tight budget, and it is a cost relative to doing nothing clever at all.

Every decision in this part so far has been made once, on data collected independently of it. Chapter 50 asks what happens when the decisions themselves choose the data the next model is trained on.

## In practice

- **Ask what the intervention changes, not who is at risk.** The quantity to rank by is the difference the intervention makes for each person.
- **Keep a randomised holdout.** Even a small group of eligible people who are not treated, chosen at random, is what makes the effect estimable and the targeting rule checkable.
- **Check whether risk tracks effect.** Split the scored population into fifths by risk and compare treated and untreated outcomes within each. If the effect is not largest in the top fifth, the risk score is choosing the wrong people.
- **Try simple effect models alongside flexible ones, especially when trials are small.** The noise of two fits adds up in their difference; here the logistic T-learner beat the boosted one at both trial sizes.
- **Report every targeting rule against random targeting of the same size**, in outcomes prevented per thousand, so the comparison that matters is always in view. This matters most when the budget is tight.

## Limits of the evidence

- **A simulated effect with a clear shape.** The offer's effect depends on risk in a way that makes risk targeting fail. Where the effect is largest for the highest-risk people, risk targeting is close to right, and a trial is the only way to know which case holds.
- **Known true probabilities.** Churn prevented was computed from the simulation's probabilities. In practice the value of a targeting rule has to be estimated from another trial, with its own noise.
- **Budgets measured on one trial size.** The budget sweep used the large trial and logistic models; with a smaller trial the effect ranking is noisier at every budget.
- **One-shot.** The offer is made once. Repeated offers, and customers who learn to expect them, are a different problem.

## Key points

1. Offering the deal to the highest predicted churners prevented 20.1 to 22.0 churns per 1,000 customers, about the same as random targeting (21.9).
2. The highest-risk fifth held more customers the offer could not help or would harm: 11% of them were made more likely to leave, against 6% overall.
3. Ranking by the predicted difference the offer makes, from models of a randomised trial's two arms, prevented up to 50.4, against a best possible 55.5; it needed the larger trial to get there.
4. Under a tight budget the risk ranking did worst: at 5% it prevented 0.9 per 1,000, against 5.5 at random.
5. Target an intervention by its estimated effect, which needs a randomised comparison, and measure the targeting rule against random targeting before trusting it.
