# The Right Threshold Is a Cost Ratio, If the Probabilities Are Calibrated

A classifier outputs a probability, and something has to turn that probability into a yes or a no. In most libraries that something is a default built into the `predict` method, and it rests on the sentence this chapter tests: *a classifier predicts the positive class when its probability is above 0.5*. A practitioner meets it every time a model's predictions are used directly as flags, alerts or approvals without anyone choosing where the line falls. If the sentence is wrong for the decision at hand, a model can be accurate and well calibrated and still cost more in use than having no model at all.

This chapter opens Part X, which follows a model's numbers past evaluation to the decisions made with them: which cases to flag, which model to deploy, which lever to pull, whom to treat, and what happens when those decisions supply the next round of training data. The chapter derives the threshold implied by the costs of the two errors, then measures on a simulated task, where the best possible decision is known, what the default costs, how close the derived threshold comes to the best, and why it fails for overconfident probabilities. It ends with the alternative of tuning the threshold, and with how much any model can be worth on this task.

## The threshold implied by the costs

Chapter 5 found that rebalancing classes is a roundabout way of moving the decision threshold. The direct question is where the threshold should be, and the answer is standard decision theory, set out for classifiers by Elkan in 2001.

Suppose a false alarm, flagging a case that turns out negative, costs one unit, and a *miss*, failing to flag a case that turns out positive, costs $r$ units. The number $r$ is the *cost ratio*. Take a case whose probability of being positive is $p$. If we flag it, the expected cost is the chance it is negative times the cost of a false alarm, $(1 - p) \cdot 1$. If we clear it, the expected cost is the chance it is positive times the cost of a miss, $p \cdot r$. Flagging is the cheaper action when $(1-p) < pr$, that is, when

$$
p > \frac{1}{1 + r}.
$$

At $r = 1$ the threshold is 0.5 and the default is right. At $r = 10$ it is $1/11$: a case should be flagged even when it is probably negative, because a miss is so expensive. The derivation assumes that $p$ is the case's actual probability of being positive, which a model's output is only if the model is *calibrated*: among cases assigned probability 0.05, about 5% are in fact positive.

## A task where the best decision is known

Chapter 4's simulated task makes the best decision known. It is a two-class problem with Gaussian features in 10 dimensions, half the cases positive, the classes overlapping heavily. The true probability that each test case is positive is known exactly, so the cost of deciding with the true probabilities, the best any model could achieve, can be computed.

Five models are trained on 2,000 rows: logistic regression, Gaussian naive Bayes, a random forest, boosted trees, and boosted trees recalibrated by *isotonic regression*, which fits a non-decreasing step function from scores to observed frequencies on held-out folds. Each policy is scored by its cost per case on 50,000 test rows, averaged over five training sets.

Two trivial policies serve as baselines: *flagging every case*, which never misses and costs the share of negatives, and *flagging no case*, which costs $r$ times the share of positives and is the dearer of the two here. The code below prints, at $r = 10$, the cost of flagging every case, the cost of deciding with the true probabilities, and for each model the cost at three thresholds: the default 0.5, the derived $1/11$, and a threshold tuned on held-out data, described later in the chapter.

```python
from standarderror.ml import decide

t = decide.threshold_costs()   # 5 training sets of 2,000 rows
print(f"flag every case: {t['flag_all'][10]:.3f} per case")
print(f"true probabilities: {t['bayes'][10]:.3f} per case")
for name, v in t["models"].items():
    r = v[10]                   # a miss costs 10 false alarms
    print(f"{name:22s} at 0.5 {r['half']:.3f}, at 1/11 "
          f"{r['derived']:.3f}, tuned {r['tuned']:.3f}")
```

```text
flag every case: 0.497 per case
true probabilities: 0.442 per case
logistic               at 0.5 2.003, at 1/11 0.494, tuned 0.498
naive Bayes            at 0.5 1.666, at 1/11 0.493, tuned 0.494
forest                 at 0.5 1.554, at 1/11 0.491, tuned 0.498
boosted trees          at 0.5 1.700, at 1/11 0.762, tuned 0.478
recalibrated boosted trees at 0.5 1.436, at 1/11 0.473, tuned 0.476
```

## The default threshold costs more than no model

At $r = 10$, every model used at 0.5 costs between 1.44 and 2.00 per case. Flagging every case costs 0.497, which is the share of negatives in the test set. A model used with the default threshold is therefore roughly three to four times worse (2.9 to 4.0 times) than not using a model at all.

At 0.5 a model clears every case it thinks more likely negative than positive. With heavily overlapping classes many of those are positives, and each costs ten units: the default accepts many misses to avoid false alarms that cost a tenth as much. Accuracy at 0.5 may be respectable; cost is not, because accuracy weights the two errors equally and the decision does not. The figure below plots cost per case against the cost ratio; the line for flagging every case is flat because that policy never misses.

## The derived threshold needs honest probabilities

Moving the threshold to $1/11$ removes most of the loss. Logistic regression then costs 0.494, within 0.013 of the *best threshold in hindsight* (the cheapest on the test rows themselves, which no one can choose in advance), and the recalibrated boosted trees cost 0.473, within 0.002 (0.003 if computed from the costs as rounded in the table below).

The raw boosted trees are the exception. At $1/11$ they cost 0.762, worse than flagging everyone. The formula assumes that a probability of 0.05 means a 5% chance of being positive. Chapter 14 found that boosting overfits its probabilities long before its accuracy, pushing scores towards 0 and 1 faster than the evidence justifies: the model is *overconfident*. The next block counts the cases each version of the boosted trees places below 0.01, and the share each flags at $1/11$.

```python
b = t["models"]["boosted trees"][10]
c = t["models"]["recalibrated boosted trees"][10]
print(f"probabilities below 0.01: raw {b['below_001']:.1%}, "
      f"recalibrated {c['below_001']:.1%}")
print(f"flagged at 1/11: raw {b['flag_rate']:.1%}, "
      f"recalibrated {c['flag_rate']:.1%}")
```

```text
probabilities below 0.01: raw 10.7%, recalibrated 1.8%
flagged at 1/11: raw 76.2%, recalibrated 93.6%
```

The raw model sends 11% of cases below 0.01, against 1.8% once recalibrated. Those cases fall under $1/11$ and are cleared, but they are not nearly as safe as the model claims, and each positive among them is a miss that costs ten. The threshold did what the formula said; the probabilities were wrong in the region where the threshold sits.

A threshold derived from the costs is exactly as good as the calibration of the probabilities it is applied to. Accuracy does not reveal this: isotonic recalibration keeps the ranking almost intact, yet it moved the cost at $1/11$ from 0.762 to 0.473. This is the practical reason Chapter 34's recalibration matters even when accuracy does not move. The table below collects the five models at three cost ratios.

## Tuning the threshold on held-out data

The alternative to trusting the probabilities is to compute the cost per case at every candidate threshold on held-out data and keep the cheapest. Tuned on 1,000 fresh rows, the raw boosted trees' threshold settled at 0.0020, far below $1/11$, and their cost fell to 0.478. Tuning absorbs miscalibration because it uses only the order of the scores: it does not matter that the model's 0.0020 is really a much higher probability, only that the cases below it are rare enough in positives for clearing them to pay.

Tuning has its own cost. The cheapest threshold on 1,000 rows plausibly depends on the handful of rare, expensive misses among them: at high $r$, one or two positives more or fewer in the region just below a candidate threshold change its cost a great deal, so the chosen threshold is noisy. At $r = 20$ the tuned threshold was worse than flagging everyone for three of the five models (naive Bayes, the forest and the recalibrated boosted trees). Tuning and calibration need data for the same reason, but tuning needs it at the operating point, where the expensive errors are rare.

## What following the costs asks of an organisation

The derived threshold can be uncomfortable in operation. At $r = 10$ it flagged 99.7% of cases for logistic regression and 93.6% for the recalibrated trees. A process built around 0.5, which flags about half, would be asked to flag nearly everything, and its cost per case would fall from 2.00 to 0.494 as a result.

The raw boosted trees, flagging only 76.2% at the same threshold, look more selective, but that selectivity is a symptom of their overconfidence. A flag rate is therefore a useful check in its own right. If the cost-derived threshold flags far fewer cases than the costs imply it should, the probabilities are probably too extreme, and the remedy is recalibration before thresholding, not a different threshold.

At $r = 1$ all of this collapses back to the familiar case. The derived threshold is 0.5, and the five models' costs at it ranged from 0.313 to 0.371, against 0.270 for the true probabilities. The default answers the question of equal costs correctly.

## How much any model can be worth here

The flat line in the first figure is a useful discipline. At $r = 10$ the best any model could do on this task, using the true probabilities with the derived threshold, is 0.442 per case. Flagging everyone costs 0.497. The perfect model saves 11% of the trivial policy's cost, and the trained models save less than half of that.

That is not a defect of the models. When misses are expensive the decision is to flag almost everything, and a model helps only by finding cases safe enough to clear; with heavily overlapping classes there are few. Provost and Fawcett's *ROC convex hull* makes the point geometrically. Each cost ratio and class balance defines a slope in ROC space, and the best *operating point* is where a line of that slope touches the upper boundary of the classifiers' curves. The trivial policies are the corners, flag nothing at the origin and flag everything at the top right, and a classifier's value is measured at its operating point against them, not by its accuracy at 0.5.

So far the model was given and only its threshold chosen. The model is usually chosen earlier, by a number that ignores thresholds; Chapter 47 asks whether that number picks the model that decides best.

## In practice

- **Write down the cost ratio** and compute $1/(1 + r)$. Do not inherit 0.5 from `predict`.
- **Check calibration near the threshold, not overall**, and recalibrate (Chapter 34) before applying a derived threshold.
- **Use the flag rate as a diagnostic.** If the derived threshold flags far fewer cases than the costs suggest, suspect overconfident probabilities.
- **If you tune the threshold instead, budget data for it.** The held-out rows must contain enough of the expensive errors; at high cost ratios a thousand may not be enough.
- **Always report the trivial policies.** Show the cost per case of flagging everything and of flagging nothing beside the model's. A model that does not clearly beat the better one adds no value to this decision.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Known, fixed costs.** Real costs are uncertain, differ from case to case, and are sometimes asymmetric in ways a single ratio cannot express. The arithmetic still applies case by case if the costs are known per case.
- **Balanced classes.** The task has half its cases positive. With rare positives the derived threshold stays at $1/(1 + r)$, but flagging nobody becomes the cheap trivial policy to beat.
- **One task with heavy overlap.** On a task where the classes separate well, models would likely save much more than 11%; that was not measured here.
- **Five training sets.** Costs at $r = 20$ are dominated by rare misses and are noisy. The counts of models beaten by flagging everyone could change with other training sets.

## Key points

1. With a miss ten times as costly as a false alarm, the 0.5 threshold cost 1.44 to 2.00 per case, roughly three to four times the cost of flagging every case (0.497).
2. The cost-derived threshold $1/(1 + r)$ came within about 0.002 of the best threshold in hindsight for recalibrated boosted trees, and cost the raw, overconfident ones 0.762, worse than flagging everyone.
3. A derived threshold is only as good as the calibration of the probabilities it is applied to; overconfident scores below the threshold become expensive misses.
4. Tuning the threshold on held-out rows works without calibration but needs data at the operating point; at $r = 20$ it lost to flagging everyone for three of five models.
5. Choose the threshold from the costs, apply it only to calibrated probabilities, and compare the result with the trivial policies before claiming the model is worth deploying.
