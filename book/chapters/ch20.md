# Monitoring and Retraining

Training and evaluation end at deployment, but the model's working life begins there. From then on, the data it sees are no longer the data it was fitted and scored on: customers change, prices move, new kinds of fraud appear, a disease becomes more or less common. Two questions follow every deployed model for as long as it runs. Is it still working? And what should be done to keep it working? This chapter, which opens Part VIII, takes them in that order.

Both questions are hard for the same reason: the outcomes that would answer them arrive late or not at all. A loan's default is known months after the decision; a fraud label waits for an investigation; some outcomes are never observed. The inputs, by contrast, arrive with every prediction. So the common practice is to watch the inputs for change and, as a defence against what watching misses, to retrain the model on a schedule using recent data. Each practice rests on a sentence that sounds safe, and the two sections test one each:

- Section 20.1: *a drift alarm on a model's inputs tells you when the model has stopped working.*
- Section 20.2: *retrain regularly on the most recent data and the model stays current.*

Section 20.1 separates the ways a deployed world can change: the inputs can move while the relation between inputs and outcome stays put (covariate shift), the relation itself can change (concept drift), or the share of positive outcomes can change (label shift). In a simulated world where each change is made on purpose, it compares what an input alarm reports with what happened to the model's accuracy, then checks two other monitors that need no labels and a real shift on the breast cancer data. The result is that input alarms fire when nothing is wrong and are silent when everything is, and that no quantity computed without outcomes can detect a change in the relation.

That finding sets up Section 20.2. If a changed relation can be seen only in labelled outcomes, the practical defence is not a better alarm but regular retraining on those outcomes. The section tests that defence on simulated data streams in four kinds of world, from stable to abruptly changing, and finds that the right amount of history to retrain on depends on how fast the world moves: the window length is the bias-variance trade of Section 6.4 laid along the time axis. It then asks whether the window can be chosen by replaying the past, and measures what it costs when the labels needed for retraining arrive late.

The two sections share one conclusion from opposite directions. Monitoring without outcomes cannot tell whether a model has stopped working, and retraining without timely outcomes cannot keep it working. Labelled outcomes, and how quickly they arrive, are what a deployed model's upkeep depends on. The closing sections gather the practical guidance from both: how to treat an alarm, how to budget for labels, how to choose and backtest a retraining window, and what to keep as a baseline. The next chapter stays with deployed models and asks two further questions about their outputs: how sure their individual predictions are, and whether they treat groups of people alike.

## An Alarm on the Inputs Cannot See the Outcome

<!-- source: ml-61-drift; hero: ml161-hero.png -->


Once a model is in production, the most common question about it is whether it is still working, and the most common answer is to watch its inputs. Labels arrive late or never; inputs arrive with every prediction. So a monitoring system compares each new batch of inputs with the training data, feature by feature, and raises an alarm when they differ. The practice rests on one sentence: *a drift alarm on a model's inputs tells you when the model has stopped working.* If it is false, teams are paged about models that are fine and left in silence while a model fails.

This section takes the first question a practitioner meets after deployment: is the model still working? It trains a model in a simulated world, changes the world on purpose, and compares what an input alarm reports with what happened to the model. It then checks two other label-free monitors and a real shift.

### The experiment: a simulated world and an input alarm


#### Three ways a deployed world can change

Write the joint distribution of inputs $x$ and outcome $y$ as $p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$. A classifier learns $p(y \mid x)$, the relation between inputs and outcome, and the world can change in three standard ways.

- **Covariate shift**: $p(x)$ changes and $p(y \mid x)$ does not.
- **Concept drift**: $p(y \mid x)$ changes. Customers change their behaviour, a new fraud pattern appears, a treatment protocol changes. Gama and colleagues' survey of concept drift calls this *real drift*.
- **Label shift**: the prevalence $p(y)$ changes while each class looks as it did, so $p(x \mid y)$ is fixed. A disease becoming more common is the usual example.

Section 15.3 found that covariate shift costs little when the model is correctly specified, its functional form matching the true $p(y \mid x)$. This section tests the alarm rather than the model.

#### The setup

The simulated world has five standard Gaussian inputs and a logistic outcome with coefficients (1.5, -1, 0.8, 0, 0) and intercept -0.3. A logistic model is trained on 5,000 rows drawn from it. The world is then changed, and the model is deployed on 50 batches of 200 rows and 50 batches of 2,000 rows for each scenario.

For each input the alarm runs a *two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test*, which compares two samples of a single variable through their empirical cumulative distribution functions $\hat F_{\text{train}}$ and $\hat F_{\text{batch}}$. Its statistic is the largest vertical gap between them,

$$
D = \sup_x \left|\hat F_{\text{train}}(x) - \hat F_{\text{batch}}(x)\right|,
$$

and its p-value is the probability of a gap at least that large if both samples came from the same distribution. It detects any kind of difference, in location, spread or shape. The alarm fires if any of the five p-values falls below 0.05 divided by five, a *Bonferroni correction* discussed below.

The code shows one batch of 2,000 rows under two changes, the first input's mean moved by one standard deviation (`shift`) and its coefficient reversed (`flip`), printing the smallest of the five p-values and the accuracy.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import ks_2samp
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from standarderror.ml import deploy

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X, y = deploy.drift_world(5000, rng)          # the world at training time
model = LogisticRegression().fit(X, y)
for kw in ({"shift": 1.0}, {"flip": 1.0}):    # inputs move; relation turns
    Xb, yb = deploy.drift_world(2000, rng, **kw)
    p = min(ks_2samp(X[:, j], Xb[:, j]).pvalue for j in range(5))
    acc = np.mean(model.predict(Xb) == yb)
    print(f"{kw}: smallest input p-value {p:.1e}, accuracy {acc:.3f}")
```

```text
{'shift': 1.0}: smallest input p-value 2.7e-199, accuracy 0.806
{'flip': 1.0}: smallest input p-value 8.9e-02, accuracy 0.466
```

The two lines contain the section's result: the test was certain when the inputs moved and the model improved, and saw nothing when the relation reversed and the model was wrong on more than half the rows.

### Input alarms fire when nothing is wrong


#### A large shift that improves the model

Move the first input's mean by one standard deviation and leave $p(y \mid x)$ unchanged: pure covariate shift. The alarm fired on every batch, of either size. Accuracy *rose*, from 0.771 on the validation rows before deployment to 0.801.

The reason is presumably geometric. The moved input carries the largest coefficient, so moving it carries more of the new rows far from the decision boundary, the surface where the model's predicted probability is one half. There the model is surer and right more often. A team paged by this alarm would find a model doing better than ever.

#### A small shift that a large batch makes significant

Move the same input by a tenth of a standard deviation instead. On batches of 2,000 the alarm fired on 74% of them; on batches of 200, on 14%. Accuracy did not change measurably: 0.776 against 0.778 with no drift.

This is ordinary significance testing. The statistic's sampling noise shrinks as the samples grow, so a fixed true difference gives ever smaller p-values, and a large enough batch makes any difference significant. Whether the difference matters is a separate question the test does not ask, and the same shift fires the alarm more often in a busy week than a quiet one.

#### Many inputs, many chances to fire

The last way to raise alarms over nothing is to watch many inputs. If each of $m$ inputs is tested at level $\alpha$ and nothing has changed, the chance that at least one fires, the *family-wise error rate*, grows with $m$; for independent tests it is $1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$.

With no drift at all and each input tested at 5%, at least one test fired in 23% of batches with 5 inputs, 63% with 20 and 99% with 100. Run weekly, a 20-input monitor would fire about 33 times a year with nothing wrong.

The Bonferroni correction tests each input at $\alpha / m$, which bounds the family-wise error rate at $\alpha$ whatever the dependence between tests. Here it brought the rate back near the 5% it promises, between 2.0% and 7.5% over 200 batches, at the price of missing small real shifts.

### Input alarms are silent when everything is wrong


#### Changing the relation, not the inputs

Now leave the inputs alone and change $p(y \mid x)$: the first input's coefficient moves from +1.5 to 0 (half-reversed) or to -1.5 (reversed). This is concept drift. The input alarm fired on 0% and 2% of batches, no more than with no drift at all. Accuracy fell to **0.633** and **0.466**, below a coin flip in the second case.

The silence is not a weakness of this test. The inputs came from exactly the training distribution, so any test of $p(x)$, however powerful, is testing a true null hypothesis and fires at its false-alarm rate.

#### Two label-free monitors with the same blind spot

Two more label-free monitors were equally blind. The first, the same test applied to the model's predicted probabilities, fired on 2% of batches: the scores are a function of the inputs, and the inputs had not moved.

The second is an estimate of accuracy built from the unlabelled batch by *importance weighting*, the method of Sugiyama and colleagues. Under covariate shift, the accuracy on the new data can be estimated from labelled validation rows by weighting each one by the *density ratio*

$$
w(x) = \frac{p_{\text{new}}(x)}{p_{\text{train}}(x)},
$$

estimated here with a logistic *domain classifier* trained to tell validation rows from deployment rows (its predicted odds are, up to a constant, the ratio). The estimate was within 0.002 of the truth when the inputs moved, because covariate shift is exactly its assumption: it reweights the inputs and keeps the old $p(y \mid x)$. Under concept drift it said 0.771 throughout. It reported the past.

#### Why nothing label-free can detect concept drift

The three monitors use nothing from the deployment batch but its inputs (the accuracy estimate's labels are the old validation labels). A change in $p(y \mid x)$ with $p(x)$ fixed leaves the distribution of every such quantity unchanged, so nothing computed without outcomes can tell the drifted world from the original. The only signal is in labelled outcomes; Section 20.2 below measures what it costs when they arrive late. The table below lists every scenario.

The score alarm tests the model's predicted probabilities in the same way.](ml161-f1-table.png){alt="A ten-row table. The input alarm is 1.00 when the inputs move a standard deviation and near zero when the relation reverses."}

### Label shift and a real shift


#### Label shift: accuracy hides what the probabilities show

Between the two sits label shift. Moving the prevalence from 0.46 to 0.20 changes the mix of the two classes' inputs, and the input alarm fired on every batch. Accuracy stayed close (0.794 against 0.771). The probabilities did not: the model's average predicted probability was 0.363 against an actual rate of 0.201. At a prevalence of 0.8 it was 0.596 against 0.803.

By Bayes' rule, $p(y \mid x)$ depends on the prior $p(y)$, so it moves with the prevalence while the model keeps the old one, and a decision at a fixed probability threshold would be wrong in proportion. Lipton, Wang and Smola showed how to estimate the new prevalence from unlabelled predictions under exactly this assumption.

#### A real covariate shift on breast cancer

The Wisconsin diagnostic breast cancer data give a real version of the first scenario. A standardised logistic model trained on 300 random tumours scored 0.967 on the other 269, with no feature alarmed. Deployed only on tumours above the training median of mean radius, nineteen of the 30 features raised an alarm, the share of malignant tumours rose from 38% to 70%, and accuracy was 0.941, 8 errors in 135 tumours. On the largest quarter of tumours, 20 features raised an alarm and accuracy was 0.969.

Differences of that size are within what so few tumours can measure, given the binomial error bar of Section 1.1. A monitor would have reported a crisis that the outcomes did not show. If the inputs cannot say whether a model has stopped working, the defence has to rest on the outcomes themselves, and the next section turns to the standard one: retraining on them.

## How Much History to Retrain On Depends on How Fast the World Moves

<!-- source: ml-64-retraining; hero: ml164-hero.png -->


The previous section found that a change in how outcomes depend on inputs shows up only in labelled outcomes; no monitor on the inputs can see it. The standard defence does not try to detect such a change at all. It retrains the model on a schedule, on recent data, so that the model follows the world wherever it goes. The practice rests on a sentence with two parts that can each fail: *retrain regularly on the most recent data and the model stays current.* "Most recent" can be the wrong amount of data, and "regularly" assumes the labels are there to retrain on. Either failure leaves a team paying for retraining that does not keep the model current.

This section tests both parts on simulated data streams. It first compares retraining windows of different lengths in four kinds of world, from stable to abruptly changing, and shows that the best window depends on how fast the world moves. It then asks whether the window can be chosen from past performance, and finally measures what a delay in labels costs.

### A stream of data and five retraining policies


#### The four worlds

The test is a stream. Each period brings 60 new rows with ten standard Gaussian inputs and a logistic outcome. The relation between inputs and outcome, the world's true coefficient vector, behaves differently in four kinds of world:

- **stable**: it stays fixed;
- **slow drift**: it takes a small random step each period;
- **fast drift**: it takes a larger random step each period;
- **jump**: it stays fixed and then, halfway through, at period 40, jumps once to a new direction.

The relation's strength, the length of the coefficient vector, is held constant throughout, so only its direction changes. A change in direction means that the same inputs now point to different outcomes: the concept drift of Section 20.1 above.

#### The policies

After ten periods of history, a logistic model is retrained every period under five policies. *Never retrain* keeps the model fitted on the first ten periods forever. *All history*, also called an *expanding window*, refits on every labelled row seen so far. The other three use a *sliding window*, refitting only on the last 1, 4 or 16 periods. Each policy is scored on the next period's rows, which the model has not seen, and everything is averaged over six streams per world.

The code generates one fast-drift stream and prints its shape.

```python
import numpy as np
from standarderror.ml import deploy

rng = np.random.default_rng([0, 2])            # one fast-drift stream
Xs, ys = deploy._stream("fast drift", rng, periods=80, rows=60, dim=10)
print(len(Xs), "periods of", Xs[0].shape[0], "rows,",
      Xs[0].shape[1], "inputs; outcome rate in period 0:",
      round(float(ys[0].mean()), 2))
```

```text
80 periods of 60 rows, 10 inputs; outcome rate in period 0: 0.43
```

### The best window depends on the world


#### In a stable world, recent data is just less data

In the stable world, the policy that sounds most current was the worst. Retraining on the last period alone, 60 rows, gave accuracy 0.729, against 0.779 for all history. Nothing was changing, so discarding old data only discarded sample size. A ten-input model fitted on 60 rows has noisy coefficients, and each period's model was a fresh draw of that noise. The model that was never retrained scored 0.777, as good as any: in a stable world, a model fitted once on enough data has nothing to catch up with.

#### Under drift, old data becomes misleading

Under slow drift the order changed, and the best policy was a long window: the last 16 periods scored 0.770, all history 0.762, the last period 0.728. Under fast drift, all history fell to 0.648, dragged towards relations that no longer held, and the last 4 periods scored 0.740. After a jump, the last 4 periods were best again (0.758).

The next block runs the comparison for the stable and fast-drift worlds and prints each policy's mean accuracy. It uses two streams per world rather than six, so its figures differ slightly from those quoted above.

```python
r = deploy.retraining(delays=(0,), reps=2)["results"]
for world in ("stable", "fast drift"):
    v = r[f"{world}/0"]["mean"]
    print(world)
    print("  " + "  ".join(f"{p} {a:.3f}" for p, a in v.items()))
```

```text
stable
  static 0.778  expanding 0.782  window 1 0.727  window 4 0.765  window 16 0.774
fast drift
  static 0.605  expanding 0.661  window 1 0.723  window 4 0.744  window 16 0.715
```

The pattern is the same: in the stable world accuracy rises with the window, and under fast drift it peaks at four periods and falls on either side.

#### A bias-variance trade along the time axis

So the best window was all history, 16, 4 and 4 in the four worlds, and no window was best in every kind of world. This is the bias-variance trade of Section 6.4, with the window length as the knob, laid along the time axis. A long window has low *variance*: it averages over many rows, so its coefficients are stable. But it has *bias* when the world moves, because it is fitted partly to relations that no longer hold. A short window tracks change, so its bias is small, but it is fitted on few rows and is noisy. The best length balances the two, and the balance point depends on how fast the relation moves relative to how many rows arrive per period.

Widmer and Kubat built adaptive windows for this in 1996, and Klinkenberg and Joachims chose window sizes by estimating error on recent data. Both work by measuring how fast the world is moving, which a fixed schedule does not. The table below collects every world and policy.

### Choosing the window by backtest


#### Right when the pace is steady

The practical fix is to choose the window the way Klinkenberg and Joachims did, by its record. A *backtest* replays the past: at each earlier date, retrain each policy on the data available then, score it on the following period, and keep the policy with the best record. Done here on the first 30 scored periods of each world (periods 10 to 39, averaged over the six streams) and judged on the remaining 40, the backtest chose all history in the stable world, which was right, and the four-period window under fast drift, also right (0.741).

#### Wrong when the pace changes

It chose wrong in the other two worlds. Under slow drift the first 30 periods favoured all history, because the relation had not yet moved far, and over the next 40 that choice scored 0.754 against 0.768 for the sixteen-period window. In the world that jumped, the first 30 periods were all before the jump and favoured all history, which then scored 0.625 over the remaining periods against 0.750 for the four-period window.

A backtest measures how fast the world moved, not how fast it will move. It works when the pace of change is itself stable, and it cannot anticipate a change that has not happened in its history.

#### The cost of never retraining

The never-retrained model is the other reference point. In the stable world it lost nothing. Under fast drift it scored 0.572, and after the jump its accuracy fell to 0.538, not far above a coin, because the old direction said little about the new one. A model that is never retrained bets that the world is stable, and the bet is either free or very expensive.

### Late labels delay every recovery


The schedule also assumes that labels are there to retrain on. Often they are not. A loan defaults months after the decision, a churned customer is known only after the renewal date, and a fraud label waits for an investigation. The *label delay* is the time between a prediction and the arrival of its outcome. With labels arriving eight periods late, each retraining could use only rows at least eight periods old.

In the stable world the delay cost nothing (0.776 against 0.779 for all history), since old rows were as good as new. Under fast drift every retrained policy got worse, and the best one's advantage over never retraining fell from 0.168 to 0.096: the delay took away 43% of what retraining bought. A delayed model is always trained on a relation that is eight periods out of date, and in a fast-moving world eight periods is a long time.

The jump makes the cost concrete. Averaged over the stream, the four-period window scored 0.730 with late labels against 0.758 with prompt ones. After the jump, it took 3 periods to recover with immediate labels and 19 with late ones, 16 more, against a delay of eight. All history did not recover within the 40 periods after the jump either way, still anchored to the relation before it. Every period of delay is a period the model spends wrong before it can even begin to learn the new relation, and nothing in the inputs can shorten it, as Section 20.1 above found. The figure below traces accuracy through the jump.

Both sections of this chapter have asked whether a deployed model is right on average, batch by batch and period by period. The next chapter asks two questions an average can hide: how far a single prediction may be off, and whether a model that is right overall is right in the same way for every group.

## In practice

### Monitoring a deployed model (Section 20.1)

- **Treat an input alarm as a reason to look, not as evidence of harm.** Before escalating, check labelled performance; a moved input can leave accuracy unchanged or improve it.
- **Do not read silence as health.** Input alarms, score alarms and importance-weighted accuracy estimates cannot detect a change in $p(y \mid x)$.
- **Correct for the number of features watched** (test each at $\alpha / m$, accepting that small real shifts will be missed), and **fix the batch size or alarm on effect size**, since on large batches a p-value alarm fires on shifts too small to matter.
- **Compare mean predicted probability with the observed rate** whenever outcomes arrive; under label shift, accuracy can hold while the probabilities are wrong.

### Labelled outcomes and their delay (both sections)

- **Budget for labelled outcomes**, however few and late: a regular labelled sample, or delayed outcomes joined back to predictions. A change in the relation shows up nowhere else, and retraining cannot follow a change without them.
- **Know your label delay and plan around it.** Under change it eats much of what retraining buys and lengthens every recovery. Shorten it where you can, for instance with faster investigation of outcomes.

### Choosing a retraining policy (Section 20.2)

- **Do not assume shorter is safer.** In a stable world a short window only adds noise; with 60 rows a period the last period alone cost 0.049 of accuracy against all history.
- **Measure how fast your relation changes** from performance on labelled outcomes over time, and choose the window from that, not from a habit or a schedule.
- **Backtest the window choice on your own history**, retraining at each past date only on data whose labels would have been available then. Re-run the backtest periodically, since it reflects the past pace of change, not the future one.
- **Keep a never-retrained baseline** in your evaluation. If it holds up, retraining is buying little; if it collapses, the world is moving.

## Limits of the evidence

### Monitoring (Section 20.1)

- **Five inputs, one model.** Real monitors watch hundreds of features and use other tests, such as population stability indices and embedding distances. Their behaviour with sample size and with many features is the same in kind as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test's.
- **Shifts chosen to separate the cases.** Real drift usually mixes a moving input distribution with a changing relation, and an input alarm will then often fire for the wrong reason at the right time. That coincidence is not a mechanism to rely on.
- **A correctly specified model.** Section 15.3 showed that a misspecified model does lose accuracy under covariate shift, so an input alarm is more informative for such a model. It still cannot see a change in the relation.
- **Labels assumed absent.** Where labels arrive quickly, monitoring the outcome directly makes most of Section 20.1 unnecessary.

### Retraining (Section 20.2)

- **Logistic model, logistic world.** The model was correctly specified in every period, so its only problems were noise and staleness. A misspecified model adds a third: more data of the wrong kind cannot fix it.
- **Fixed windows.** Weighting rows by age, or retraining only when monitored performance drops, sit between the policies measured here; their best settings depend on the drift rate in the same way.
- **Constant volume.** Sixty rows a period made short windows noisy. With far more rows per period, short windows lose less in a stable world.
- **Six streams per cell.** Differences of under a point between neighbouring windows are within the noise; the ordering of the best policies across worlds was not.

## Key points

1. An input alarm fired on every batch when an input moved a standard deviation, and accuracy rose from 0.771 to 0.801.
2. When the relation between inputs and outcome reversed and the inputs stayed put, input and score alarms fired no more than with no drift, a label-free accuracy estimate said 0.77, and accuracy was 0.47.
3. With 20 inputs tested separately, 63% of batches raised an alarm with nothing wrong; with large batches, a shift too small to matter raised it 74% of the time.
4. Treat input drift as a reason to look, not as evidence of harm. Budget for labelled outcomes, however few and late, because a change in the relation shows up nowhere else.
5. In a stable world, retraining on the last period alone cost 0.049 of accuracy against all history, and never retraining cost nothing.
6. The best window was all history, the last 16 periods, the last 4 and the last 4 across four worlds; no fixed choice was best everywhere. Window length is a bias-variance knob.
7. A backtest chose the right window when the pace of change was steady and the wrong one when it was not.
8. Labels eight periods late took 43% of retraining's gain under fast drift and added 16 periods to the recovery after a jump.
9. Choose a retraining window by measuring how fast your relation changes, from performance on labelled outcomes over time. Plan around your label delay; shorten it where you can, because it adds to every period a changed model spends wrong.
