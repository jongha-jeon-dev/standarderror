# Pictures of High-Dimensional Data

Many data sets are too wide to look at or to model comfortably, and the routine answer is to reduce them: to replace dozens or hundreds of columns by a few derived ones. This chapter examines the two reductions a practitioner meets most often. Principal component analysis (PCA) is run before a model to make a wide table narrow, and it is also the plainest way to draw a data set in two dimensions. t-SNE is run only to draw, and its scatter plots of cells, documents and embeddings are read every day in reports and papers. Both are applied as a matter of course and rarely measured, and both are read as keeping more than they do. A reduction that discards the direction that predicts the target, or a picture whose group sizes and gaps are artefacts, gives no warning of either.

The chapter opens Part VI, on methods fitted to data without labels. Its two sections each test one sentence that practitioners rely on:

- Section 16.1: *the first principal components keep the information that matters*;
- Section 16.2: *a t-SNE plot shows the data's clusters, how big they are and how far apart*.

Each sentence is tested in the way used throughout the book. First a simulation is built in which the answer is known, so that the method can be compared with the truth; then the question is replicated on real data. Section 16.1 uses the breast-cancer and diabetes data bundled with scikit-learn, where a prediction target is available to judge what a reduction kept. Section 16.2 uses the UCI handwritten digits, where the digit classes are known even though t-SNE never sees them.

The sections build on each other. Section 16.1 establishes what PCA computes: the directions along which the inputs vary most, in the units the inputs are given in, chosen without any reference to the target. It finds that the units of measurement can choose the first component, that a small component can predict more than larger ones, and that the number of components kept before a model is better chosen by held-out performance than by a share of variance. Section 16.2 then uses PCA in two roles. It is the control picture, a linear projection that keeps sizes and gaps along its directions, against which t-SNE's distortions can be attributed to t-SNE itself rather than to the drop to two dimensions. And it is the starting point from which t-SNE can be initialised, which turns out to decide whether t-SNE's global layout is stable.

Together the two sections show reductions failing in opposite places. PCA keeps variance and the broad layout of the data but loses neighbourhoods, and it can drop what predicts. t-SNE keeps neighbourhoods but equalises the sizes of groups and loses the distances between them. The common lesson is that a reduction keeps what its objective rewards, and anything else read off it has to be checked in the original space. Chapter 17 asks the same question of clustering algorithms, and Chapter 18 asks it of distance itself.

## Principal Components Keep Variance, Not Relevance

<!-- source: ml-31-pca; hero: ml131-hero.png -->


The first reduction is principal component analysis, one of the routine steps applied around a model and rarely measured. Before a model it is justified by one sentence: *the first principal components keep the information that matters*. A practitioner meets it whenever a wide table is made narrow before fitting, often with the default "keep the components that explain 95% of the variance". If the sentence is false, the reduction can discard the direction that predicts the target, and nothing in the variance figures will say so.

This section first defines what PCA computes and shows, on a constructed example, that it can drop the only predictive direction. On the breast-cancer data it shows that the units of measurement choose the first component. On the diabetes data it finds a small component that predicts more than two larger ones together, and compares PCA with partial least squares. Finally it chooses the number of components by held-out performance and says what PCA is the right tool for.

### What PCA computes, and what it never sees


*Principal component analysis* (PCA) finds, for centred inputs, the direction along which the data vary most, then the direction of most remaining variance at right angles to it, and so on. These directions are the eigenvectors of the inputs' covariance matrix, ordered by their eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_p$, and $\lambda_j$ is the variance along direction $j$. A row's projection onto direction $j$ is its score on the $j$th *principal component*, and the component's *share of variance* is $\lambda_j / (\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_p)$. The usual reduction keeps the fewest leading components whose shares sum to a threshold such as 95% or 99%; regressing on them is *principal component regression*.

Two properties matter for what follows. The computation uses only the inputs, so PCA cannot know which direction predicts the target; keeping the leading components assumes that the directions of greatest variance carry the most information. And variance is measured in the units the features are given in, so rescaling a feature changes the components.

#### A constructed case with the signal in the last component

The assumption can fail completely. Ten independent normal features have standard deviations from 10 down to 0.3, and a binary label depends only on the last and smallest. Because the features are independent, the components are, up to sampling noise, the features themselves in order of standard deviation. The code fits logistic regression on the first $k$ components for each $k$ and prints the accuracies, then the last component's share of variance.

```python
from standarderror.ml import around

sim = around.pca_simulation()  # label depends on the smallest feature
print("accuracy with k components: " + " ".join(
    f"{a:.2f}" for a in sim["acc"]))
print(f"variance in the last component: {sim['share'][-1]:.2%}")
```

```text
accuracy with k components: 0.51 0.50 0.50 0.51 0.50 0.50 0.49 0.50 0.53 0.92
variance in the last component: 0.03%
```

With nine components out of ten, logistic regression is at chance: none of the directions it sees carries information about the label. The tenth, holding 0.03% of the variance, raises accuracy to 0.915, and a 95% or 99% variance threshold would have dropped it. The example is built to fail and settles only the principle: a variance criterion has no access to relevance. Whether this matters on real data is a separate question.

### The units of measurement choose the first component


The breast-cancer data hold thirty measurements of cell nuclei, such as radius, area, smoothness and concavity, each as a mean, a standard error and a "worst" value. The code runs PCA on the raw measurements and again after *standardising* each feature, subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation so that each has variance one. It prints the first component's share, its leading feature, and how many components the 95% and 99% rules keep.

```python
bc = around.pca_breast_cancer()
for key in ("raw", "std"):
    r = bc[key]
    print(f"{key}: PC1 holds {r['share'][0]:.1%}, led by {r['top']!r}; "
          f"95% rule keeps {r['k95']}, 99% keeps {r['k99']}")
```

```text
raw: PC1 holds 98.2%, led by 'worst area'; 95% rule keeps 1, 99% keeps 2
std: PC1 holds 44.3%, led by 'mean concave points'; 95% rule keeps 10, 99% keeps 17
```

On the raw measurements the first component holds 98.2% of the variance and is led by worst area. This is not a finding about tumours. Area is measured in square pixels and runs into the thousands, while smoothness and concavity are fractions near zero. Variance scales with the square of the units, so area's variance is millions of times larger, and PCA, which counts variance in the units it is given, puts the area axis first. The 95% rule keeps one component: in effect, the size of the largest nucleus.

Standardised, the first component holds 44.3% and is led by mean concave points, and the 95% rule keeps 10 components. The downstream model notices: with one raw component logistic regression scores 0.905, with standardised components 0.981. The table below sets the two versions side by side.

![Nothing about the tumours changed between the two rows, only the units in which their variance is counted.](ml131-f2-table.png){alt="A two-row table: on raw features the first component holds 98% of the variance, is dominated by worst area, and the variance rules keep one or two components; standardised, they keep 10 and 17, and accuracy is higher at every k."}

Standardising before PCA is the usual advice for this reason, and the comparison shows it is not a technicality. Choosing how to scale the features is choosing what "variance" means, and the components follow that choice. Section 6.2 found the same for the lasso, whose kept features changed when one feature was re-expressed in other units.

### A small component can carry the most signal


The diabetes data of Efron and colleagues have ten features, including age, body-mass index and blood serum measurements, already centred and scaled, so units cannot be the problem; the target is disease progression after one year. For each component the code prints its share of feature variance and the share of the target's variance it explains alone, the $R^2$ of regressing the target on that component. The *coefficient of determination* $R^2$ is one minus the ratio of residual to total sum of squares: the fraction of the target's variance a fit accounts for.

```python
d = around.pca_diabetes()
for j, (v, r2) in enumerate(zip(d["share"], d["r2"]), 1):
    print(f"PC{j:<2d} variance {v:5.1%}   target explained {r2:5.1%}")
```

```text
PC1  variance 40.2%   target explained 30.8%
PC2  variance 14.9%   target explained  3.8%
PC3  variance 12.1%   target explained  2.6%
PC4  variance  9.6%   target explained 12.8%
PC5  variance  6.6%   target explained  0.0%
PC6  variance  6.0%   target explained  0.7%
PC7  variance  5.4%   target explained  0.5%
PC8  variance  4.3%   target explained  0.1%
PC9  variance  0.8%   target explained  0.0%
PC10 variance  0.1%   target explained  0.4%
```

The two columns are not in the same order. The first component explains 30.8% of disease progression, so here the leading direction does carry signal. The second and third hold 27.0% of the feature variance between them and explain 6.4%. The fourth, smaller than either at 9.6%, explains 12.8%. Because principal components are uncorrelated in the sample, their contributions to a linear fit do not overlap, so the comparison is fair: the fourth adds more than the second and third together. A pipeline that keeps "the first three components" keeps two nearly useless ones and drops the one that is not, as the figure below shows.

![PC4 holds 9.6% of the variance and explains 12.8% of the target; PC2 and PC3 together hold 27.0% and explain 6.4%.](ml131-f0-components.png){alt="Paired bars for ten components: the blue variance bars fall steadily; the orange target bars are tall for PC1, small for PC2 and PC3, and tall again for PC4."}

#### Partial least squares lets the target choose the directions

Principal component regression on the first $k$ components, with the PCA fitted inside each training fold, reaches a cross-validated $R^2$ of 0.349 with three components and 0.479 with four; the jump comes from admitting the fourth. *Partial least squares* (PLS) also builds linear combinations of the inputs, but chooses each one for its covariance with the target rather than for variance alone. A direction like the fourth component then need not wait behind larger, irrelevant ones. PLS reaches 0.479 with two components, the value PCA needed four for, as the figure below shows.

![Partial least squares reaches R² 0.479 with two components; PCA needs four (0.479).](ml131-f1-pls.png){alt="The PCA curve stays near 0.3 for one to three components and jumps at four; the PLS curve is near 0.48 from two components on."}

The point is not new. Jolliffe made it in 1982 with examples from the literature: components with small variance can be the ones that predict, and dropping them by variance alone is a decision taken without looking at the target.

### Choosing the number of components by held-out performance


The repair within PCA is to treat the number of components $k$ as a *hyperparameter*, a setting chosen by scoring candidates on held-out data with the PCA refitted inside each training fold, rather than as a share of variance to reach. On diabetes the cross-validated $R^2$ is 0.292 with one component, 0.349 with three, 0.479 with four, 0.475 with five and 0.484 with all ten. A search lands at four or more, within 0.005 of using every component.

So once the target chooses, it keeps at least four components, and the reduction buys little. What the target supplies is where to stop. A rule that looked only at variance, and stopped once the second and third components had brought the total near two-thirds, would have stopped one component short of the one that matters. On breast cancer the same search on standardised components finds its best at 10, scoring 0.981, where the 99% rule would keep 17. The two rules need not agree because they answer different questions: one asks how much of the inputs is kept, the other how much of the prediction. Section 4.1 adds a caution for any such search: the best cross-validated score among many candidates is itself optimistic.

### What PCA is the right tool for


None of this makes PCA a bad tool; it is a tool for a different job. To describe the inputs, to compress images, plot a high-dimensional data set or remove redundancy among nearly collinear features, variance is the right criterion and the leading components the right ones to keep. When the inputs happen to vary most along the directions that matter, PCA before a model costs little. Nothing guarantees that, and the variance shares cannot reveal whether it holds.

For prediction, the number of components is a hyperparameter like any other, and the PCA belongs inside the folds like every fitted preprocessing step (Section 3.1 measures what fitting such steps on all rows can cost). If the reduction should serve the prediction, a supervised method uses the target to decide what to keep: partial least squares, or simply a regularised model on all the features. Section 17.1 meets a related habit in clustering, where an unsupervised answer about the inputs is read as a finding about structure.

PCA's descriptive job includes drawing a data set in two dimensions, and there it meets a method built for drawing alone. The next section uses the first two principal components as the control against which t-SNE's picture is judged.

## A t-SNE Picture Keeps the Neighbours and Loses the Map

<!-- source: ml-56-tsne; hero: ml156-hero.png -->


Section 16.1 above could still judge a reduction by a target: whatever PCA kept, a model fitted afterwards said how much of the prediction survived. Before this part, every part of the book had something of that kind to check a model against: a label, a known parameter, an exact sum. Part VI removes the labels. Clustering, mixture models, density methods and anomaly scores are fitted to data with no right answer in them, which makes their output easy to believe and hard to test. The test remains the one used throughout the book: simulate data whose structure is known, then replicate on real data where some of it is known. The most believed unsupervised output is a picture. t-SNE turns high-dimensional points into a two-dimensional scatter in which similar points sit together, and its plots of cells, documents and embeddings are read every day as maps: these groups exist, this one is bigger, those two are close. That reading is the sentence under test: *a t-SNE plot shows the data's clusters, how big they are and how far apart*. If it is false, analysts report group sizes and separations that the data do not contain.

This section first explains what t-SNE computes, then measures cluster sizes and gaps on simulated data where both are known, with the first two principal components as a control. It then turns to the 1,797 UCI handwritten digits to measure what t-SNE does keep, the neighbourhoods, and how its global layout depends on how it is started.

### What t-SNE optimises


t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding) was introduced by van der Maaten and Hinton in 2008. It does not try to preserve distances; it converts distances into probabilities of being neighbours and makes those probabilities match between the original space and the picture. In the original space, for each point $x_i$ it defines a conditional probability that $x_i$ would pick $x_j$ as its neighbour,

$$
p_{j\mid i} = \frac{\exp\!\left(-\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2\right)}{\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\!\left(-\lVert x_i - x_k\rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2\right)},
$$

a Gaussian kernel centred on $x_i$ with its own bandwidth $\sigma_i$. The bandwidth is chosen so that the distribution $p_{\cdot\mid i}$ has a fixed *perplexity*, $2^{H}$ where $H$ is its entropy in bits. Perplexity can be read as the effective number of neighbours each point has; the user sets it. The conditional probabilities are symmetrised into joint probabilities $p_{ij}$. In the two-dimensional picture, with points $y_i$, the corresponding similarities $q_{ij}$ use a heavy-tailed Student t kernel with one degree of freedom, proportional to $(1 + \lVert y_i - y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}$. The positions $y_i$ are found by gradient descent on the Kullback–Leibler divergence

$$
\mathrm{KL}(P\,\Vert\,Q) = \sum_{i\neq j} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}},
$$

starting from random positions or from the first two principal components. scikit-learn's implementation, used here, approximates the gradient with the Barnes-Hut method.

Two features of this construction matter below. The per-point bandwidth gives every point the same effective number of neighbours, wherever it sits. And the loss weights each pair by $p_{ij}$, so pairs that are not neighbours in the original space enter it only through a general repulsion that keeps points apart, not through their actual distance.

Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson warned in 2016, with interactive examples, that cluster sizes and the distances between clusters in a t-SNE plot may mean nothing. This section puts numbers on the warning. The first block builds two simulations with known sizes and gaps and prints a ratio in the data and in the picture at a perplexity of 30.

```python
from standarderror.ml import unsup

for r in unsup.tsne_sizes(perplexities=(30,)):   # one wide, one narrow
    print(f"radius ratio: data {r['data_ratio']:.2f}, "
          f"t-SNE {r['embedded_ratio']:.2f}")
for r in unsup.tsne_distances(perplexities=(30,)):  # gaps 6 and 60
    print(f"gap ratio:    data {r['data_ratio']:.2f}, "
          f"t-SNE {r['embedded_ratio']:.2f}")
```

```text
radius ratio: data 10.06, t-SNE 1.01
gap ratio:    data 9.84, t-SNE 1.06
```

Both ratios are close to ten in the data and to one in the picture.

### Cluster size in the picture does not reflect spread in the data


The first simulation has two Gaussian clusters of 500 points each in ten dimensions, far apart, one with ten times the other's standard deviation. In the data the wide cluster's radius is 10.1 times the narrow one's. In the t-SNE picture it was 0.99 to 1.01 times as large, at perplexities of 5, 30 and 100. The simulations used the PCA initialisation, so the result is not an artefact of a random start.

This is not a malfunction; it is the per-point bandwidth doing its job. Because each $\sigma_i$ is tuned to give the same perplexity, a point in a sparse cluster gets a wide bandwidth and a point in a dense cluster a narrow one. After the conversion the neighbour probabilities inside the wide cluster look just like those inside the narrow one. The absolute spread has been divided out before the picture is drawn, and nothing in the loss puts it back. t-SNE equalises density by design, so the size of a cluster in the picture is not a statement about how spread out its points are. The figure below shows the two clusters before and after.

![Cluster radii in the data and in the t-SNE picture. The wide cluster's radius over the narrow one's is 10.1 in the data and 1.01 in the picture.](ml156-f0-sizes.png){alt="Left, a tiny dense cluster beside a large diffuse one. Right, two round clusters of the same size."}

### Gaps between clusters are not distances


The second simulation places three clusters of 300 points on a line in ten dimensions: A, then B six units away, then C sixty units beyond B. In the data the B–C gap is 9.8 times the A–B gap. In the picture the ratio was 0.98 at perplexity 5, 1.06 at 30 and 1.45 at 100. A cluster ten times farther away was drawn about as far away as a near neighbour.

The objective explains it. t-SNE matches the probabilities that points are each other's neighbours. Two clusters that share no neighbours at all have essentially zero probability of being neighbours, whether they are 6 units apart or 60. Every pair straddling two such clusters has $p_{ij} \approx 0$, so the loss is nearly indifferent to how far apart the clusters are drawn, provided they do not overlap. Nothing in it rewards placing them in proportion.

Larger perplexities widen each point's bandwidth, so that more distant points receive non-negligible neighbour probability. That is presumably why the ratio grew with perplexity, and why Kobak and Berens recommend large perplexities and a PCA initialisation when the global layout matters. The table below collects the ratios.

![Size and gap ratios in the data and in the t-SNE picture at three perplexities. Every cluster has 300 or 500 points in ten dimensions; there is one t-SNE run per cell.](ml156-f1-ratios.png){alt="A three-row table. The data's ratios are about 10; the picture's are all close to 1."}

### A linear projection keeps sizes and gaps


A distortion could belong to t-SNE or simply to squeezing ten dimensions into two. The control is the plainest two-dimensional picture there is: the first two principal components of Section 16.1 above, which project the data onto the two orthogonal directions along which it varies most. On the same two clusters PCA drew the wide one 10.7 times the radius of the narrow one, and on the three clusters on a line it drew the far gap 9.8 times the near one. Both are close to the data's 10.1 and 9.8: a projection keeps what lies along its directions, and here the gaps and most of the wide cluster's spread do.

So the distortions belong to t-SNE, not to the reduction in dimension. The linear picture has its own price. It keeps variance, not neighbourhoods: on the digits below it kept 12% of each point's ten nearest neighbours. And, as Section 16.1 above found when it asked whether the first principal components keep what matters for a prediction, when the structure of interest does not lie along the directions of greatest variance, PCA draws the wrong thing faithfully. The two methods fail in opposite places, which is the argument for looking at both before believing either.

### What the picture keeps: neighbourhoods


The digits test the other half of the claim, the part t-SNE is built for. The measure is *neighbourhood preservation*: for each of the 1,797 images, take its ten nearest neighbours in the 64 pixel dimensions, count how many are still among its ten nearest in the picture, and average the fraction over all images.

The global layout needs a different measure. The ten digit classes each have a centroid, and there are 45 distances between pairs of centroids. Computing the 45 distances in pixel space and in the picture, and correlating their ranks, gives a *rank correlation* (Spearman's): 1 if the picture orders the class-to-class distances exactly as the pixels do, 0 if there is no relation. It asks only whether near classes are drawn near and far classes far.

The block below computes both measures for two principal components and for one t-SNE run started from random positions.

```python
d = unsup.tsne_digits(seeds=(0,))
t = d["tsne"][0]
print(f"neighbours kept: PCA {d['pca']['knn']:.3f}, t-SNE {t['knn']:.3f}")
print(f"class-distance rank corr.: PCA {d['pca']['global']:.3f}, "
      f"t-SNE {t['global']:.3f}")
```

```text
neighbours kept: PCA 0.118, t-SNE 0.588
class-distance rank corr.: PCA 0.815, t-SNE 0.750
```

Two principal components, the linear control used above, kept 12% of neighbours. t-SNE kept 58.3% to 58.8% across five seeds. Locally it is far better, as its objective, dominated by pairs with large $p_{ij}$, asks.

### The global layout depends on the initialisation


On the class-to-class distances the ordering reverses. PCA scored a rank correlation of 0.81. The five t-SNE runs started from random positions scored 0.65 to 0.75. More tellingly, two of those runs that differed only in the random seed agreed with each other on the 45 distances with rank correlations ranging from 0.50 to 0.97. With random starts, which digit classes sit next to each other in the picture is partly a property of the seed.

A plausible reason, not tested here, lies in the objective. Because far-apart pairs contribute almost nothing to the loss, it constrains where whole classes go only weakly, and a random start can leave well-formed clusters in a random arrangement.

Started instead from the first two principal components, which is scikit-learn's default and what Kobak and Berens recommend, every one of the five seeds scored 0.817 against the pixels and kept 58.5% of neighbours, and the five pictures agreed with one another at rank correlations of 1.00. The PCA start carries the linear picture's global layout into t-SNE, and t-SNE then rearranges the neighbourhoods inside it. The layout of a t-SNE picture is only as trustworthy as its initialisation.

### Clustering the picture


A common shortcut is to cluster the t-SNE coordinates themselves. The clustering method used here is HDBSCAN, a density-based method that groups points lying in dense regions, needs no number of clusters, and labels points in sparse regions as noise; Section 17.3 describes how it works. Agreement with the true classes is measured by the *adjusted Rand index* (ARI), which counts the pairs of points that two partitions treat alike (together in both, or apart in both) and corrects that count for the agreement expected by chance: it is 1 for identical partitions and near 0 for unrelated ones. Here HDBSCAN's noise points are counted as one more group, which is scikit-learn's convention.

On the digits the shortcut worked. HDBSCAN on the randomly started t-SNE coordinates recovered the classes with an ARI of 0.79 to 0.84, finding 10 or 11 clusters. For comparison, k-means on the raw pixels scored 0.67, and HDBSCAN on the raw pixels scored 0.17, calling 54% of the images noise. The digits have well-separated classes whose neighbourhoods t-SNE preserves. The same shortcut inherits the picture's equal sizes and arbitrary gaps wherever those matter. The table below collects the digits results.

## In practice

### Reducing the inputs before a model

- **Decide on scaling before PCA and report it.** On raw breast-cancer features the first component was, in effect, the feature with the largest units. Standardise unless the features share meaningful units.
- **Put each component's variance share beside its relation to the target.** Compute both for every component. If a dropped component relates to the target more strongly than a kept one, the variance rule has cut in the wrong place.
- **Choose the number of components by cross-validation, not a threshold,** with the PCA fitted inside each training fold.
- **Compare with methods that see the target.** Fit partial least squares and a regularised model on all features. If the PCA pipeline does not beat them, the reduction is not earning its place.
- **Stop citing "95% of the variance" as a reason in a predictive pipeline.** It describes the inputs; keep it for compression, plotting and removing redundancy.

### Reading a picture of the data

- **Read a t-SNE plot for neighbourhoods only.** "These points are similar" is what the method preserves; how large a group is, or how far apart two groups are, is not.
- **Measure sizes and distances in the original space.** Compute within-group spread and distances between centroids in the data, and report those instead of the picture's.
- **Start from PCA.** Use the PCA initialisation (scikit-learn's default) whenever the global layout will be read at all. With random starts, the arrangement of classes changed with the seed.
- **Check any layout claim across seeds and perplexities.** Rerun with several seeds and at least two perplexities, including a large one; a claim that changes between runs was about the run.
- **Look at PCA beside t-SNE, and treat clusters found on t-SNE coordinates with care.** The linear picture keeps sizes and gaps along its directions; t-SNE keeps neighbourhoods. Clusters of the picture inherit its equalised densities and arbitrary gaps.

## Limits of the evidence

### Principal components (Section 16.1)

- **Linear structure only.** PCA, PLS and the models after them are linear here. A target that depends on a nonlinear function of the inputs can be invisible to every linear component.
- **Small data sets.** Thirty features and ten. With hundreds of features and few rows the variance ranking itself is noisy, and the case for some reduction before the model is stronger, of whichever kind.
- **One target per data set.** A component that does not predict this target can predict another. PCA's neutrality about the target is a cost for one prediction task and a benefit when the reduced features serve many.
- **The simulation is extreme by design.** It shows the mechanism, not its frequency. The diabetes result is the measure of how often it bites: once in four components, on a standard teaching data set.

### t-SNE (Section 16.2)

- **Gaussian clusters in ten dimensions.** Each simulation isolates one property. Real data mix them, and with continuous structure, a trajectory rather than groups, t-SNE can also cut one group into pieces, which was not measured here.
- **t-SNE only.** UMAP is now used as often and has a different objective. It is often said to keep more global structure, but its sizes and gaps are not distances either. It was not measured here.
- **One run per cell in the simulations.** The size ratios were within 0.05 of 1 at every perplexity, which is far from 10 by any standard of run-to-run noise. The gap ratios moved more with perplexity and would move with the seed.
- **Digits are easy.** Well-separated classes are the best case for clustering a picture. Overlapping classes are where the picture's gaps mislead most.

## Key points

1. PCA ranks directions by the variance of the inputs, in their units, and never sees the target.
2. On raw breast-cancer data the first component held 98.2% of the variance and was led by worst area; the 95% rule kept 1 component raw and 10 standardised.
3. Variance is not relevance. On diabetes the fourth component explained 12.8% of the target, more than the second and third together.
4. Regression on three components reached a cross-validated $R^2$ of 0.349 and on four 0.479; partial least squares reached 0.479 with two.
5. Before a model, standardise deliberately, choose the number of components by cross-validated performance, and consider a method that sees the target.
6. A cluster with ten times the spread of another came out of t-SNE 0.99 to 1.01 times its size; a gap 9.8 times another came out 0.98 to 1.45 times as wide.
7. Two principal components kept the size and gap ratios close to the data's, so the distortions belong to t-SNE, not to the reduction to two dimensions.
8. On the digits t-SNE kept 59% of each point's ten nearest neighbours, against 12% for two principal components.
9. Started at random, t-SNE ranked the distances between classes less faithfully than PCA, and two seeds agreed on them as little as 0.50; started from PCA, every seed matched PCA's 0.81 and the layouts agreed.
10. Read a t-SNE plot for who sits next to whom. Measure sizes and distances in the data, start from PCA, and check any layout claim across seeds and perplexities.

## Further reading

- Jolliffe, "A note on the use of principal components in regression", *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society C* (1982): examples from the literature in which low-variance components are the predictive ones.
- Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, *The Elements of Statistical Learning*, section 3.5: principal component regression and partial least squares as methods built on derived input directions.
- van der Maaten and Hinton, "Visualizing data using t-SNE", *JMLR* (2008): the original description of the method and its Student t kernel in the low-dimensional space.
- Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson, "How to use t-SNE effectively", *Distill* (2016): interactive examples of misleading cluster sizes and distances, the warning Section 16.2 quantifies.
- Kobak and Berens, "The art of using t-SNE for single-cell transcriptomics", *Nature Communications* (2019): practical recommendations, including large perplexities and PCA initialisation to preserve global structure.
- Efron, Hastie, Johnstone and Tibshirani (2004): the source of the diabetes data.
- Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) data, as bundled with scikit-learn: the thirty nuclear measurements used for the units comparison.
