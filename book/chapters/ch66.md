# The Book, Rerun With Five More Seeds

Every number in this book was produced by code, and every chapter's claims were checked by that code each time the book was built. Part XIV, the book's last, turns the book's methods on the book itself, and begins with the most basic question one can ask of a measured result: would it come out the same on another random draw? The sentence under test is the book's own working assumption: *a verdict measured once, and pinned so the build fails if it drifts, will hold on the next draw*. A practitioner makes the same assumption whenever a result from one split, one simulated data set or one training run goes into a report as a conclusion. If it is false, some conclusions describe one draw rather than the method or the data.

The chapter first explains what a pinned assertion does and does not protect against. It then reruns the twelve verdicts in the book that rested on a single draw, each on five new seeds, and sorts them by how often they held. It closes with what the failures share: how close the measured number sat to the word chosen for it.

## A pinned assertion guards against drift, not against luck

Each chapter's build ends with a list of *assertions*: statements in the code that check a computed value against what the prose says. Some pin a number the text quotes; others pin a qualitative claim, such as "the knee falls below the good radii". If a library update or an edit to the code changes a result, the assertion fails, the build fails, and the chapter cannot be published with a stale number.

That protection is real but narrow. An assertion checks that the code still produces what it produced when the chapter was written, not that what it produced was typical. Many verdicts were averaged over repeated draws inside their chapter, so their spread was already measured. Some, however, came from a single split, a single simulated data set or a single random initialisation. For those, the assertion pinned whatever that one draw showed, and a verdict that was partly luck was pinned as firmly as one that was not.

A *random seed* is the integer that initialises a pseudo-random number generator. Fixing it makes a computation reproducible; changing it gives a new, equally legitimate draw. Rerunning on several seeds is the cheapest way to see how much of a result belongs to the method and how much to the draw.

## How the twelve verdicts were rerun

Twelve verdicts qualified: each rested on one draw, and each chapter's function takes a seed. Every one was rerun with seeds 1 to 5, using the chapter's own code and the conditions its assertions state. Seed 0 reproduces every published value, so the comparison is purely between draws, with the code, the settings and the claim's threshold held fixed.

The code below shows the mechanism for Chapter 58's verdict about DBSCAN's radius. `course.CLAIMS` holds, for each claim, the chapter number, the verdict as written, what is measured, and a `check` function that takes a seed and reports the value and whether the verdict held.

```python
from standarderror.ml import course

ep, verdict, what, check = next(c for c in course.CLAIMS if c[0] == 58)
print(verdict)
for seed in range(3):
    r = check(seed)
    print(f"seed {seed}: {what} {r['value']:.3f}, held: {r['held']}")
```

```text
the k-distance knee falls below the half-moons' good radii
seed 0: knee's score 0.573, held: True
seed 1: knee's score 0.960, held: False
seed 2: knee's score 0.798, held: False
```

Seed 0 gives the published 0.573 and the verdict holds; seeds 1 and 2 give 0.960 and 0.798, and it fails on both.

## Seven verdicts held on every seed, and two on four of five

Seven verdicts held on all five new seeds: those of Chapters 3, 5, 33, 40, 48, 56 and 57. Some held with essentially the same number every time. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC), which Chapter 57 showed counts Gaussian components rather than clusters, chose 9 components for three uniform squares on every draw. t-SNE, which Chapter 56 showed keeps neighbourhoods but not sizes, drew a cluster with ten times another's spread at 0.997 to 1.015 times the narrow one's size.

Others moved but stayed far from their threshold. In Chapter 48, dropping the test result inflated the treatment's apparent effect 1.62 to 1.67-fold, against a claim of more than 1.6. In Chapter 40, a target encoding fitted on the training rows ranked its own labels at an AUC of 0.773 to 0.781. (The *AUC* is the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative; one half is chance.)

Two verdicts held on four of five. Chapter 11 said that none of 200 *bootstrap trees*, trees refitted on resamples of the rows drawn with replacement, reproduced the full-data tree's top three levels; in one new split, 3 did. Chapter 31 said only the tenth principal component predicted, at accuracy above 0.9; in one new simulation it scored 0.889. Both points survive in substance: three trees in 200 is still almost none, and 0.889 is still the only component that predicts. What failed was the threshold written into the prose, "none" and "above 0.9", which was tighter than one draw could support. The table below lists all twelve verdicts.

## Three verdicts did not hold as written

In each of the remaining three, the underlying observation was correct; the word or number attached to it was not.

### The k-distance knee on the half-moons (Chapter 58)

DBSCAN groups points that have enough neighbours within a radius, and its result depends heavily on that radius. A common heuristic is the *k-distance plot*: sort every point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour and take the radius at the knee, where the curve bends sharply upwards. Chapter 58 found that on two half-moons the knee fell just below the window of good radii and scored 0.57.

On five other draws of the moons it fell inside the window every time, scoring 0.80 to 0.96. The published miss was that draw's: the knee sits close to the window's lower edge, and which side it lands on depends on the noise. The chapter's broader point, that the heuristic picked a wrong radius on the digits, stands; the moons no longer illustrate it.

### The share of tumours whose top feature changed (Chapter 18)

A SHAP value attributes a prediction relative to a *background*, a reference set of rows that defines what the prediction is compared with. Chapter 18 found that explaining tumours against benign ones instead of all of them changed the top feature for 58% of tumours, and wrote "most". On five other splits the share was 33% to 53%: still large, and the real claim, that the explanation depends on the background, is untouched. But "most" was that split's.

### Grouped anomalies and a round AUC threshold (Chapter 59)

A simple anomaly score is a point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour. If anomalies form a group larger than k, each one's k nearest neighbours are other anomalies, so it looks normal. Chapter 59 asserted that grouped anomalies then scored an AUC below 0.2. On the new seeds the worst AUC ranged from 0.18 to 0.24: always far below chance, the chapter's real claim, but above the 0.2 its assertion wrote down in three of five.

All three chapters have been revised to report the range from this rerun. The figure below counts the seeds on which each verdict held.

## What the failures have in common: a small margin

The verdicts that broke were not the surprising ones. Each was a correct observation whose number sat close to the word the prose attached to it: a knee just below a window, a share just above a half, an AUC just under a round threshold. Near a threshold, a small draw-to-draw variation is enough to carry a value across, and a single draw says nothing about how large that variation is.

Bouthillier, Laurent and Vincent made the same observation about deep-learning papers in 2019: results reported from one seed often fail to reproduce under another, not because the method is wrong but because the margin was smaller than the run-to-run variation. Chapter 20 measured that variation for networks that differ only in their seed; this chapter measured it for the book's own claims.

The verdicts that held had one of two protections: they were averages over many draws inside their chapter, or the effect was far larger than any plausible variation, such as nine components for three squares or a tenfold size difference drawn as none. A single draw is enough evidence for a large effect and not for a precise number, and a pinned assertion is only as good as the margin between the measured value and the threshold it checks.

The cost of finding this was small. The twelve reruns took about six minutes of computer time, less than writing any one of the chapters they checked, and they changed three of them. Whenever a number is about to become a word, such as *most*, *none*, *always* or *below* a round threshold, rerun it with a few seeds and see whether the word survives the range.

Seeds test one source of fragility, the random draw. A result can also depend on the analyst's choices, made one after another while watching the scores; Chapter 67 measures how far such a sequence can move a reported result.

## In practice

- **Rerun single-draw results before they become conclusions.** A model comparison, feature ranking, tuned setting or cluster count from one split or one initialisation should be rerun on a few other seeds, varying both the split and the model's own randomness.
- **Report the range, not the draw**, and choose words the whole range supports.
- **Watch the margin.** Before writing "above 0.9", "none" or "most", compare the distance to that threshold with the spread across seeds. If they are comparable, the word is not supported.
- **Set automated checks from the range.** If you pin results in tests, take thresholds from several seeds, so that the check fails on a real change rather than an ordinary draw.

## Limits of the evidence

- **Twelve of many.** These were the verdicts that rested on one draw and whose functions take a seed. Verdicts averaged inside their chapter were not rerun, on the reasoning that their spread was already measured; some may still depend on choices other than the seed.
- **Five new seeds.** Enough to catch a verdict that fails often, not one that fails one time in twenty.
- **Seeds change different things.** For some functions the seed draws a new data set, for others only a new split or a new model initialisation; claims about real data cannot be given new data this way.
- **The same author checking the same code.** A rerun cannot find a mistake in the code itself, only in how much one draw was trusted.

## Key points

1. A pinned assertion protects a number against drift in the code, not against its having been a lucky draw.
2. Of twelve single-draw verdicts, seven held on five of five new seeds and two on four of five, where a threshold in the prose was tighter than the evidence.
3. Three did not hold as written: a heuristic's radius that missed in one draw and hit in five (Chapter 58), a share that was "most" in one split and 33% to 53% in others (Chapter 18), and an AUC threshold crossed in three of five reruns (Chapter 59).
4. The broken verdicts were observations close to the words chosen for them; the ones that held were averages or very large effects.
5. Before writing a number into a claim, rerun it with a few seeds, write down the range, and choose words the range supports.
