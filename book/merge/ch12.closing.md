# Closing material for Chapter 12: Thresholds and Costs

# From Section 12.1 (episode 46): The Right Threshold Is a Cost Ratio, If the Probabilities Are Calibrated

## In practice


- **Write down the cost ratio** and compute $1/(1 + r)$. Do not inherit 0.5 from `predict`.
- **Check calibration near the threshold, not overall**, and recalibrate (Section 11.3) before applying a derived threshold.
- **Use the flag rate as a diagnostic.** If the derived threshold flags far fewer cases than the costs suggest, suspect overconfident probabilities.
- **If you tune the threshold instead, budget data for it.** The held-out rows must contain enough of the expensive errors; at high cost ratios a thousand may not be enough.
- **Always report the trivial policies.** Show the cost per case of flagging everything and of flagging nothing beside the model's. A model that does not clearly beat the better one adds no value to this decision.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Known, fixed costs.** Real costs are uncertain, differ from case to case, and are sometimes asymmetric in ways a single ratio cannot express. The arithmetic still applies case by case if the costs are known per case.
- **Balanced classes.** The task has half its cases positive. With rare positives the derived threshold stays at $1/(1 + r)$, but flagging nobody becomes the cheap trivial policy to beat.
- **One task with heavy overlap.** On a task where the classes separate well, models would likely save much more than 11%; that was not measured here.
- **Five training sets.** Costs at $r = 20$ are dominated by rare misses and are noisy. The counts of models beaten by flagging everyone could change with other training sets.

## Key points


1. With a miss ten times as costly as a false alarm, the 0.5 threshold cost 1.44 to 2.00 per case, roughly three to four times the cost of flagging every case (0.497).
2. The cost-derived threshold $1/(1 + r)$ came within about 0.002 of the best threshold in hindsight for recalibrated boosted trees, and cost the raw, overconfident ones 0.762, worse than flagging everyone.
3. A derived threshold is only as good as the calibration of the probabilities it is applied to; overconfident scores below the threshold become expensive misses.
4. Tuning the threshold on held-out rows works without calibration but needs data at the operating point; at $r = 20$ it lost to flagging everyone for three of five models.
5. Choose the threshold from the costs, apply it only to calibrated probabilities, and compare the result with the trivial policies before claiming the model is worth deploying.

## Exercises


1. For a classifier you use, write down what a missed positive and a false alarm each cost, even roughly, and compute $1/(1 + r)$. Check a calibration curve near that threshold, not overall.
2. Compute the cost per case of your current threshold, of $1/(1 + r)$, and of the two trivial policies, flagging everything and flagging nothing. If the model does not clearly beat the better trivial policy, its value lies elsewhere than in this decision.
3. Derive $1/(1 + r)$ from the expected costs of flagging and clearing a case, and say which step fails when the probabilities are overconfident. Why is a tuned threshold unaffected by this, yet noisier as $r$ rises?

## Further reading


- Elkan, "The foundations of cost-sensitive learning", *IJCAI* (2001): the derivation of the optimal threshold from a cost matrix, and its consequences for training and evaluating classifiers.
- Provost and Fawcett, "Robust classification for imprecise environments", *Machine Learning* (2001): the ROC convex hull, and how to choose classifiers and operating points when costs and class balance are uncertain.

# From Section 12.2 (episode 47): The Model With the Best AUC Was Never the Best When Misses Cost Ten Times More

## In practice


- **Decide the operating point before comparing models.** From the costs (Section 12.1) or from the capacity, work out where on the ROC curve the model will be used.
- **Compare models at that point.** Report each candidate's cost at its operating point, or its precision in the top k when capacity is fixed. Use the AUC only as a first filter.
- **Plot the ROC curves together.** If they cross, the AUC's ranking says nothing about which model is better at a given operating point.
- **Inspect both ends of the ranking.** Compute the positive rate among the cases each model rates safest and riskiest. A model can lead at one end and trail at the other.
- **Do not rely on a partial AUC without knowing where the decision sits.** It helps only if its range covers the operating point.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One task with overlapping classes.** On tasks where models separate the classes well, their ROC curves may cross less often; that was not measured here.
- **Each model at its own best threshold.** Real deployments choose the threshold on validation data, as in Section 12.1, which adds noise to every cost and can change the winner in close cases.
- **Ten training sets.** The counts of wins are out of ten; the forest's zero at $r = 10$ and its ten by AUC are the robust part.
- **Ranking-only decisions.** Decisions that depend on calibrated probabilities, not just the ranking, add calibration to the comparison, as Section 12.1 measured.

## Key points


1. The forest had the highest AUC in all ten training sets and the lowest cost when both errors cost the same.
2. When a miss cost ten times a false alarm it was the cheapest in none; logistic regression, last by AUC at 0.677, was cheapest in 3, and at twenty times in 5.
3. At those costs the decision depends on the cases a model rates safest, where the forest put 5.6% positives and logistic regression 2.8%.
4. The AUC averages over the whole ranking, and over implicit cost ratios that differ between models; two models can each be better at opposite ends of one ranking.
5. Choose a model by its cost at the operating point you will use, or by precision at the capacity you have, and use the AUC only as a first filter.

## Exercises


1. Take two models you compared by AUC and plot both ROC curves on the same axes. Mark the point each would operate at, from the costs or the capacity of the decision.
2. If the curves cross, compute each model's cost or precision at that point and see whether the AUC's choice survives.
3. Sort each model's scores and compare the positive rate among the 2% it rates safest and the 2% it rates riskiest. Which end does your decision use?
4. Explain why a partial AUC can still choose a different model from the cost at the operating point, and what you would need to know to choose its range.

## Further reading


- Hand, "Measuring classifier performance: a coherent alternative to the area under the ROC curve", *Machine Learning* (2009): shows that the AUC implicitly averages costs over a classifier-dependent distribution, and proposes an alternative.
- Provost and Fawcett, "Robust classification for imprecise environments", *Machine Learning* (2001): the ROC convex hull and choosing classifiers when costs are uncertain.
- McClish, "Analyzing a portion of the ROC curve", *Medical Decision Making* (1989): the partial AUC, restricted to the region of the curve relevant to a decision.

# From Section 12.3 (episode 69): One Project, Every Check, and the Threshold Mattered More Than the Model

## In practice


- **Write down the costs first.** State the cost ratio of the two kinds of mistake before comparing models, and derive the threshold from it; treat 0.5 as a choice that needs a reason.
- **Choose the threshold before the model, and the model by whether it can support the threshold.** Check calibration where the threshold sits, not only on average: a model whose probabilities pile up near 0 and 1 can leave its errors beyond any threshold's reach.
- **Do not over-read a model comparison.** Use the corrected resampled t-test on folds and McNemar's test on a test set; if neither separates the models, stop tuning the comparison.
- **Use nested cross-validation for tuned models** and look at the test set once.
- **Turn instability into a review queue.** Flags that change under bootstrap refits are the cases to send to a person.
- **Treat subgroup and shift checks as qualifications.** On a small test set they locate the hard cases and the changed inputs; they do not establish rates.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One split.** The project used one 70/30 split, as a project would. Section 22.2's reruns showed how often single-draw results move; the costs here differ between models by a few tumours, and another split could reorder them. The threshold's effect, 20 cost units across the held-out tumours, is larger than that.
- **An easy data set.** Breast cancer is nearly separable. On harder problems the choice of model can matter more than it did here.
- **A known cost ratio.** The threshold's value depends on the ratio of ten, which in practice is a judgement, and is worth stating and varying.
- **Best thresholds on the test rows.** Those costs are bounds, used only to compare spreads; the project's own result is the 1/11 threshold fixed in advance.

## Key points


1. Cross-validation preferred logistic regression (0.985 against 0.957, corrected p 0.11); the test set preferred boosted trees (0.965 against 0.953, McNemar p 0.5). Neither could choose.
2. A threshold derived from the cost of a miss cut logistic regression's missed malignancies from 4 to 1 and its cost from 0.257 to 0.140; the models' best costs spanned 0.140 to 0.175.
3. Boosted trees, with the better Brier score, missed the same 3 malignancies at both thresholds, because their probabilities were too extreme for a threshold to reach them.
4. Subgroups, resampling and the shift check qualified the decision without changing it; 23 of 171 flags changed under resampling. Recalibrating the boosted model cost more than it saved.
5. Choose the threshold before you choose the model, and choose the model whose probabilities can support the threshold.

## Exercises


1. Take a project you have finished and rerun it in this section's order: baseline, corrected comparison, nested tuning, one look at the test set, calibration, a threshold from costs you write down, subgroups, resampled flags and a shift check.
2. Mark each check from Exercise 1 by whether it changed the decision, qualified it or did nothing. If the comparison of models did nothing and the threshold did, explain where the time spent choosing between models should have gone.
