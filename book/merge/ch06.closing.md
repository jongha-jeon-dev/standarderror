# Closing material for Chapter 6: Linear Models

# From Section 6.1 (episode 6): On Separable Data, Your Logistic Coefficients Are Your Stopping Rule

## In practice


- **Test for separation before reporting coefficients.** Fit a linear SVM with a very large $C$ and read the training accuracy. At 100%, the unpenalised estimate does not exist.
- **Refit at two tolerances.** If the coefficients move by more than the precision you report, they were never estimates; report signs and ratios, not magnitudes, odds ratios or Wald p-values.
- **Know which estimator you ran.** scikit-learn penalises by default; turning the penalty off, or using a package that fits plain maximum likelihood, exposes you to all of this.
- **Choose the penalty deliberately**, by cross-validation on log loss if the probabilities matter, rather than letting a tolerance or iteration limit choose it.
- **Check the confidence, not just the accuracy.** If many training rows sit within a hair of 0 or 1, do not trust the probabilities on new data; add a penalty or recalibrate on held-out data (Section 11.3).

## Limits of the evidence


- **Separation is a property of the training sample.** A population can overlap while every sample of it you can afford is separable. The breast cancer data is not separable in principle, only in every half of it measured here. Whether the unpenalised maximum exists can change with one added row.
- **Quasi-complete separation behaves the same way for some coefficients.** In *quasi-complete separation* a hyperplane separates the classes except for rows lying exactly on it; some coefficient estimates diverge and the rest are fine. Software warnings for it are inconsistent.
- **Standard errors are the first casualty.** *Wald standard errors*, computed from the curvature of the log-likelihood at the fitted point, come out enormous under separation and the p-values beside them are meaningless. Firth's penalised likelihood, which keeps the estimates finite, is the usual repair when coefficients rather than predictions are the goal.
- **The gradient descent path uses one pair of digits.** The logarithmic growth of the norm and the max-margin limit are theorems. The step at which the test log loss turns depends on the data and on the step size.

## Key points


1. The maximum-likelihood logistic fit does not exist when a hyperplane separates the training classes, and informative features make separation more likely, not less. All 50 half splits of the breast cancer data were separable.
2. Unpenalised, the coefficients a library returns are set by its stopping rule: across tolerances, a norm from 2.4 to 1,289 and a test log loss from 0.079 to 1.575.
3. Gradient descent's weight norm grows like $\log t$, about 3.3 per tenfold more steps here, and its direction converges to the max-margin direction of the problem it solves. With the intercept counted in the norm, that limit is 5.9 degrees from scikit-learn's hard-margin SVM here.
4. Along that path test accuracy can improve while test log loss worsens: the direction improves and the confidence overshoots.
5. A penalty gives the question an answer. Choose its strength deliberately; otherwise a tolerance chooses it.

## Exercises


1. Check whether a training set you have used is separable by fitting a linear SVM with a very large $C$ and reading the training accuracy. If it is 100%, refit any unpenalised logistic model at two tolerances and compare the coefficients with the precision you reported.
2. For the same model, compute the share of training rows whose predicted probability lies within a hair of 0 or 1. If it is large, decide whether a penalty or calibration on held-out data is the better repair for your use of the probabilities, and say why.
3. Explain why scaling a separating weight vector by a constant greater than one increases every term of the log-likelihood, and why the same scaling cannot change the training or test accuracy.
4. Explain why gradient descent that includes the intercept in its weight vector converges to a different direction from a hard-margin SVM with a free intercept, even on the same separable data.

## Further reading


- Albert and Anderson, "On the existence of maximum likelihood estimates in logistic regression models", *Biometrika* (1984): the conditions under which the logistic maximum-likelihood estimate exists, including complete and quasi-complete separation.
- Soudry, Hoffer, Nacson, Gunasekar and Srebro, "The implicit bias of gradient descent on separable data", *JMLR* (2018): the proof that the weight norm grows logarithmically and the direction converges to the max-margin solution.
- Cover (1965): the counting argument for how many randomly labelled points a hyperplane can separate in a given dimension.
- Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) and UCI Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits, as bundled with scikit-learn (CC BY 4.0): the two datasets used in this section.

# From Section 6.2 (episode 7): Change a Feature's Units and the Lasso Changes Its Mind

## In practice


- **Treat units as part of the model specification.** Before fitting a lasso, list each feature with its units and decide how each should be priced. Write the decision down.
- **Test the selection's sensitivity to units.** For the features you are least sure about, multiply the column by 10 and by 0.1, refit with cross-validation, and see whether any change status. If one does, its presence was partly a unit decision.
- **Decide how binary features are scaled.** Refit once with everything standardised and once with binaries left as 0/1. If the selected set changes, choose explicitly between an effect per occurrence and an effect per standard deviation, and say which.
- **Do not use cross-validated error to choose between selections.** Here the selections differed while the errors were within a few per cent; the error cannot arbitrate.
- **Report selected sets conditionally.** State that a feature was selected *in the units used*, or do not present the selected set as a finding.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Prediction is barely affected.** Everything here concerns *which* features a lasso keeps, not how well it predicts. If the model is used only for its predictions, the units matter far less, though the coefficients still do not mean what they appear to.
- **Ridge has the same dependence, without the visible flips.** A *ridge* penalty, λ times the sum of squared coefficients, is also charged per unit of coefficient, so rescaling a feature changes how much it is shrunk. Ridge never sets a coefficient to zero, so no feature visibly enters or leaves.
- **Cross-validation does not fix it.** Every refit here re-chose λ by cross-validation. The units are not a tuning problem: they change the shape of the penalty, the relative prices of the features, and not its overall strength.
- **Weighted penalties make the choice explicit rather than removing it.** The *adaptive lasso* gives each feature its own penalty weight, usually derived from a preliminary fit, and *group penalties* price whole blocks of features together. Both choose the per-feature prices on purpose instead of inheriting them from the units; neither makes the prices disappear.
- **One dataset and one simulation.** The count of seven flips belongs to the diabetes data and to factors up to a hundred. A feature that flips only at a factor of 100 may never be recorded in a unit that coarse; one that flips at 3 easily could be.

## Key points


1. A lasso penalty is a price per unit of coefficient, and a coefficient's units are the inverse of its feature's units. Rescaling a feature by $k$ is the same as giving it a private penalty $\lambda / k$.
2. On the diabetes data, 7 of 10 features are kept or dropped depending on their units alone; only sex, bmi and bp survive every factor from 0.01 to 100.
3. The cross-validated errors of all those models span 5.3% of the base model's: the data cannot distinguish them.
4. Standardising is a choice of units. A binary feature at 2% prevalence is kept in 24 of 40 datasets standardised and 7 of 40 raw.
5. Report a lasso's selected set as a property of the features *in the units you chose*, or not as a finding at all.

## Exercises


1. Take a lasso you have fitted and list its features with their units. Pick the two you are least sure about, multiply each column by 10 and by 0.1 in turn, refit with cross-validation, and record whether either changes status. If one does, write the sentence a reader of your selected set should be told.
2. If your model mixes binary and continuous features, refit once with everything standardised and once with the binaries left as 0/1 and only the continuous features standardised. If the selected set changes, decide which question you meant to ask, effect per occurrence or per standard deviation, and justify it.
3. Show that multiplying a feature's column by $k$ leaves the lasso's squared-error term unchanged at the rescaled coefficient $\beta_j / k$, and use this to explain why the rescaling cannot be undone by re-tuning a single λ.
4. Explain why standardising a rare binary feature makes the lasso more likely to keep it, and why the raw 0/1 version is not thereby the "correct" one.

## Further reading


- Tibshirani, "Regression shrinkage and selection via the lasso", *JRSS B* (1996): the original lasso paper, which standardises the features and explains why.
- Gelman, "Scaling regression inputs by dividing by two standard deviations", *Statistics in Medicine* (2008): the case for putting continuous and binary inputs on a comparable scale.
- Efron, Hastie, Johnstone and Tibshirani (2004): the source of the diabetes data, as bundled with scikit-learn.

# From Section 6.3 (episode 8): Cross-Validated Lasso Is Tuned to Predict, Not to Select

## In practice


- **Do not read a CV-minimum lasso's non-zero coefficients as a list of real effects.** At that penalty some passengers are expected. Use the CV minimum for prediction.
- **Use the 1-SE rule, or a stricter one, when selection is the goal,** and accept that it drops some weak real effects. Compare the two selected sets: features in both are the most defensible list; features only in the CV-minimum set are likely passengers.
- **Bootstrap the selection and tabulate signs.** Refit on 100 resamples and count, for each feature, how often it is kept with each sign. A feature kept often with both signs is not a finding about its direction.
- **Look for pairs that trade places.** Two features of which one is kept when the other is not are a single signal the data cannot attribute. Report the pair, not the member that happened to win.
- **Do not expect the elastic net or stability selection to resolve a near-copy.** They group or stabilise; they do not identify which of two correlated features is the real one.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Prediction is not the casualty.** Everything here concerns the selected set. The CV-minimum lasso is a perfectly good predictor; what fails is reading its non-zero coefficients as a list of real effects.
- **Stability selection is the principled fix, and it changes the question.** Meinshausen and Bühlmann's procedure keeps the features selected in a large share of subsamples across many penalties, with a guarantee that controls the expected number of noise features selected. It is still blind to which of two near-copies is the real one; the guarantee is about noise features, not stand-ins.
- **The elastic net groups; it does not choose.** Adding a ridge component (half and half, tuned by the same cross-validation) keeps correlated features together instead of picking one. On this design it does so by keeping the stand-in *more* often, 42% of the time at ρ = 0 and 90% at ρ = 0.99, with 3.6 noise features kept on average. Grouping is not choosing, and it is no closer to the truth.
- **One simulation design.** Ten features, 100 rows, effects of 0.5. How the number of passengers changes with more rows was not measured; a penalty tuned for prediction is known not to be selection-consistent in general, so do not count on it falling to zero. The stand-in problem should shrink with more rows: in this design b's correlation with the true features is ρ, below 1, so the irrepresentable condition holds and Zhao and Yu's theory says a suitably tuned lasso eventually separates a from b. The rows that takes grow as ρ approaches 1, and nothing here measures how many.
- **Bootstrap selection frequencies are themselves estimates.** A hundred resamples pin a share down to within about ±10 points at 95%; the sign splits quoted, with both shares far above zero, are well clear of that.

## Key points


1. Cross-validation tunes the lasso to predict. At its minimum, the lasso here kept 2.8 of 7 pure-noise features on average, and an irrelevant feature about a third of the time at any correlation.
2. The 1-SE rule drops most of them (0.2 on average) at a small cost in prediction, and at some cost in real effects missed.
3. Neither rule reliably separated a feature from a highly correlated stand-in at 100 rows: at ρ = 0.99 the stand-in was kept 38% of the time and the real feature 70% under the 1-SE rule.
4. On the diabetes data, age was kept in 82% of bootstrap fits with its sign split 37% positive and 45% negative. Report sign stability, not only selection frequency.
5. Treat a lasso's selected set as a property of the penalty, the units and the sample as much as of the process that generated the data.

## Exercises


1. Refit a lasso you have used on 100 bootstrap resamples and tabulate, for every feature, how often it is kept with each sign. Mark any feature kept often with both signs, and any pair of features that trade places across resamples.
2. Refit the same model with the 1-SE rule and compare the two selected sets. Classify each feature as in both, only in the CV-minimum set, or only in the 1-SE set, and say which of these you would report as findings.
3. Explain, using the penalty $|\beta_a| + |\beta_b|$ and the sum $\beta_a + \beta_b$, why the lasso cannot reliably choose between two nearly identical columns, and why a ridge penalty keeps both instead.
4. Explain why the cross-validation minimum keeps the irrelevant b about as often when it is uncorrelated with a as when the correlation is 0.95, but the 1-SE rule keeps it more often as the correlation rises.

## Further reading


- Zhao and Yu, "On model selection consistency of lasso", *JMLR* (2006): the irrepresentable condition and when the lasso recovers the true set of features.
- Meinshausen and Bühlmann, "Stability selection", *JRSS B* (2010): selecting features by their frequency across subsamples and penalties, with error control on noise features.
- Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, *The Elements of Statistical Learning* (2009), section 7.10: cross-validation and the one-standard-error rule.
- Efron et al. (2004): the source of the diabetes data, as bundled with scikit-learn.

# From Section 6.4 (episode 9): The Bias–Variance U Belongs to a Knob, Not to Complexity

## In practice


- **Before expecting a U, ask what the knob does.** If it changes the predictor itself (a neighbourhood size, a depth, a penalty, a degree), look for an optimum by cross-validation. If it only averages more copies of the same kind of predictor, expect a monotone curve that levels off.
- **Do not tune the number of trees in a random forest as if it could overfit.** Add trees until the validation error stops improving by an amount you care about; more trees cost compute, not accuracy.
- **Estimate the floor of an ensemble.** Compare the variance of one member with that of many to estimate $\rho$. If the ensemble is already near $\rho\sigma^2$, adding members is wasted compute; reduce the correlation between members instead.
- **Tune the members of an ensemble, not only its size.** A forest of fully grown trees on a smooth, low-dimensional target may be beaten by a simpler model; the per-tree knobs are where its bias–variance trade lives.
- **Use a simulation to understand a knob.** On a synthetic version of your problem, the decomposition shows which term a knob moves, which cross-validation on real data cannot.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Squared error only.** The clean additive split into bias, variance and noise belongs to squared error. For 0-1 loss there are several competing decompositions, and in none of them does variance simply add.
- **One target, one sample size.** Theory says that with more training points the k-NN U should move to larger $k$, and with a rougher target to smaller $k$; neither was measured here. The forest's floor depends on the target too. The shapes, not the numbers, are the finding.
- **The decomposition needs many training sets.** On real data there is one. The bootstrap gives a rough stand-in, but it estimates variance from resamples that overlap heavily, and bias cannot be computed at all without the true function.
- **Not the whole story past interpolation.** A 1-nearest-neighbour fit reproduces its training labels exactly, a fully grown tree those of its bootstrap sample, and both sit at the high-variance end here. Section 6.5 shows a family of interpolating models whose error falls again as they grow, which the U has no room for.
- **Forest defaults.** scikit-learn's regression forest considers every feature at each split by default. With one input feature here that makes no difference, but on wide data the setting changes $\rho$, and with it the floor.

## Key points


1. For squared error, bias squared, variance and noise add up exactly to the expected test error; here they matched the measured error to within 0.003.
2. $k$ in k-NN trades bias for variance, and test error formed a U with its bottom at $k = 8$.
3. Adding trees to a random forest kept bias near zero, moving only within simulation error, and lowered variance every time, from 0.160 to 0.075. There was no U.
4. The variance of an average of correlated models has a floor of $\rho\sigma^2$; for this forest $\rho = 0.46$. Lower it by decorrelating the members, not by adding more.
5. Before drawing a U, ask whether the knob changes the predictor or only averages copies of it.

## Exercises


1. Pick a model you tune and the knob you tune it with. On a simulated version of your problem, with the same numbers of rows and features and a target you choose, run the decomposition: 100 training sets, predictions at fixed test points, bias squared and variance averaged over them. Does your knob draw a U, or only a descending curve?
2. Do the same for the number of members in an ensemble you use, and estimate $\rho$ from the variance of one member and of many. Is the ensemble already close to its floor?
3. Explain why averaging more trees cannot increase the bias of a random forest, and why considering fewer features at each split can.
4. Explain why the variance of a 1-nearest-neighbour regression is close to the noise variance $\sigma^2$ when the target is smooth and the training points are dense.

## Further reading


- Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat, "Neural networks and the bias/variance dilemma", *Neural Computation* (1992): the classic statement of the bias–variance dilemma for learning machines.
- Breiman, "Random forests", *Machine Learning* (2001): introduces random forests and bounds a forest's error by the mean correlation between its trees, the infinite-forest form of the floor in this section.
- Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, *The Elements of Statistical Learning* (2009), sections 7.3 and 15.2: the decomposition, and the variance of an average of correlated trees.

# From Section 6.5 (episode 10): The Second Descent Is Real, and a Ridge Penalty Makes It Unnecessary

## In practice


- **Do not fear parameter count; ask about regularisation.** A model with more parameters than rows is not overfitted by that fact alone. What matters is whether it is regularised, implicitly or explicitly.
- **Avoid the threshold when fitting without a penalty.** If an unpenalised fit has about as many free parameters as rows, expect it to be at its worst. Move away from $p = n$, or add a penalty.
- **Prefer an explicit penalty to a second descent.** Here a ridge penalty taken from a small grid of five values, with about $p = n$ features, beat both the small model and the model with twenty times as many features as rows, at a fraction of the second model's size.
- **Record the weight norm alongside the error.** A peak in test error accompanied by a peak in the norm is the interpolation threshold; a peak in one without the other means something else is going on.
- **Tune the penalty for each model size** by cross-validation rather than using one fixed value across sizes; the best size moves with the penalty.

## Limits of the evidence


- **The penalty was picked from a grid, not tuned.** Five values of λ, the largest 10. Where each is best moves with it: λ = 1 does best at $p = 40$ (0.212), λ = 10 around $p = n$. A larger λ, or one chosen by cross-validation for each $p$, might do better still and put its best elsewhere. The claim needs only one penalty that beats both descents, and λ = 10 is one.
- **Random features, not trained networks.** Here the features are fixed and only the last layer is fitted, which is what makes the minimum-norm solution computable. In trained networks double descent has been reported in width, in training time and in the number of samples, with the same role for label noise, but the implicit regularisation of gradient descent is harder to pin down than a pseudo-inverse.
- **"Never wins" is about these settings.** Theory gives cases where the overparameterised minimum-norm fit beats every underparameterised one, for instance when the signal is spread over many weak directions and the extra features add regularisation the small model lacks. The claim here is narrower: on this target, at these sizes and noise levels, it did not.
- **Medians, not means.** Near the threshold some training sets give errors far above the median, because the smallest singular value of the feature matrix is nearly zero. A mean would make the peak taller still; the median is the more conservative summary.
- **One kind of input.** Gaussian inputs, ReLU features, a smooth target. The threshold at $p = n$ is general for linear fits on fixed features; the heights are not.

## Key points


1. For least squares on random features, test error peaked at $p = n$, for 50, 100 and 200 rows alike, and fell again beyond it.
2. The peak needs something the fit must interpolate but cannot represent. Label noise is one source; a target outside the features' reach is another. Without any label noise the peak was still 23.3.
3. In every setting measured, the best model below the threshold beat the model with twenty times as many features as rows: the second descent was a recovery, not an improvement.
4. A ridge penalty removed the peak. At λ = 10 the best models sat around $p = n$, from 40 to 150 features, with error down to 0.162, better than anything the unpenalised fit reached.
5. Double descent is a reason not to fear large models that are regularised, implicitly or explicitly. It is not a reason to expect that adding parameters to an unregularised fit will help.

## Exercises


1. Run the random-features experiment on your own problem: take your inputs, draw $p$ random ReLU features for a range of $p$ around your number of rows, and fit with the pseudo-inverse. Find the peak, and record the weight norm beside the test error.
2. Refit with ridge at a few values of λ chosen by cross-validation, and compare the best penalised model with the best model on each side of the threshold.
3. If a production model of yours interpolates its training data, decide which side of a threshold it sits on, and whether a penalty would do the same job with less capacity.
4. Explain, using the singular values of the feature matrix, why the minimum-norm fit is worst at $p = n$ and why a ridge penalty removes the peak.

## Further reading


- Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal, "Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias-variance trade-off", *PNAS* (2019): names double descent and documents it across model families.
- Hastie, Montanari, Rosset and Tibshirani, "Surprises in high-dimensional ridgeless least squares interpolation", *Annals of Statistics* (2022): an exact analysis of the minimum-norm least-squares fit and its risk as the number of parameters passes the number of rows.
- Nakkiran et al., "Optimal regularization can mitigate double descent", *ICLR* (2021): proves that an optimally tuned ridge penalty removes the peak in certain linear regression settings.
