# From Section 14.1 (episode 12): Impurity Importance Rewards Features With Many Places to Split

## In practice


- **Do not read the default importance chart as a ranking of what matters.** `feature_importances_` in scikit-learn's forests is MDI, computed on the training rows. Treat it as a description of where the trees split, not of what the model needs.
- **Add noise columns as a baseline.** Add one standard-normal column and one fair-coin column before fitting. Any real feature that ranks below the continuous noise column has an impurity importance you cannot distinguish from nothing.
- **Use permutation importance on held-out rows.** Compute it on data the forest was not trained on, and report its uncertainty across repeats or resamples.
- **Be most suspicious where features differ in cardinality.** When binary flags sit beside continuous measurements, identifiers, timestamps or high-cardinality codes, expect MDI to favour the latter. Check whether identifier-like columns should be in the model at all.
- **Constrain the trees if you must use MDI.** A minimum leaf size reduces the bias, but it did not remove it here; do not treat it as a correction.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Permutation importance needs held-out rows.** Computed on the training data, permutation importance should inherit much of the same bias, since the forest has memorised the noise feature's values there and shuffling them hurts. That version was not run here; the fresh rows are what make the numbers above honest.
- **Categorical coding matters.** Here the categories are coded as integers, which is what scikit-learn's forests see. *One-hot coding*, which gives each level its own binary column, would change which columns MDI favours; whether it removes the preference for many split points was not tested here.
- **Corrected MDI exists.** Out-of-bag versions of impurity importance, which score each tree's splits on the rows left out of its bootstrap resample (the *out-of-bag* rows, the subject of Section 7.2), and the debiased MDI of Li et al. (2019) score splits on rows the tree did not train on, which is designed to remove most of this bias. They are not what the default `feature_importances_` attribute reports.
- **Twenty datasets per setting.** The counts quoted are per dataset, and they are not all unanimous. With no signal, MDI rose with the number of distinct values in all 20 datasets. With signal, it ranked the binary feature last in 19 of 20 and first in none; permutation importance ranked it first in 95%. With leaves of 20 the picture is mixed: MDI ranked the binary feature first in 25% of datasets and last in 5%. The mean shares carry standard errors of at most 0.004 with fully grown trees and up to 0.022 with leaves of 20.

## Key points


1. Impurity importance (MDI) is computed on the training data and credits every split. Features with more distinct values get more chances to split, and more credit.
2. With no signal anywhere, MDI gave a continuous feature 42% of the importance and a binary one 5%, in order of distinct values in all 20 datasets.
3. With a real effect on the binary feature alone, MDI never ranked that feature first and ranked it last in 19 of 20 datasets; permutation importance on fresh data ranked it first in 95%.
4. Larger leaves reduced the bias; they did not remove it. MDI ranked the informative feature first in only 25% of datasets with leaves of at least 20 rows.
5. On data with strong signals the bias barely shows. It matters most where importance charts are most tempting: weak effects, mixed feature types, deep trees.

## Exercises


1. Add two columns to your training data: one of standard-normal noise and one of fair coin flips. Refit your forest and look at where they land in its `feature_importances_`. Every real feature ranked below the noise column is one whose impurity importance you cannot distinguish from nothing.
2. Then compute permutation importance on a held-out set and compare the two rankings. Where they disagree, find out how many distinct values each disputed feature takes.
3. Explain why a binary feature can contribute to MDI at most once on any path from the root, while a continuous feature can contribute many times, and why this matters more for fully grown trees than for trees with large leaves.

## Further reading


- Strobl, Boulesteix, Zeileis and Hothorn, "Bias in random forest variable importance measures", *BMC Bioinformatics* (2007): the null design with features of different cardinality that this section reproduces.
- Breiman, "Random forests", *Machine Learning* (2001): introduces random forests and permutation importance.
- Li et al., "A debiased MDI feature importance measure for random forests", *NeurIPS* (2019): a corrected impurity importance that scores splits on rows the tree did not train on.

# From Section 14.2 (episode 16): Permutation Importance Measures the Model, Not What the Data Need

## In practice


- **Say which question an importance number answers.** Permutation importance is "reliance of this fitted model on the column", not "importance to the outcome".
- **Compute drop-column importance for the top features.** For the handful of features that matter to a decision, refit without each and compare with its permutation importance. Where the two disagree by more than a factor of two, look for other columns that carry the same information.
- **Look for near-copies before ranking.** Compute the correlation matrix of the inputs, group features above a threshold, and report importance per group, by joint permutation or by refitting without the group.
- **Check how many shuffled rows are realistic.** For the strongest correlated pairs, plot the pair before and after shuffling and count the shuffled rows outside the real cloud. If the share is large, consider conditional permutation or grouping.
- **Do not prune on a low or negative permutation importance alone.** A correlated partner may be covering for the feature; removing both may cost as much as the feature was ever worth.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Refitting answers its own question, with its own distortion.** Drop-column importance measures what the *data* lose without a feature, given the model class. It is expensive, needing one refit per feature, and it assigns zero to every member of a group of near-copies, which is equally misleading in the other direction. The honest unit is often the group.
- **$R^2$ on 133 test patients is noisy.** The numbers are averages of three splits, and single splits differ a lot: test $R^2$ ranged from 0.26 to 0.42, and the loss from refitting without bmi from -0.07 to +0.13. The direction held in every split, since permuting bmi always cost more than refitting without it, but the size of the drop-column numbers, in the text and in the table, is uncertain to about a tenth of $R^2$.
- **Other models distribute reliance differently.** A linear model with an exact copy has no unique coefficients at all, and neither does a lasso: any division of the coefficient between the two columns with the same sign has the same penalty, so the answer depends on the solver. With near-copies, Section 6.3 found the lasso's choice between the two varying from fit to fit. Permutation importance inherits whatever the fitting procedure did.
- **A copy is the extreme case.** At correlation 0.8 the forest still preferred the original and the copy's permutation importance was near zero; at 0.95 the copy started to take a share, and the original still dominated. Most real pairs sit somewhere in between, and so does the distortion.

## Key points


1. Permutation importance measures how much one fitted model leans on a column, not how much the outcome depends on it or how much the data would lose without it.
2. On the diabetes data, shuffling bmi cost 0.168 of test $R^2$; refitting the forest without it cost 0.043.
3. With an exact copy, the two columns' permutation importances summed to 0.038, a quarter of bmi's alone. Exact copies hide each other; at a correlation of 0.8 the effect had not yet appeared.
4. Shuffling a correlated feature invents rows: 29% of shuffled (s1, s2) pairs lay outside the region holding 99% of real ones.
5. Group correlated features before computing importance, and report what is lost when the group is removed, not each member's share.

## Exercises


1. Compute permutation importance for one of your models and, for its top five features, the drop-column importance when each is removed and the model refitted. Where the two disagree by more than a factor of two, list the features most correlated with the one in question.
2. Pick your most strongly correlated pair of features, shuffle one of them in a copy of your test set, and plot the pair before and after. Count the shuffled rows that lie outside the real cloud. That is the share of the importance score computed on data no one has seen.
3. Explain in words why the permutation importances of a feature and its exact copy need not add up to the importance the feature had alone, and why the drop-column importance of the pair removed together does not suffer from the same problem.

## Further reading


- Efron et al. (2004): the source of the diabetes data used in this section.
- Breiman, "Random forests", *Machine Learning* (2001): introduces random forests and the permutation measure of variable importance.
- Strobl et al., "Conditional variable importance for random forests", *BMC Bioinformatics* (2008): shows how correlated predictors distort permutation importance and proposes conditional permutation.
- Hooker, Mentch and Zhou, "Unrestricted permutation forces extrapolation", *Statistics and Computing* (2021): argues that permute-and-predict methods evaluate models off the data and reviews alternatives.

# From Section 14.3 (episode 17): Partial Dependence Asks the Model About Rows That Do Not Exist

## In practice


- **Check correlations before reading a partial dependence plot.** For the feature plotted, find the feature it is most correlated with. If the correlation is high, treat the PDP as a description of the model off the data, not of the data.
- **Draw the support check.** Scatter the pair and overlay the rows the PDP evaluates at its lowest and highest grid values. Report the share that falls inside the real cloud alongside the plot.
- **Use ALE when features are correlated.** Twenty quantile bins are a reasonable start. Overlay ALE and the PDP; where they disagree, the PDP is reporting the model off the data.
- **Do not take ICE curves as a fix.** They show heterogeneity across rows but evaluate the same off-data rows.
- **Treat any plot of one feature as incomplete when effects interact.** If the effect of one feature depends on another, a single curve averages over that dependence; look at two-feature plots instead.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Individual conditional expectation curves inherit the problem.** ICE plots show each row's curve $v \mapsto \hat f(v, x_{i,C})$ instead of their average. That reveals heterogeneity between rows, but it evaluates the same off-band rows.
- **Two features is the easy case.** In ten dimensions, "off the data" is the normal state of a modified row, and checking a two-dimensional scatter will not reveal it. ALE's local moves are the safer default whenever features are correlated at all.
- **The truth here is additive.** With an interaction between $x_1$ and $x_2$, the effect of $x_1$ depends on $x_2$ and no single curve captures it. ALE's second-order plots and the functional-ANOVA decomposition, which splits a function into main effects and interaction terms, address that; one-dimensional plots do not.
- **Three datasets per setting.** Single datasets move the slopes by up to about a tenth; the forest's ALE slope at the highest correlation ranged from 0.83 to 1.03. In every dataset, though, the PDP's slope fell as the correlation rose and stayed far below the ALE's.

## Key points


1. A partial dependence plot sets a feature to each grid value in every row and averages the predictions, including over combinations the data never contain.
2. With $y = x_1 + 2x_2^2$ and $x_1$, $x_2$ correlated at 0.985, a forest's PDP showed a slope of 0.30 for an effect of slope 1. ALE showed 0.92.
3. With independent inputs both methods were within a few hundredths of the truth. The PDP's failure grows with the correlation.
4. The PDP is an accurate picture of the model in regions with no data; ALE is a picture of the model where the data are.
5. Check the correlation of a feature with the others before reading its PDP. If it is high, use ALE.

## Exercises


1. Pick a feature whose partial dependence plot you have shown someone. Scatter it against the feature it is most correlated with and draw, on the same axes, the vertical lines of rows the PDP evaluates at the lowest and highest grid values. How many of those rows lie inside the cloud?
2. Compute ALE for the same feature, with twenty quantile bins, and overlay the two curves. Where they disagree, the PDP was reporting the model off the data.
3. Explain in words why a forest's partial dependence curve for $x_1$ flattens when $x_2$ closely tracks $x_1$ and carries most of the signal, and why ALE's construction avoids this.
4. Describe a case in which what the model does off the data is the question you need answered, and say which of the two plots you would then use.

## Further reading


- Friedman, "Greedy function approximation", *Annals of Statistics* (2001): introduces gradient boosting and the partial dependence plot.
- Apley and Zhu, "Visualizing the effects of predictor variables in black box supervised learning models", *JRSS B* (2020): defines accumulated local effects and compares them with partial dependence.
- Hooker, Mentch and Zhou, "Unrestricted permutation forces extrapolation", *Statistics and Computing* (2021): the general argument that methods which edit real rows evaluate models off the data.

# From Section 14.4 (episode 18): A SHAP Value Is an Answer to "Compared With What?"

## In practice


- **Name the background in every explanation.** Write "compared with the average benign tumour, this one's concavity added …", not "concavity was the most important feature".
- **Choose the background from the question.** Use the cases the person asking regards as normal: unflagged cases for an individual who was flagged, the whole population for a question about the model's behaviour overall.
- **Prefer a sample of real rows to a single row of means.** The means shortcut has a base value close to the default and can still flip many signs.
- **Show two backgrounds when an explanation matters.** Report agreement in the top features as evidence of robustness, and disagreement as part of the answer.
- **State the scale and the definition.** Say whether attributions are in log-odds or probability, and whether they are interventional or conditional.
- **Do not infer individual explanations from global bar charts.** A stable ranking of mean absolute values says nothing about any one prediction.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Five features is a toy for enumeration.** Exact computation costs $2^p$ model evaluations per background row. Practical explainers sample coalitions or exploit the structure of trees; their answers approximate the same quantities and inherit the same dependence on the background.
- **Log-odds, not probabilities.** Attributions computed on predicted probabilities are different numbers from those computed on log-odds, and libraries differ in which they report. Everything here is in log-odds. The dependence on the background is part of the definition on either scale, but its size was measured on this one only.
- **One model, one split.** A different model fitted to the same data could rank the features differently for the same tumour even with the background fixed. That was not measured here; Section 1.2 shows that models of near-equal accuracy can disagree on the predictions themselves, which is a reason to expect it. Section 22.2's rerun on other splits narrowed the benign background's effect on the top feature to between 33% and 53% of tumours.
- **Global summaries average the problem away.** Mean absolute Shapley values, the usual "SHAP importance" bar chart, rank the five features in the same order under all four backgrounds, while their sizes move by up to 49% and the leading feature of individual explanations changes for up to 58% of tumours. That stability of the ranking is real and says nothing about any one prediction.

## Key points


1. Shapley values are the unique fair split of the difference between a prediction and a reference. The reference is a choice, and the theorem does not make it.
2. Computed exactly, the values added up to within 7e-15, and switching the background from all tumours to benign ones changed the top feature for 58% of tumours on this split (33% to 53% on five others).
3. The feature-means shortcut kept the base value almost unchanged and still flipped 19% of attribution signs.
4. Interventional and conditional definitions answer different questions; with correlated features they disagree too.
5. State the background in every explanation you show.

## Exercises


1. Take one prediction your model made that someone questioned. Compute its SHAP values against your default background, then against a background of rows from the class or group the person had in mind as "normal". Compare the top three features in each.
2. If the top features in Exercise 1 differ, the explanation you gave answered a question the person did not ask. Write down, in one sentence, what the reference was for the explanation you would now give.
3. Explain why, for a nonlinear model, a background consisting of a single row of feature means can give a base value close to that of the full training set and still produce different attributions.
4. Describe a situation in which a conditional Shapley value would give credit to a feature the model never reads, and say whether that is what the person asking for the explanation would want.

## Further reading


- Shapley, "A value for n-person games" (1953): the original definition of the value and the uniqueness theorem.
- Lundberg and Lee, "A unified approach to interpreting model predictions", *NeurIPS* (2017): brings Shapley values to model explanation as SHAP.
- Janzing, Minorics and Bloebaum, "Feature relevance quantification in explainable AI: a causal problem", *AISTATS* (2020): argues for the interventional definition when explaining what a model does.
- Sundararajan and Najmi, "The many Shapley values for model explanation", *ICML* (2020): catalogues the variants of Shapley-based attribution and where they disagree.

# From Section 14.5 (episode 19): A Planted Shortcut Wins the Test Set and Loses Everywhere Else

## In practice


- **List the features that describe how a row was collected.** Source system, site, device, batch, timestamp, file format, or an ID that encodes any of these. Find where each falls in the importance ranking.
- **Treat a surprising top feature as a finding.** If a feature that should not matter to the task ranks near the top, investigate before shipping.
- **Score on slices where the suspected shortcut is broken.** Split the test set by collection variables and score each part separately; if one part is much worse, find out what the model is using on the other.
- **Keep an external test set.** Data from another site, device or period is the only evidence that can separate a model that learned the task from one that learned the collection process.
- **Do not let a larger or repeated random split stand in for a different source.** It narrows the error bar around the same target.

## Limits of the evidence


- **A planted shortcut is obvious once named.** One lit pixel is easy to find. Real shortcuts are spread over many features, such as texture, background or image statistics, and importance methods find them only partly, which is why external validation matters more than any explanation.
- **A forest, not a network.** Deep networks are often argued to be, if anything, more prone to shortcuts, because they tend to pick up the simplest predictive features first; Geirhos and colleagues review the evidence. No network was trained here. The direction of the result should not depend on the model; its size will.
- **The shortcut is perfectly stable here.** In practice a shortcut's reliability drifts, as when a hospital changes scanners, and a model that leaned on it fails gradually, which is harder to notice than a collapse.
- **Three splits.** The scores are means over three half splits of about 900 test images each, so differences of a few thousandths are within noise. That includes the 50% marker's gain on its own test set; the other gaps quoted are several times larger.

## Key points


1. A held-out set drawn like the training set rewards any feature that predicts the label in both, including shortcuts.
2. With a marker pixel on 90% of four classes, the shortcut model beat a clean model on the test set (0.986 to 0.971) and lost on clean images (0.947), on the marked classes (0.895 to 0.954) and badly when the markers moved (0.663).
3. The more reliable the shortcut, the higher the test score and the lower the clean score.
4. Permutation importance ranked a marker pixel first of 64. A feature that should not matter at the top of an importance chart is a finding, not a curiosity.
5. Evaluate on data collected differently, from another site, device or period, before believing that a better score is a better model.

## Exercises


1. List the features of your model that describe how a row was *collected* rather than what it *is*: source system, site, device, batch, timestamp, file format, an ID that encodes any of these. Check where each falls in your permutation-importance ranking.
2. Split your test set by the collection feature most likely to change in deployment, and score the model on each part separately. If one part is much worse, find out what the model is using on the other.
3. Explain why a larger test set, or repeated cross-validation, would make the shortcut model in this section look more convincingly better, not less.
4. In the control row (rate 0%), the clean model's score did not change when markers were placed on the wrong classes. Explain why, and what that row establishes about the other rows.

- Zech et al., "Variable generalization performance of a deep learning model to detect pneumonia in chest radiographs", *PLOS Medicine* (2018): a pneumonia detector whose performance varied across hospitals, partly because it picked up which hospital an X-ray came from.
