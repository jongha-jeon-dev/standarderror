# Closing material for Chapter 11: Probabilities and Calibration

# From Section 11.1 (episode 5): Resampling Moves the Threshold and Breaks a Logistic Model's Probabilities

## In practice


- **Separate ranking, probabilities and decisions when evaluating a correction.** Report AUC or average precision for the ranking, calibration or log loss for the probabilities, and precision and recall at the operating point for the decisions.
- **Move the threshold before resampling.** If the goal is more recall, lower the plain model's threshold until it reaches the recall you need. Here that matched every correction's precision or beat it, without retraining.
- **Choose the threshold from costs.** A threshold near the prevalence corresponds to weighting a miss like many false alarms; make that weighting explicit and set the threshold from it.
- **Check what consumes the probabilities.** If a resampled or reweighted logistic model feeds a dashboard, an expected-cost calculation or another model, compare its mean prediction with the true rate, and apply the prior correction or retrain on the data as it comes.
- **For flexible models, measure rather than assume.** On boosted trees the corrections lowered the ranking but improved under-confident probabilities. Check calibration before deciding either way.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Severe imbalance with tiny counts.** With about 100 positives the plain model is well estimated. With ten, every method is noisy, and some regularisation from reweighting can help a badly overfitted model; the fix there is regularisation or more positives, not balance.
- **Models that ignore the threshold.** Some pipelines take the argmax and have no threshold to move, such as a deep network's softmax inside a larger system. There reweighting is the only lever available, and it is the threshold in disguise; it should still be corrected before the probabilities are used.
- **Losses that are not proper.** A *proper* loss, such as log loss, is one minimised by reporting the true probabilities. The comparison assumes the model is trained on log loss. Trained to maximise accuracy directly, a model on imbalanced data can genuinely learn to ignore the minority class, and reweighting then changes what it learns.
- **One simulated task.** The tree result may depend on how closely the trees can fit individual rows; their regularisation was not varied here. The direction, no gain in ranking, held in all three settings measured.

## Key points


1. Separate ranking, probabilities and decisions. Imbalance corrections change the last two and, at best, leave the first alone.
2. On logistic regression at 2% prevalence, oversampling, SMOTE and class weights changed AUC by about -0.0012; undersampling by -0.0039. The recall they gain at 0.5 the plain model gets with a threshold near the prevalence (0.021), at the same precision or better.
3. On logistic regression they inflate predicted probabilities about 11-fold, and the prior correction undoes it.
4. On boosted trees they made the ranking worse: about -0.01 AUC, and 0.046 of average precision for SMOTE. The plain trees' probabilities were too low (mean 0.008), and oversampling and class weights improved them; check calibration before assuming either way.
5. Imbalance is not a defect in the data. It is a cost question, and a threshold is where costs belong.

## Exercises


1. Find a model in your stack trained on resampled or reweighted data. Train the same model on the data as it comes, and compare the two on the same test rows: AUC, average precision, and precision at the recall you actually operate at. If the plain model matches or beats the corrected one at that recall, the correction was a threshold; replace it with one.
2. Look at what consumes the corrected model's probabilities. If anything treats them as probabilities, such as a dashboard, an expected-cost calculation or another model, check its mean prediction against the true rate. If it is several times too high, either apply the prior correction or retrain without the resampling.
3. Explain why adding the same constant to every row's log-odds leaves the ROC AUC unchanged but changes the recall at a fixed threshold of 0.5.
4. Explain why the prior correction restored a resampled logistic model's probabilities but made the resampled boosted trees' probabilities worse.

## Further reading


- Chawla et al., "SMOTE: synthetic minority over-sampling technique", *JAIR* (2002): the original description of SMOTE.
- Elkan, "The foundations of cost-sensitive learning", *IJCAI* (2001): how misclassification costs translate into a decision threshold, and into rebalancing of the training data.
- Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker, "Adjusting the outputs of a classifier to new a priori probabilities", *Neural Computation* (2002): the prior correction used in this section, and how to apply it when class priors change.
- van den Goorbergh et al., "The harm of class imbalance corrections for risk prediction models", *JAMIA* (2022): the clinical study finding that imbalance corrections improved no discrimination measure and overestimated risks.

# From Section 11.2 (episode 43): Naive Bayes Counts Correlated Evidence Twice, and Only Its Probabilities Notice

## In practice


- **Do not read naive Bayes probabilities as probabilities.** Check log loss and the share of predictions above 0.99 or below 0.01, and count the errors among them.
- **Use it for what the defence covers.** Its sign and order survive correlated features; it is a reasonable fast baseline for classification and ranking.
- **Recalibrate before using its scores downstream.** Fit Platt scaling on out-of-fold log-odds; it repairs the scale without changing the ranking.
- **Look for families of redundant columns.** Compute within-class correlations and find groups that measure the same thing. Naive Bayes weights each group by its column count, so the feature set itself sets the weights.
- **Do not expect decorrelation to rescue it.** Whitening across all rows is not independence within classes.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One data set with heavy redundancy.** Breast cancer is close to the worst case for double-counting. With features that really are close to independent within class, naive Bayes' probabilities are much better, as the naive Bayes model on Section 11.3's simulated task, already close to calibrated, showed.
- **Gaussian naive Bayes only.** The multinomial and Bernoulli versions used for text double-count in the same way, through words that co-occur, and are calibrated no better.
- **Copies are extreme.** Exact duplicates make the scaling exact. Real correlated features double-count partially and unevenly, which is the reweighting case rather than the scaling one.
- **Five-fold cross-validation on 569 tumours.** Differences of a point of accuracy are within noise; the factor of eight in log loss and the share of confident errors are not.

## Key points


1. On breast cancer, naive Bayes was 0.041 less accurate than logistic regression and had 8 times its log loss; 67% of its errors came at above 0.99 confidence.
2. Copying every feature $k$ times multiplies the log-odds by $k$: the AUC of the log-odds was unchanged to four decimal places, and log loss rose to 1.789 at $k$ = 10.
3. Copying one family reweights the evidence and changes the answers: 0.914 with the size features copied ten times, 0.950 with texture copied five.
4. Recalibrating the log-odds cut log loss from 0.611 to 0.149 with accuracy unchanged.
5. Use naive Bayes for ranking and quick baselines; recalibrate its log-odds before reading them as probabilities, and remember that its weights are set by how many columns each kind of evidence occupies.

## Exercises


1. Train naive Bayes on a data set you know and plot a histogram of its predicted probabilities, then the same for logistic regression. Count the predictions above 0.99 and below 0.01, and the errors among them.
2. Compute the correlation matrix of your features within one class and find the largest group of strongly correlated columns. Remove all but one column of that group, retrain, and compare log loss and accuracy with the full model.
3. Using the log-odds formula, explain why duplicating every feature cannot change naive Bayes' AUC computed from the log-odds, but duplicating one feature can.

## Further reading


- Domingos and Pazzani, "On the optimality of the simple Bayesian classifier under zero-one loss", *Machine Learning* (1997): why naive Bayes can classify optimally even when its independence assumption fails.
- Zhang, "The optimality of naive Bayes", *FLAIRS* (2004): a further analysis of when dependent features leave naive Bayes' classifications intact.
- Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, "Predicting good probabilities with supervised learning", *ICML* (2005): an empirical comparison of calibration across model families, naive Bayes among them.

# From Section 11.3 (episode 34): Recalibration Fixes the Average, Given Enough Rows

## In practice


- **Check calibration before correcting it.** Compute log loss and a calibration curve on held-out data first. A model that is already close to calibrated is likely to be made worse by recalibration on a small set.
- **Use Platt scaling on small calibration sets.** Here it helped an overconfident model from 50 rows; isotonic regression hurt at 50 and needed 100. Reserve isotonic regression for calibration sets of thousands of rows.
- **Judge a recalibration by log loss on rows it was not fitted on.** Split the held-out data, fit on one part and score on the other; keep the correction only if log loss improves.
- **Expect isotonic regression to cost some ranking.** If the AUC or the ordering of rows matters, prefer a monotone parametric map, or check the AUC after isotonic regression.
- **Check calibration within the groups that matter.** Plot the calibration curve separately by region, product, age band or any other variable that bears on the decision. If a group is off while the overall curve is fine, recalibrating the score will not help; give the model the information that distinguishes the group.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One task, two models.** The row counts at which recalibration starts to help depend on how badly calibrated the model is and how noisy the task is. What transfers is the order: Platt scaling before isotonic regression on small sets, and checking before correcting.
- **Calibration sets drawn fresh.** In practice the calibration rows are often a slice of the training data, or the validation set already used for tuning, which adds its own optimism.
- **Expected calibration error is binned.** It used ten equal-width bins; with other binning the numbers move a little. Log loss, which needs no bins, tells the same story here.
- **Two groups, known.** Real subgroups are many, overlapping and often unrecorded. Calibration within the groups one can name is a lower bound on the problem.

## Key points


1. Recalibration is a fitted model, with variance. On 50 rows Platt scaling cut an overconfident model's log loss from 0.748 to 0.612; isotonic regression raised it to 0.761.
2. Recalibrating a model that is already calibrated makes it worse until the calibration set is large: 1000 rows for Platt scaling here, more than 5,000 for isotonic regression.
3. Platt scaling leaves the ranking untouched; isotonic regression lowered the AUC at every size.
4. Calibration is an average. A model calibrated to 0.005 overall was off by 0.19 in a group holding 30% of the rows.
5. Check calibration within the groups that matter, and fix a group's miscalibration with information, not with a correction of the score.

## Exercises


1. Take a classifier whose probabilities you use, and a held-out set. Plot its calibration curve overall, then separately for each value of a variable that matters to the decision (region, product, age band), whether or not the model uses it. If the overall curve is close to the diagonal and a group's is not, explain why recalibrating the score will not help that group.
2. Fit Platt scaling on a quarter of your held-out rows and check, on the rest, whether log loss improved at all. Repeat with isotonic regression and compare the AUC before and after each.
3. Explain why a strictly increasing map cannot change the AUC, and why isotonic regression can lower it.
4. Explain why recalibrating an already calibrated model on a small set is expected to make its log loss worse, and why the effect is larger for isotonic regression.

## Further reading


- Platt, "Probabilistic outputs for support vector machines" (1999): the original logistic map from a classifier's score to a probability.
- Zadrozny and Elkan, "Transforming classifier scores into accurate multiclass probability estimates", *KDD* (2002): isotonic regression as a calibration method.
- Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, "Predicting good probabilities with supervised learning", *ICML* (2005): a comparison of calibration across model families, with the advice on calibration-set size.
- Hébert-Johnson et al., "Multicalibration", *ICML* (2018): calibration required to hold within every identifiable subgroup.
