# Closing material for Chapter 16: Pictures of High-Dimensional Data

# From Section 16.1 (episode 31): Principal Components Keep Variance, Not Relevance

## In practice


- **Decide on scaling before PCA and report it.** On raw breast-cancer features the first component was, in effect, the feature with the largest units. Standardise unless the features share meaningful units.
- **Put each component's variance share beside its relation to the target.** Compute both for every component. If a dropped component relates to the target more strongly than a kept one, the variance rule has cut in the wrong place.
- **Choose the number of components by cross-validation, not a threshold,** with the PCA fitted inside each training fold.
- **Compare with methods that see the target.** Fit partial least squares and a regularised model on all features. If the PCA pipeline does not beat them, the reduction is not earning its place.
- **Stop citing "95% of the variance" as a reason in a predictive pipeline.** It describes the inputs; keep it for compression, plotting and removing redundancy.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Linear structure only.** PCA, PLS and the models after them are linear here. A target that depends on a nonlinear function of the inputs can be invisible to every linear component.
- **Small data sets.** Thirty features and ten. With hundreds of features and few rows the variance ranking itself is noisy, and the case for some reduction before the model is stronger, of whichever kind.
- **One target per data set.** A component that does not predict this target can predict another. PCA's neutrality about the target is a cost for one prediction task and a benefit when the reduced features serve many.
- **The simulation is extreme by design.** It shows the mechanism, not its frequency. The diabetes result is the measure of how often it bites: once in four components, on a standard teaching data set.

## Key points


1. PCA ranks directions by the variance of the inputs, in their units, and never sees the target.
2. On raw breast-cancer data the first component held 98.2% of the variance and was led by worst area; the 95% rule kept 1 component raw and 10 standardised.
3. Variance is not relevance. On diabetes the fourth component explained 12.8% of the target, more than the second and third together.
4. Regression on three components reached a cross-validated $R^2$ of 0.349 and on four 0.479; partial least squares reached 0.479 with two.
5. Before a model, standardise deliberately, choose the number of components by cross-validated performance, and consider a method that sees the target.

## Exercises


1. Take a data set you have reduced with PCA before modelling. For each component, compute its share of the input variance and its correlation with the target, and list them side by side.
2. If a component outside the ones you kept has a larger correlation than one inside, refit with the number of components chosen by cross-validation and compare. Then fit the same model on all the features with a regularisation penalty, which often makes the reduction unnecessary.
3. Explain why, for independent features with different standard deviations, the principal components are the features themselves, and why this makes the constructed example fail at nine components.
4. Explain why rescaling one feature can change the first principal component but not the predictions of an unpenalised linear regression on all the features.

## Further reading


- Jolliffe, "A note on the use of principal components in regression", *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society C* (1982): examples from the literature in which low-variance components are the predictive ones.
- Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, *The Elements of Statistical Learning*, section 3.5: principal component regression and partial least squares as methods built on derived input directions.
- Efron, Hastie, Johnstone and Tibshirani (2004): the source of the diabetes data.
- Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) data, as bundled with scikit-learn: the thirty nuclear measurements used for the units comparison.

# From Section 16.2 (episode 56): A t-SNE Picture Keeps the Neighbours and Loses the Map

## In practice


- **Read a t-SNE plot for neighbourhoods only.** "These points are similar" is what the method preserves; how large a group is, or how far apart two groups are, is not.
- **Measure sizes and distances in the original space.** Compute within-group spread and distances between centroids in the data, and report those instead of the picture's.
- **Start from PCA.** Use the PCA initialisation (scikit-learn's default) whenever the global layout will be read at all. With random starts, the arrangement of classes changed with the seed.
- **Check any layout claim across seeds and perplexities.** Rerun with several seeds and at least two perplexities, including a large one; a claim that changes between runs was about the run.
- **Look at PCA beside t-SNE, and treat clusters found on t-SNE coordinates with care.** The linear picture keeps sizes and gaps along its directions; t-SNE keeps neighbourhoods. Clusters of the picture inherit its equalised densities and arbitrary gaps.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Gaussian clusters in ten dimensions.** Each simulation isolates one property. Real data mix them, and with continuous structure, a trajectory rather than groups, t-SNE can also cut one group into pieces, which was not measured here.
- **t-SNE only.** UMAP is now used as often and has a different objective. It is often said to keep more global structure, but its sizes and gaps are not distances either. It was not measured here.
- **One run per cell in the simulations.** The size ratios were within 0.05 of 1 at every perplexity, which is far from 10 by any standard of run-to-run noise. The gap ratios moved more with perplexity and would move with the seed.
- **Digits are easy.** Well-separated classes are the best case for clustering a picture. Overlapping classes are where the picture's gaps mislead most.

## Key points


1. A cluster with ten times the spread of another came out of t-SNE 0.99 to 1.01 times its size; a gap 9.8 times another came out 0.98 to 1.45 times as wide.
2. Two principal components kept the size and gap ratios close to the data's, so the distortions belong to t-SNE, not to the reduction to two dimensions.
3. On the digits t-SNE kept 59% of each point's ten nearest neighbours, against 12% for two principal components.
4. Started at random, t-SNE ranked the distances between classes less faithfully than PCA, and two seeds agreed on them as little as 0.50; started from PCA, every seed matched PCA's 0.81 and the layouts agreed.
5. Read a t-SNE plot for who sits next to whom. Measure sizes and distances in the data, start from PCA, and check any layout claim across seeds and perplexities.

## Exercises


1. Take a t-SNE or UMAP plot you have shown someone and the statement it supported: these two groups are close, this group is diffuse, this one is small. Compute that quantity in the original space: the distance between group centroids, the spread within each group, the count.
2. Rerun the same embedding with three seeds and two perplexities. Decide which of the statements from Exercise 1 survive both checks; those are statements about the data.
3. Using the perplexity-calibrated bandwidth $\sigma_i$, explain why two clusters with very different spreads are drawn at about the same size, and why a PCA start does not change that.

## Further reading


- van der Maaten and Hinton, "Visualizing data using t-SNE", *JMLR* (2008): the original description of the method and its Student t kernel in the low-dimensional space.
- Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson, "How to use t-SNE effectively", *Distill* (2016): interactive examples of misleading cluster sizes and distances, the warning this section quantifies.
- Kobak and Berens, "The art of using t-SNE for single-cell transcriptomics", *Nature Communications* (2019): practical recommendations, including large perplexities and PCA initialisation to preserve global structure.
