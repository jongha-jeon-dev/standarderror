# Closing material for Chapter 1: Scores, Error Bars and Comparisons

# From Section 1.1 (episode 1): Your Test Score Has an Error Bar, and It Is Wider Than Your Gain

## In practice


- **Put an error bar on every test score.** Compute $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$ for your test set and report the 95% interval with the score. If it is wider than the gap you are reporting, say so.
- **Do not use repeated random splits as a measure of generalisation uncertainty.** On one pool they mostly re-measure that pool's test-set noise. Use them to see how often a ranking flips, not to bound performance on new data.
- **Compare models on the same rows.** Score both on identical test rows or identical out-of-fold predictions, count the rows where only one is right, and use McNemar's exact test.
- **Do a power calculation before claiming an improvement.** If the rows-needed formula asks for more rows than you have, record the gap as not resolved on this data.
- **Check that test rows are independent.** If several rows come from one writer, user, session or document, the binomial interval is too narrow; Section 2.4 measures what happens when rows from the same people are treated as new data.

## Limits of the evidence


- **The binomial error bar assumes independent test rows.** If test rows share a source, such as several digits from one writer, several frames of one video or several sentences of one document, the effective sample is smaller and the error bar wider. The same caveat applies to any interval computed as if the rows were independent, such as the coverage of a prediction interval.
- **The pool-corrected spread is not wrong; it is a different quantity.** If the question is how much this dataset's split could move the score, the repeated splits answer it. If the question is how the model would do on new data, they understate the uncertainty and say almost nothing about the training-set component.
- **The rows-needed formula is an approximation.** It is the normal approximation to McNemar's test with the disagreement rate taken as known. At small disagreement counts it is optimistic; it is for planning, not testing.
- **Cross-validated predictions are not quite independent either.** Each image is predicted by a model trained on 90% of the others, and those models overlap. Dietterich's 1998 comparison of tests is the standard reference for what that does to the *type I error*, the rate of declaring a difference where there is none; McNemar's test on a single split is the conservative choice it recommends. Section 2.2 returns to this for t-tests on cross-validation folds.

## Key points


1. A test accuracy is a proportion with standard error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. On 360 images at 98%, the 95% interval is about ±1.4 points.
2. Repeated random splits of one dataset reproduce that spread with a finite-population correction. They measure the pool's test-set noise, not training-set variation and not the spread on new data.
3. Compare models on the same rows. Only disagreements count, and pairing shrinks the standard error of a difference (0.0040 to 0.0030 here).
4. 3-NN beats the RBF SVM on digits by 0.6 points with p = 0.06. Settling it would take about 3,381 images; the dataset has 1,797.
5. Before reporting an improvement, compute how many test rows it would take to detect it. If the answer is more than you have, the improvement is not yet a result.

## Exercises


1. Take the last model comparison you reported and redo it as a paired comparison: the same test rows, a count of the rows where only one model was right, and McNemar's p-value.
2. Plug the disagreement rate and the gap from Exercise 1 into the rows-needed formula. If the answer is larger than your test set, write the gap down as "not resolved on this data" and keep the comparison open.
3. Check whether your test rows are independent. If they share writers, users, sessions or documents, explain why the binomial error bar is then a floor rather than an estimate.
4. Explain in words why pairing reduces the standard error of a difference most when the two models make almost the same mistakes.

- Alpaydin and Kaynak (1998), UCI Machine Learning Repository: the source of the handwritten digits data used in this section.
- McNemar, "Note on the sampling error of the difference between correlated proportions or percentages", *Psychometrika* (1947): the original paired test on discordant counts.
- Dietterich, "Approximate statistical tests for comparing supervised classification learning algorithms", *Neural Computation* (1998): compares tests for classifier differences and warns against resampled t-tests.

# From Section 1.2 (episode 20): Same Accuracy, Different Answers

## In practice


- **Report disagreement with accuracy.** Retrain with several seeds, or fit several near-equal models, and report the largest pairwise disagreement and the share of rows that receive both labels.
- **Use the bounds to judge when it matters.** If the models' error rates are small, disagreement must be small too. If they are large, the bound leaves room for substantial disagreement; measure it.
- **Do not break ties by test score or by accident.** Among models within noise of each other, the choice is a choice of who gets which answer; make it deliberately or avoid it.
- **Average near-equal models where a single prediction is needed.** The ensemble no longer depends on one seed, and its probabilities on disputed rows sit nearer the middle.
- **Flag disputed rows in decisions about individuals.** Send them to review, a second model or a request for more information.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Ensembling reduces disagreement but does not resolve it.** Averaging the five networks would give one set of predictions that changes less with any one seed, as the forests show. It does not choose between the boundaries; it blurs them. On a disputed row the members' probabilities straddle 0.5, so their average is closer to 0.5 than the more confident of them, which is honest.
- **One hard task, simulated.** The networks are small, with light regularisation and at most 1,500 training iterations. Larger networks, more data or stronger regularisation would all be expected to change the amount of disagreement; none was tried here, and none can remove the bound, which is arithmetic.
- **Disagreement is not error.** On a row where two models disagree, one of them is right. The point is not that both are bad but that the test score cannot say which to trust on those rows, and the person affected cannot either.
- **The one-point tolerance is a choice.** With a tolerance of zero, no two models qualify; with five points, more would, and the largest disagreement among them could only grow or stay the same. One point is roughly the noise in an accuracy measured on 5,000 rows.

## Key points


1. Accuracy says how many rows a model gets right, not which. Two models can disagree on as many rows as the sum of their error rates.
2. On a task where the best possible rule errs on about 28% of test rows, five networks differing only in seed spanned 0.010 in accuracy and disagreed on up to 22.5% of rows; 43% of rows got both labels from some seed.
3. The two best model families, 0.002 apart in accuracy, disagreed on 9.4% of rows.
4. On breast cancer, with errors near 2%, near-equal models disagreed on 1.4%: rare errors leave no room.
5. On hard problems, report how much near-equal models disagree, and for decisions about individuals, consider averaging them or flagging the disputed rows.

## Exercises


1. Retrain your model five times with different seeds, changing nothing else, and score each on your test set. Count the test rows on which at least two of the five disagree, and the largest disagreement between any pair.
2. If the count in Exercise 1 is more than a few percent, look at who those rows are. They are the cases where your model's answer is an accident of training, and they are often the cases where the decision matters most.
3. Using the bounds in this section, explain why two models that are each right on almost every row cannot disagree much, while two models that are each wrong on a third of the rows can disagree on many.
4. A colleague proposes to choose between two models whose accuracies differ by less than the noise by picking the higher one. Describe what you would report instead, and what you would do about the rows on which they disagree.

## Further reading


- Breiman, "Statistical modeling: the two cultures", *Statistical Science* (2001): introduces the Rashomon effect, the multiplicity of models that fit equally well.
- Marx, Calmon and Ustun, "Predictive multiplicity in classification", *ICML* (2020): defines ambiguity and related measures of how often near-optimal models disagree.
- D'Amour et al., "Underspecification presents challenges for credibility in modern machine learning", *JMLR* (2022): shows that training pipelines with equal held-out scores can yield models that behave differently, and why that matters.
