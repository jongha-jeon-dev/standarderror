# Closing material for Chapter 18: Distance and Anomalies

# From Section 18.1 (episode 33): The Curse of Dimensionality Counts the Dimensions the Data Use

## In practice


- **Measure before blaming dimension.** For a sample of rows, compute each row's nearest distance divided by its median distance to the rest, and compare with uniform data in a range of dimensions. The matching uniform dimension is a rough guide to how many directions your data really use.
- **Do not rule out distance-based methods by column count.** On 64 correlated pixels 5-NN beat logistic regression.
- **Look for columns that carry nothing.** Irrelevant columns enter an unweighted distance on equal terms with informative ones. Remove them, down-weight them, or scale features so that the distance reflects what matters.
- **Do any selection inside the cross-validation folds.** Choosing columns on all the rows lets the test rows influence the choice.
- **When many columns may be irrelevant, use a method that weights or selects them.** Logistic regression, which learns per-column weights, tolerated 1,024 noise columns far better than an unweighted distance, and 5-NN after selection inside the folds did better still.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Uniform noise is the worst kind.** Real irrelevant features are often correlated with each other or nearly constant, and fill fewer dimensions than independent uniform columns; they hurt less.
- **Euclidean distance, unweighted.** Scaling or selecting features, learning a metric, or using a representation from a trained model all change what "near" means, and are the standard repairs. None was applied here, by design, apart from the selection step.
- **One data set with low intrinsic dimension.** Data that genuinely spread in many directions, such as some text or genomic data, can be nearly as hard as the uniform case. The column count does not say which case a data set is in; the distance ratio, or an intrinsic-dimension estimate, does.
- **$k = 5$, one split scheme.** Other choices of $k$ move the numbers a little and not the pattern.

## Key points


1. For points that fill their space, distances concentrate: the nearest-to-median ratio rose from 0.03 in 2 uniform dimensions to 0.94 in 1,024.
2. Real data rarely fill their columns. The digits' 64 pixels had the ratio of about 8 uniform dimensions, and 5-NN scored 0.986.
3. Irrelevant columns do fill their dimensions. With 1,024 noise columns 5-NN fell to 0.659; logistic regression, which weights columns, fell to 0.871.
4. Selecting 64 columns by F statistic inside the folds, with the noise still present, restored 5-NN to 0.982.
5. Before blaming dimension, measure the nearest-to-median distance ratio of your data, and remove or down-weight columns that carry nothing.

## Exercises


1. For a data set you use with a distance-based method, take 200 rows and compute, for each, its nearest distance over its median distance to the rest. Compute the same for uniform data with your number of columns and with a few smaller numbers, and find the uniform dimension that matches your data's ratio.
2. If the matching dimension in Exercise 1 is far below your column count, the curse is not your problem. If it is close, check how many of your columns actually carry information about the target, for instance with an F statistic computed inside each training fold.
3. Explain why the relative spread of Euclidean distances between uniform points shrinks as the dimension grows, and which assumption of that argument the digits' pixels violate.
4. Explain why logistic regression is less affected by appended noise columns than a nearest-neighbour classifier.

## Further reading


- Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan and Shaft, "When is 'nearest neighbor' meaningful?", *ICDT* (1999): the conditions under which nearest and farthest distances become indistinguishable.
- Levina and Bickel, "Maximum likelihood estimation of intrinsic dimension", *NeurIPS* (2004): estimating how many dimensions data actually occupy from nearest-neighbour distances.
- Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits, UCI Machine Learning Repository: the digits data, as bundled with scikit-learn.

# From Section 18.2 (episode 59): An Anomaly Score Ranks; the Contamination Setting Only Cuts

## In practice


- **Treat the score as the output and the flag as your decision.** Keep the full ranking; do not let `contamination` or `'auto'` choose how many rows are flagged.
- **Choose the cut from a budget or a labelled sample.** Flag as many rows as can be investigated, or label a sample and estimate precision at several cuts. Do not read the contamination setting as an estimate of the anomaly rate.
- **Ask whether anomalies could arrive in groups.** If one fault can produce many similar records, a local method needs a neighbourhood larger than the group; check results at several values of $k$, or compare with an isolation forest.
- **Select features before detecting.** Anomalies unusual in a few features are diluted by many irrelevant ones. Use the features in which an anomaly would be expected to show, and compare with a run on that subset.
- **Inspect the top of the ranking.** Look at the flagged rows and those just below the cut, preferably with someone who knows the data, before trusting either the score or the cut.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Planted anomalies.** The simulated anomalies were shifted versions of normal points, the easiest kind to define. Real anomalies include points that are individually ordinary and unusual only in combination or in sequence, which these detectors do not look for.
- **Scores on the training data.** Every score here was computed on the data the detector was fitted to, which is how the tools are commonly used. Fitting on clean data and scoring new data would presumably remove some masking, since the group would not be among the neighbours, and none of the feature problem; neither was measured.
- **Two detectors, default settings.** Other methods, such as one-class support vector machines or autoencoders, use distances or reconstructions differently and fail differently. The masking and irrelevant-feature effects were measured for these two.
- **Breast-cancer features are good ones.** Its 30 features were chosen by people who knew what malignancy looks like. Features chosen without that knowledge would be closer to the 200-feature simulation.

## Key points


1. The contamination parameter flagged exactly its own value; `'auto'` flagged 10.5% to 13.4% whatever the true rate. The ranking underneath scored an AUC of 0.99 or more throughout.
2. Fifty grouped anomalies looked normal to local scores until the neighbourhood was larger than the group; at $k = 20$ the distance score's AUC was 0.17, and on five other seeds its worst AUC stayed far below chance.
3. Anomalous in 2 features of 200, the planted anomalies were found by the isolation forest with an average AUC of 0.58; on the two features alone, 0.997.
4. Eight malignant tumours among 357 benign ones were ranked well (average AUC 0.95), but flagging 5% caught between 38% and 88% of them depending on the draw and the forest's seed.
5. Treat an anomaly score as a ranking under assumptions about features and neighbourhoods. Choose how many to flag from a budget or a labelled sample, and check what the top of the ranking actually contains.

## Exercises


1. Take the rows your detector flagged most recently and the next 50 below the cut. Look at both groups by hand, or have someone who knows the data do it. If the two groups look alike, the cut is arbitrary and the ranking is all there is.
2. If the flagged group in Exercise 1 is dominated by one kind of row, check whether many similar rows are sitting just as far from normal, and whether your neighbourhood size could see them.
3. Explain why the k-NN distance score can rank a tight group of anomalies below normal points, giving an AUC under 0.5, while the isolation forest ranks the same group perfectly.
4. A colleague sets `contamination=0.01` because "about 1% of transactions are fraud". Explain what the setting actually does and what you would need to know to choose the cut instead.

## Further reading


- Liu, Ting and Zhou, "Isolation forest", *ICDM* (2008): the original isolation forest and its path-length score.
- Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander, "LOF: identifying density-based local outliers", *SIGMOD* (2000): the local outlier factor and the idea of comparing a point's density with its neighbours'.
- Zimek, Schubert and Kriegel, "A survey on unsupervised outlier detection in high-dimensional numerical data", *Statistical Analysis and Data Mining* (2012): how irrelevant attributes hide anomalies and how different detectors suffer.
