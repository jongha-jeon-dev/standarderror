# Closing material for Chapter 10: Generalisation in a Network

# From Section 10.1 (episode 26): The Network That Memorises Noise Learns the Signal First

## In practice


- **Do not read training accuracy as evidence of learning.** A network of ordinary size can reach 100% on labels that mean nothing; only held-out performance separates learning from memorisation.
- **Do not argue from small capacity.** If your network can fit random labels, as networks of ordinary size often can, the evidence that it generalises has to come from data it did not see. To find out, shuffle the training labels and retrain with the same settings.
- **Monitor a validation set at every epoch and keep the best checkpoint.** With noisy labels, validation accuracy can peak early and then fall while training accuracy keeps rising.
- **Report the gap between the peak and the final validation score.** It measures what memorising the noisy rows cost, and what early stopping is buying.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One small network, one data set.** The ordering of signal before noise is a tendency of gradient descent on data with shared structure, not a theorem. It depends on the learning rate, optimiser and architecture, and can be weakened, for instance by very large learning rates or by training on each example many times in a row.
- **Uniform random labels.** Real label noise is rarely uniform. Annotators confuse a 4 with a 9 more often than with a 0, and structured noise of that kind can look like signal to the network and be fitted early.
- **No regularisation.** No weight decay, dropout or augmentation was used. Each would be expected to slow the memorisation of noise and move or flatten the peak; none was tried here. Zhang and colleagues found that such regularisers did not stop their networks fitting random labels.
- **Full-batch training.** One step per pass over the data keeps the comparison clean. With minibatches the same phases would be expected to appear, measured in epochs rather than steps and with more noise; that was not run here.

## Key points


1. A network that generalises can also fit noise. The same network reached 100% training accuracy on true labels in 41 steps (test 0.968) and on random labels in 73 (test 0.120).
2. Capacity therefore does not explain generalisation here. The labels do: on real data, what fits one example of a class fits the rest.
3. Given both signal and noise, the network fitted the signal first. With 20% of labels randomised, by step 20 it was right on 97.3% of unchanged rows and 2.4% of changed ones, and test accuracy peaked at 0.928.
4. Memorising the noise afterwards cost 0.091 of test accuracy with 20% noise and 0.291 with 50%, invisible in training accuracy and visible only on held-out data.

## Exercises


1. Take a classifier you trained, shuffle its training labels completely, and retrain with exactly the same settings. Record how long it takes to reach the training accuracy the real run reached. If it gets there, explain why the real run's test score is the only evidence that it did not memorise its data.
2. Flip 10% of the real labels at random, train once more, and plot validation accuracy against epoch. The gap between the peak and the end is what early stopping is buying you.
3. In the 20% run the network was right on fewer changed rows than chance would give. Explain why this suggests it was predicting their true class, and why the recorded curve cannot confirm it.
4. Explain why structured label noise, such as confusing 4 with 9, might be fitted early, and what that would mean for early stopping.

- Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht and Vinyals, "Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization", *ICLR* (2017): the random-label experiments on image networks and the case that capacity cannot explain why networks generalise.
- Arpit et al., "A closer look at memorization in deep networks", *ICML* (2017): the ordering of real patterns before noise in larger networks.

# From Section 10.2 (episode 27): Perfect Training Accuracy Was Not the End of Learning

## In practice


- **Treat 100% training accuracy as a statement about fit, not about learning.** It says the network fits the training set, not which function it fits; only held-out data say which.
- **Do not stop a run because the training set is fitted.** Base stopping on a validation curve, and when the task plausibly has a simple underlying rule, look at what happens well past the point of fit before deciding.
- **Track the parameter norm alongside validation accuracy.** Here the norm kept falling under weight decay after the training set was fitted, while the network moved from the table to the rule; a norm that is still changing is a hint that the run may not be finished.
- **Set early-stopping patience with this in mind.** A validation score flat at its floor for a long stretch is not proof that nothing more will happen; here it stayed there for over a thousand steps before rising.

## Limits of the evidence


- **A task with an exact rule.** Modular addition has a compact rule that fits every pair, including the unseen ones. Most real data have no such rule, only a noisy relationship, and Section 10.1's network, trained on real digits with noisy labels, got worse after fitting, not better. Grokking shows the sentence is false; it is not evidence that training past 100% is generally wise.
- **Strong weight decay, full batches.** A weight decay of 1 with AdamW is far stronger than usual, and full-batch training removes the noise of minibatches. Both make the slow drift easy to see. Power and colleagues report a longer delay with weaker decay; without any, here, the rule did not arrive at all.
- **Early stopping would have failed.** A validation set drawn from the unseen pairs would have shown accuracy at 0 and stayed there for over a thousand steps after the training set was fitted. Any reasonable patience setting would have stopped the run long before the rule arrived. The same tool that rescued Section 10.1's network discards this one.
- **One seed per setting.** Each row of the table is one run, and the timings would move with the seed. The pattern, fitting first and generalising much later under weight decay, is the one reported across many seeds in the literature.

## Key points


1. 100% training accuracy says the network fits the training set, not how. Here a memorised table and the rule both fitted it perfectly.
2. Learning continued after the training set was fitted. Training accuracy reached 100% at step 200 with test accuracy 0.000, and test accuracy passed 99% at step 4,900.
3. In these runs weight decay drove the switch, the parameter norm falling from 112 to 76 on the way. Without it, in the one control run, test accuracy was 0.1% after 10,000 steps.
4. More training data brought the rule sooner: step 1,500 with 60% of the pairs.
5. Neither "stop at 100%" nor "train past it" is a rule. What happens after the fit depends on whether there is a simpler solution to find and whether anything pushes the network towards it.

## Exercises


1. Take a small model you train to 100% training accuracy and keep training it for ten times as many steps, with weight decay, recording validation accuracy and the parameter norm every few hundred steps. If validation accuracy rises again after the fit, your early-stopping rule would have thrown that away; if it only falls, your data are more like Section 10.1's than this section's, and stopping early was right.
2. Explain why test accuracy of 0.000 at step 200 is evidence of a memorised table rather than of a partly learned rule.
3. Using the account of weight decay in this section, explain why removing weight decay should leave the network at the memorised table, and why a smaller training fraction should delay the rule.

## Further reading


- Power, Burda, Edwards, Babuschkin and Misra, "Grokking: generalization beyond overfitting on small algorithmic datasets" (2022): the original report of delayed generalisation on algorithmic tasks, and how the delay depends on training fraction and weight decay.
- Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith and Steinhardt, "Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability", *ICLR* (2023): what a transformer trained on modular addition learns internally, numbers on circles and addition of angles.
- Liu, Michaud and Tegmark, "Omnigrok: grokking beyond algorithmic data", *ICLR* (2023): the account of grokking in terms of weight norm, and its extension beyond algorithmic data.

# From Section 10.3 (episode 28): Augmentation Adds an Assumption, Not Data

## In practice


- **Do not count augmented copies as data.** They add an assumption, not observations. Judge an augmentation by a held-out comparison with and without it, not by the size of the enlarged training set.
- **Build a test set that varies the way you expect deployment to vary.** An augmentation's benefit may be invisible on an ordinary test set and large on a shifted one. Score with and without the augmentation on both.
- **Check that each transformation preserves the label.** Look at a sample of augmented images with their labels. Mirrors, half-turns, crops and time reversals can all change the class of some examples.
- **Watch the training budget.** With a fixed number of steps, augmented copies displace the original examples, and a true but unneeded invariance can cost accuracy on the ordinary test set.
- **Drop augmentations whose invariance the test data never exercise.** They are wasted at best.

## Limits of the evidence


- **A small network on centred images.** One hidden layer on raw pixels has no built-in tolerance for position, which is why a one-pixel move costs it so much. A convolutional network with *pooling*, which summarises each small neighbourhood of a feature map by its maximum or mean, builds part of the shift invariance into its architecture and needs the augmentation less. The point about what augmentation supplies is the same; the size of the effect is specific to this model.
- **Equal steps, not equal epochs.** Every network took exactly 300 gradient steps, so each image in the augmented and the larger real sets was seen fewer times. Giving every network the same number of epochs instead would have given the 150-digit network a fifth of the steps, a different comparison.
- **One kind of shift.** The moved test set uses exactly the transformation the shift augmentation uses. Test data that vary in a different way, by rotation of a few degrees or by thickness of stroke, would reward a different augmentation and not this one.
- **Small data.** With 150 training images every source of information shows. With tens of thousands, real data already contain much of the variation, and augmentations matter mostly for the invariances the real data never exhibit.

## Key points


1. Augmented copies are not new observations. On the ordinary test digits, four times more real data added 0.041; four shifted copies of each image changed accuracy by -0.027, and noisy copies by +0.001.
2. An augmentation asserts that the label survives a transformation. Shifted copies added 0.320 on test digits moved by a pixel, where four times more centred real digits added 0.016.
3. A false assertion costs accuracy. Mirrors and half-turns change the label of at least three digit classes, and training on them cost 0.092 and 0.123.
4. Choose augmentations by the variation the test data will have and the label will survive, not by how much they enlarge the training set.

## Exercises


1. Take an image or text model you trained with augmentation and build two extra test sets: one where each test example has been transformed by one of your augmentations, and one where it has not. Train once with the augmentation and once without, and score both models on both test sets. If the augmentation helps only on the transformed test set, state what invariance it is buying and whether your real test data need it.
2. Look at a sample of your augmented training images with their labels and count how many you would label differently yourself. Explain, using the orbit-averaging view, what those examples do to the model.
3. Explain why pixel noise preserved the label and still changed nothing, and describe a test set on which it would be expected to help.
