# Closing material for Chapter 13: Causes and Feedback

# From Section 13.1 (episode 48): What a Model Relies On Is Not What to Change

## In practice


- **Read importance and what-if analyses as statements about the model.** They say what the model uses to predict, not what would happen if a feature were changed.
- **Classify each important feature by timing.** For each, record whether it is measured before or after the outcome could have influenced it. Anything recorded after the outcome began, or produced by the process that records it, is a consequence, not a lever.
- **List the plausible common causes.** For any proposed lever, write down what might drive both it and the outcome. If those causes cannot be measured, adjustment is not available.
- **Build a separate model for the causal question.** Include the treatment and the measured confounders, exclude anything downstream, and state the assumed structure explicitly.
- **Prefer an experiment when one is possible.** Where a change has been made deliberately, in a trial or a policy change, compare the observed effect with the model's what-if before trusting either.

## Limits of the evidence


- **A system written down in advance.** The causal structure here is known because it was chosen. In practice it is a hypothesis, and the adjusted estimate is only as good as the assumption that $U$ is the only common cause.
- **The common cause was measurable.** $U$ was handed to the adjusted model. When it cannot be measured, adjustment is impossible, and the options are randomised experiments, instrumental variables or bounds.
- **Linear effects.** The treatment's effect is linear in the log-odds. Effects that differ between people, the subject of Section 13.2, need more than one number.
- **Four features.** Real models have hundreds, and the downstream ones are often hard to recognise: a variable recorded after the outcome began, or produced by the same process that records it.

## Key points


1. The feature the models relied on most, a consequence of the outcome, carried 0.45 of AUC importance and has no effect when changed.
2. A fellow traveller of the outcome lowered the predicted risk ($-0.029$) and has no effect; the real lever ranked no higher than it in importance, with a what-if under a third of its true effect.
3. A regression on the treatment and the common cause recovered $+0.089$ against a true $+0.085$; the treatment alone gave $+0.202$, and dropping the downstream feature alone gave $+0.144$.
4. Which features are in the model decides the apparent effect of a lever; the right set depends on the causal structure, which no inspection of the model can reveal.
5. Questions about what to change need the causal structure and a model built for that question, or an experiment.

## Exercises


1. List the features your model relies on most and, for each, write down whether it is recorded before or after the outcome could have influenced it, and what common causes it might share with the outcome.
2. For any feature someone proposes to change, find out whether the change has ever been made deliberately, in an experiment or a policy change, and compare what happened with what your model's what-if predicts.
3. Explain why including the test result in the model shrinks the treatment's apparent effect, while omitting both the test result and $U$ inflates it.
4. In the system above, which feature would you need to measure to estimate the habit's effect correctly, and what would you expect the estimate to be?

## Further reading


- Pearl, *Causality* (2009): causal graphs, back-door paths and the conditions under which an effect can be estimated from observational data.
- Hernán and Robins, *Causal Inference: What If* (2020): a practitioner-oriented treatment of confounding, adjustment and target trials.
- Zhao and Hastie, "Causal interpretations of black-box models", *Journal of Business & Economic Statistics* (2021): when a partial dependence plot can be given a causal reading.

# From Section 13.2 (episode 49): Target the Effect, Not the Risk

## In practice


- **Ask what the intervention changes, not who is at risk.** The quantity to rank by is the difference the intervention makes for each person.
- **Keep a randomised holdout.** Even a small group of eligible people who are not treated, chosen at random, is what makes the effect estimable and the targeting rule checkable.
- **Check whether risk tracks effect.** Split the scored population into fifths by risk and compare treated and untreated outcomes within each. If the effect is not largest in the top fifth, the risk score is choosing the wrong people.
- **Try simple effect models alongside flexible ones, especially when trials are small.** The noise of two fits adds up in their difference; here the logistic T-learner beat the boosted one at both trial sizes.
- **Report every targeting rule against random targeting of the same size**, in outcomes prevented per thousand, so the comparison that matters is always in view. This matters most when the budget is tight.

## Limits of the evidence


- **A simulated effect with a clear shape.** The offer's effect depends on risk in a way that makes risk targeting fail. Where the effect is largest for the highest-risk people, risk targeting is close to right, and a trial is the only way to know which case holds.
- **Known true probabilities.** Churn prevented was computed from the simulation's probabilities. In practice the value of a targeting rule has to be estimated from another trial, with its own noise.
- **Budgets measured on one trial size.** The budget sweep used the large trial and logistic models; with a smaller trial the effect ranking is noisier at every budget.
- **One-shot.** The offer is made once. Repeated offers, and customers who learn to expect them, are a different problem.

## Key points


1. Offering the deal to the highest predicted churners prevented 20.1 to 22.0 churns per 1,000 customers, about the same as random targeting (21.9).
2. The highest-risk fifth held more customers the offer could not help or would harm: 11% of them were made more likely to leave, against 6% overall.
3. Ranking by the predicted difference the offer makes, from models of a randomised trial's two arms, prevented up to 50.4, against a best possible 55.5; it needed the larger trial to get there.
4. Under a tight budget the risk ranking did worst: at 5% it prevented 0.9 per 1,000, against 5.5 at random.
5. Target an intervention by its estimated effect, which needs a randomised comparison, and measure the targeting rule against random targeting before trusting it.

## Exercises


1. For an intervention targeted by a risk score, find whether a randomised comparison exists, even a small holdout of people who qualified and were not treated.
2. Split the scored population into fifths by risk and, within each, compare outcomes between treated and untreated. If the treatment's effect is not largest in the top fifth, the risk score is choosing the wrong people, and a model of the difference is worth building.
3. Report every targeting rule as outcomes prevented per thousand people next to what random targeting of the same number would prevent.
4. Explain why the T-learner needs random assignment, and what its difference would estimate if the offer had been given to the customers a risk model ranked highest.

## Further reading


- Radcliffe and Surry, "Real-world uplift modelling with significance-based uplift trees" (2011): uplift modelling in marketing practice, and the four groups of customers.
- Künzel, Sekhon, Bickel and Yu, "Metalearners for estimating heterogeneous treatment effects using machine learning", *PNAS* (2019): the T-learner and related meta-learners, and when each works well.
- Athey and Imbens, "Recursive partitioning for heterogeneous causal effects", *PNAS* (2016): causal trees, which estimate treatment effects directly rather than as a difference of two fits.

# From Section 13.3 (episode 50): A Rule That Chooses Its Own Data Learns Its Own Choices

## In practice


- **Audit where the training data came from.** For a model that decides where to look, whom to review or what to show, count how much of its training data came from units it selected and how much from anywhere else.
- **Estimate rates per unit of effort.** Replace counts of findings with findings per inspection, per review or per impression, so that looking more does not by itself make a unit look worse.
- **Keep a share of decisions independent of the model.** A fixed fraction allocated at random or evenly keeps every unit measured; here 10% cut wrong lock-ins from 30% to 8%.
- **Check the units the model rarely selects.** Compare its estimates for them with an independent measurement such as an audit, a survey or a random sample.
- **Treat a stable record as evidence about the rule first.** A loop that has locked in shows up as units the model is confident about and nobody has looked at.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Two districts, fixed rates.** Real programmes have many units whose rates change, partly in response to the inspections. That adds a second loop: deterrence lowers the rate where inspectors go, and the record then says those places improved.
- **Violations found perfectly when present.** Inspections that miss some violations, or find more where inspectors expect them, bias even the per-visit estimate.
- **Simple rules.** A real system retrains a model on features, not a ratio on counts. The mechanism is the same whenever the model's training data come from where its own predictions sent people.
- **One year.** Lock-in under the greedy rules is decided early, but under the proportional rule with unequal rates the year-end share depends on the horizon, and the effect of exploration over much longer horizons was not measured.

## Key points


1. Sending inspectors where the most violations had been found put every visit in one district by year's end in every run, the district with the lower true rate in 46% of runs.
2. Splitting visits in proportion to violations found settled at random, as a Pólya urn does: within 0.1 of the truth in 21% of runs.
3. Dividing by visits made put the split within 0.1 of the truth in every run, in both worlds.
4. When the rule could starve a district of visits, keeping 10% of visits for exploration cut wrong lock-ins from 30% to 8%.
5. When a model's decisions decide its data, estimate rates per unit of effort, keep a share of decisions independent of the model, and treat a stable record as evidence about the rule before treating it as evidence about the world.

## Exercises


1. For a model that decides where to look, whom to review or what to show, find the training data that came from places, people or items it selected, and count how much came from anywhere else.
2. If almost none did, compare the model's estimates for the units it rarely selects with any independent measurement of them, such as an audit, a survey or a random sample.
3. Explain why the proportional rule converges to a random share in the identical world, while the per-visit rule converges to an even split.
4. Explain why dividing by effort is not enough for a rule that sends every visit to the unit with the highest estimated rate, and what exploration adds.

## Further reading


- Ensign, Friedler, Neville, Scheidegger and Venkatasubramanian, "Runaway feedback loops in predictive policing", FAT\* (2018): models predictive policing as a Pólya urn and shows how its allocations run away.
- Lum and Isaac, "To predict and serve?", *Significance* (2016): applies a predictive-policing algorithm to Oakland drug-arrest records and shows it returning to the same neighbourhoods.
- Sutton and Barto, *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction* (2018): the exploration-exploitation trade-off in learning from one's own actions.
