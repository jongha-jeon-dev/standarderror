# From Section 9.1 (episode 21): Initialisation Must Break Symmetry, at the Right Scale

## In practice


- **Never initialise a layer's weights to a constant.** The units stay identical through training, and the layer is one unit wide in effect.
- **Match the initialiser to the activation.** Use He initialisation (variance $2/\text{fan-in}$) for ReLU layers and Glorot initialisation for tanh and sigmoid layers. Check which initialiser your layers actually use rather than assuming.
- **Measure the activations before training.** Pass one batch through the untrained network, record the standard deviation of each layer's output and plot it against depth on a log scale. A line that rises or falls steadily means the initial scale is wrong.
- **Compute the per-layer factor.** It should be close to 1; for ReLU it should be close to $g/\sqrt{2}$, and if it is far from that, suspect that the activation or the gain is not what you think.
- **Check the gradients as well.** A level forward signal can hide exploding gradients. Record the gradient norm at the first and last layers after one backward pass.
- **Treat depth as the multiplier of any mis-scaling.** A choice that is harmless at three layers can stop a thirty-layer plain network from training.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Plain stacks of layers.** Residual connections, which add each block's input to its output, and normalisation layers, which rescale activations at every layer (Section 9.4), both make a network far less sensitive to its initial scale. Most modern deep networks have both, which is why this failure is rarer in practice than the arithmetic suggests.
- **One seed and four learning rates.** The verdicts are large enough to survive both limits: the four failing networks score at most 0.132 at any rate tried, against chance at 0.10. The differences among the networks that do train are not established here.
- **Thirty epochs.** A network whose gradients are of order 1e-5 might eventually begin to learn with a much larger learning rate or many more epochs. Neither was tried, and a much larger rate would risk overflow in the layers whose activations are not small.
- **The theory describes the start.** The $g/\sqrt{2}$ factor describes the network at initialisation. Training changes the weights, and the scales with them, which is why the ReLU network at $g = \sqrt{2}$ can train at all: the initialisation only has to give it a place to start.

## Key points


1. Symmetry must be broken. With every weight equal, the 128 units of a layer were still identical after training, and the network scored 0.197.
2. Breaking symmetry is not enough in a deep network. Each layer multiplies the signal's scale by a factor set by the initial scale, about $g/\sqrt{2}$ for ReLU, measured at 0.719 for $g = 1$ and 1.438 for $g = 2$.
3. Over thirty layers those factors decided whether training happened at all: ReLU scored 0.132 at $g = 1$, 0.919 at $g = \sqrt{2}$ and 0.099 at $g = 2$.
4. Tanh saturates, so its forward signal can stay level while its gradients explode; the scale must be right in both directions.
5. With three layers every random scale trained above 0.9. Depth turns a modest mis-scaling into a failure. Use the initialiser that matches the activation, and check the activations' scale at the last layer before training.

## Exercises


1. Take a deep network you train without normalisation or residual connections. Before the first update, record the standard deviation of each layer's output on one batch and plot it against depth on a log scale.
2. If the line in Exercise 1 is not roughly flat, compute the per-layer factor and compare it with what your initialiser's gain and your activation predict. Then multiply the initial weights by the factor's inverse and see whether training starts sooner.
3. Explain why a constant initialisation fails at three layers as well as thirty, while a mis-scaled random initialisation fails only at thirty.

# From Section 9.2 (episode 22): Learning Rate and Batch Size Are One Knob, Until the Rate Hits a Wall

## In practice


- **Treat the ratio of learning rate to batch size as the setting.** When you change the batch size, scale the learning rate by the same factor, and record the ratio alongside the rate.
- **Do not copy a tuned learning rate to a different batch size.** A rate tuned at one batch size is a ratio; moving to four times the batch at the same rate divides the effective setting by four.
- **Locate the wall before scaling up.** Run a short sweep at doubling rates and note where training fails. If the batch size you want needs a rate near or above it, the linear rule will not hold.
- **Beyond the wall, pay with epochs.** A larger batch at a capped rate takes fewer, shorter-travelling steps; budget more epochs or accept a higher loss.
- **Consider warm-up for large rates.** Goyal and colleagues raised the rate gradually over the first epochs to keep large-batch runs from diverging at the start; warm-up was not tested in this section.
- **Remember the rule was measured with plain SGD.** With momentum the ratio applies to the effective rate; with Adam the relation changes, as the next section explains.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Plain SGD only.** With momentum, the effective step is the learning rate divided by one minus the momentum, and the same ratio argument applies to that effective rate. With Adam, whose step size does not scale with the gradient's magnitude, the relation is closer to a square root and depends on the noise. Section 9.3 uses Adam.
- **Training loss, not test accuracy.** The question was optimisation. Test accuracy followed the training loss closely here; on larger problems it can prefer the noisier, smaller-batch runs at equal training loss, a separate question with a contested answer.
- **One network and one budget.** The position of the wall depends on the network's curvature, and the critical batch size depends on the task's gradient noise, so neither number transfers to other problems. What transfers is the shape: a ratio that sets the run, and a wall that does not move with the batch.
- **A coarse grid.** Rates double, so "best" and "fails" are each known only to within a factor of two.

## Key points


1. With plain SGD and a fixed number of examples, learning rate and batch size act through their ratio. Away from divergence, runs with the same ratio reached the same training loss to within 2.3%, from batches of 8 to 512.
2. The best rate grew fourfold from a batch of 8 to a batch of 32 (0.192 to 0.768), with the same loss and test accuracy.
3. For large batches, training failed (mean training loss above 1) at a learning rate that did not move with the batch: 3.072 for both 128 and 512, diverging at 128 and degraded to 1.27 at 512.
4. Batches that needed a rate beyond that wall reached a training loss 3 and 55 times higher than the batch of 32's.
5. Change the batch size and the learning rate together; beyond the wall, a larger batch means less training, not faster training.

## Exercises


1. Take a training script and find its best learning rate at your usual batch size. Halve the batch size and run it at the old rate and at half the old rate, for the same number of epochs. If the halved rate matches the original run and the old rate does not, your setting was the ratio.
2. Then double the batch size instead, at twice the rate, and see whether it trains or diverges. If it diverges, you have located your wall.
3. Using the arithmetic argument in this section, explain why runs with the same ratio part company as the learning rate approaches the point of failure.
4. Explain why a limit set by curvature would be the same learning rate for every batch size, while a limit set by gradient noise would scale with the batch.

## Further reading


- Alpaydin and Kaynak, UCI Machine Learning Repository: the Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits data used in this section, as bundled with scikit-learn.
- Goyal et al., "Accurate, large minibatch SGD: training ImageNet in 1 hour" (2017): the linear scaling rule and learning-rate warm-up applied at very large batch sizes.
- Smith and Le, "A Bayesian perspective on generalization and stochastic gradient descent", *ICLR* (2018): the noise scale of SGD and its dependence on the ratio of learning rate to batch size.
- McCandlish, Kaplan, Amodei et al., "An empirical model of large-batch training" (2018): the critical batch size and how it depends on the gradient noise of a task.

# From Section 9.3 (episode 23): Adam Is a Little Faster and No Better

## In practice


- **Tune every optimiser you compare by the same procedure.** Same grid width, same validation data, same budget. A comparison that tunes one and uses defaults for the other measures the tuning.
- **Say what the rate was tuned for.** A rate chosen for validation accuracy and a rate chosen for speed can differ, and the speed comparison reverses between them.
- **Compare paired runs over several splits.** Start both optimisers from the same weights on each split, report the mean difference with its standard error, and treat a difference within two standard errors of zero as not distinguishable from no difference.
- **Check the validation set's resolution.** If one validation example is worth more accuracy than the differences you are ranking, expect ties, and decide deliberately how ties are broken.
- **Value Adam for robustness, not for a better model.** Its smaller sensitivity to the learning rate is useful when tuning is expensive; it is not evidence that it finds better solutions.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Small networks, small data.** Two hidden layers on 900 digits or 2,000 simulated rows. On large language models Adam (or AdamW) is the near-universal choice, and it is common in vision, partly because SGD is hard to tune at that scale and partly because adaptive scaling helps with very different gradient scales across layers, such as embeddings.
- **A coarse grid.** Five rates per optimiser, spaced by about a factor of three. Both "fastest" and "tuned" are known only to that resolution, and validation accuracy on a fifth of one split (180 digits) has ties: Adam's 3e-4 and 1e-3 scored the same, and the smaller was taken.
- **One schedule.** Constant learning rates, no warm-up, no decay. Schedules change both speed and final accuracy, for both optimisers.
- **No weight decay.** Adam's interaction with weight decay is its own subject (AdamW exists because of it), and was left out by giving neither optimiser any.

## Key points


1. Tuned on validation data and run over twenty splits, Adam and SGD with momentum reached the same test accuracy: differences of +0.0009 ± 0.0007 on the digits and +0.0005 ± 0.0007 on a hard simulated task.
2. Speed belongs to the learning rate. At the rates validation chose, SGD's loss fell faster; at each optimiser's fastest rate, Adam reached a loss of 0.01 in 6 epochs and SGD in 8.
3. The fastest rates scored as well as any on test digits. Validation on 180 digits could not tell them apart from slower ones, and its tie-break chose the slower.
4. Adam's accuracy varied less across its grid (0.961 to 0.977) than SGD's (0.928 to 0.975), a practical virtue distinct from speed.
5. Compare optimisers only with each tuned by the same procedure, and report what the rates were tuned for.

## Exercises


1. Take a model you train with Adam at its default learning rate. Train it with SGD with momentum 0.9 at five rates spaced by a factor of three, and with Adam at five rates around the default, all for the same number of epochs from the same initial weights.
2. Plot validation accuracy against rate for both optimisers from Exercise 1. If the best SGD point is within noise of the best Adam point, your choice of optimiser is a choice about tuning effort, not about the model you end up with.
3. Work out how much accuracy one example is worth on your own validation set, and compare it with the differences between your candidate learning rates. Explain how ties among those rates would be broken by your tuning code.
4. Explain why dividing each step by a running estimate of the gradient's scale might make an optimiser less sensitive to its learning rate, and why that does not by itself imply faster convergence.

## Further reading


- Alpaydin and Kaynak, UCI Machine Learning Repository: the Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits data used in this section, as bundled with scikit-learn.
- Kingma and Ba, "Adam: a method for stochastic optimization", *ICLR* (2015): the original description of Adam and its bias-corrected running averages.
- Wilson, Roelofs, Stern, Srebro and Recht, "The marginal value of adaptive gradient methods in machine learning", *NeurIPS* (2017): the report that adaptive methods can generalise worse than SGD.
- Choi et al., "On empirical comparisons of optimizers for deep learning" (2019): how rankings of optimisers depend on how each is tuned.

# From Section 9.4 (episode 24): Batch Normalisation Normalises the Batch, Not the Example

## In practice


- **Check the mode at serving time.** Confirm that every normalisation layer is in inference mode when the model serves predictions; a training-mode model's answers depend on the rest of the batch.
- **Shuffle before batching.** Make sure batches at training time are random samples. Data sorted by label, date or source give batch statistics that describe the sort, not the population.
- **Avoid batch normalisation with very small batches.** Below a batch size at which training-mode and inference-mode accuracy agree, consider layer or group normalisation, or batch renormalisation (none was tested here).
- **Compare the two modes on held-out data.** Evaluate a trained network in both modes, inside random batches of the training size. Close agreement is evidence that the running averages are trustworthy.
- **Do not rely on gradient accumulation to enlarge the normalisation batch.** It enlarges the gradient's batch, not the statistics'.
- **Watch the running statistics when fine-tuning.** Freeze them, or check them against the original data, when fine-tuning in small or unrepresentative batches.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Fully connected layers on small images.** In convolutional networks the statistics are pooled over all spatial positions as well as over the batch, so a batch of 2 images contributes many values per channel, and the dependence is likely weaker than here; that was not measured.
- **Frozen running statistics in the first study.** Their momentum was set to zero so that the evaluation did not change the network. In a real training-mode deployment the running averages would also drift with every batch.
- **One architecture, one data set.** The batch size at which normalisation turns from hurting to helping, between 4 and 16 here, depends on the network and on how much the batch statistics vary, and is not a general constant.
- **Adam at one learning rate.** The batch-size study holds the learning rate at 1e-3 for every batch size. Section 9.2 showed, for plain SGD, that the best rate moves with the batch, and Adam's rate is unlikely to be immune, so the plain network's flat line across batch sizes is partly a matter of where 1e-3 happens to sit.

## Key points


1. Batch normalisation normalises with the batch's statistics. In training mode one digit's label changed with its random companions in 45% of batches of 2 and 0.6% of batches of 64.
2. The companions must be a random sample. With 15 companions from one class, the label changed in 23% of batches of 16, against 3.2% with random ones.
3. In a batch of 2 every normalised unit is ±1 before the learned scale and shift: the layer keeps only which example was larger. Trained that way, the network scored 0.863 in inference mode, against 0.972 without normalisation.
4. From batches of 16 up, normalisation scored 0.5 and 0.8 points higher over three seeds, and at 64 the two modes agreed to within a point.
5. Before trusting batch normalisation, check that batches are large enough and mixed enough that their statistics are the population's.

## Exercises


1. Take a trained network with batch normalisation and pick 50 test examples. Predict each one three ways: in evaluation mode alone; in training mode inside random batches of your usual size; and in training mode inside batches whose other members all share one label or one source.
2. Count how often the label changes between the first prediction in Exercise 1 and each of the other two. If the third count is much larger than the second, explain what any process that feeds your model sorted batches, at training or at serving, is doing to its answers.
3. Show that in a batch of 2 the normalised values are $+1$ and $-1$ whatever the two inputs. What happens to the result if the two inputs are equal, and what role does $\epsilon$ play?
4. Explain why layer normalisation computes the same function in training and inference, and why that removes the dependence on batch companions.

## Further reading


- Alpaydin and Kaynak, UCI Machine Learning Repository: the Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits data used in this section, as bundled with scikit-learn.
- Ioffe and Szegedy, "Batch normalization", *ICML* (2015): the original description of the layer, its batch statistics and its running averages.
- Ioffe, "Batch renormalization: towards reducing minibatch dependence in batch-normalized models", *NeurIPS* (2017): a correction for small and non-independent batches.
- Wu and He, "Group normalization", *ECCV* (2018): normalisation over groups of units within one example, independent of the batch.

# From Section 9.5 (episode 25): Weight Scaling Keeps Dropout's Answers, Not Its Doubts

## In practice


- **Use weight scaling for labels.** If only the predicted class matters, evaluation mode with weight scaling gives almost exactly the labels of averaging many masks, at the cost of one forward pass.
- **Do not trust weight-scaled probabilities without checking.** Measure log loss and mean top-class probability against a Monte Carlo average on held-out data; on the digits, five masks already improved log loss.
- **Average masks when probabilities drive decisions.** If thresholds, rankings or expected costs use the probabilities, consider averaging a modest number of masks, or recalibrating on held-out data (Section 11.3 measures recalibration).
- **Never serve a dropout model in training mode.** A single mask is worse than both rules and gives a different answer for the same input on each call. Test that evaluation mode is set.
- **Look at where the masks disagree.** Here the rows on which the two rules disputed the label were rows where the averaged probability was close to a coin flip; that drop in averaged probability is information weight scaling discards. Whether the spread of the masks is a calibrated uncertainty was not tested.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One dropout rate.** At $p = 0.5$ each mask removes half the units, the largest perturbation in common use. At $p = 0.1$ the thinned networks are much closer to each other and to the scaled one, and both gaps should shrink; that was not measured here.
- **Small fully connected networks.** Two hidden layers; the gap compounds with depth, so deeper networks could differ more. Convolutional networks usually apply dropout only near the output, which limits how much nonlinearity sits between the masks and the prediction.
- **300 masks is an estimate.** The average of 300 masks is not the exact average over all of them. The curves are flat by 100 masks, which suggests the remaining error is small.
- **Calibration was not fixed.** A temperature fitted on held-out data could bring the weight-scaled network's log loss down without any sampling. The comparison here is between the two rules as they come, which is how they are used.

## Key points


1. Weight scaling reproduces the labels of mask averaging closely: 99.7% agreement on the digits, 94.3% on a hard task, with accuracy within half a point.
2. It does not reproduce the probabilities. On the digits it was sharper (mean top probability 0.985 against 0.954) and its log loss 28% higher; 5 masks were enough to beat it.
3. The two rules are identical for a linear unit; they differ because nonlinearities do not commute with averaging.
4. The labels the two rules dispute are the ones the masks dispute. The averaged top probability on them was 0.42 on the digits and 0.53 on the hard task.
5. One sampled mask, the prediction of a model left in training mode, was worse than both rules on accuracy and log loss.

## Exercises


1. Take a network you trained with dropout and evaluate it on a held-out set three ways: in evaluation mode, with dropout on and one pass, and with dropout on averaged over 30 passes. Compare accuracy, log loss and the mean top-class probability.
2. Sort the held-out rows from Exercise 1 by how much the 30 passes disagree with each other, and compare the error rate in the top tenth with that in the bottom tenth. If the disagreement tracks the errors, you have a free warning signal that evaluation mode was discarding.
3. Show that for a single linear unit, weight scaling by $1 - p$ gives exactly the expected output over masks. Then give an example with one ReLU unit where the two differ.
4. Explain how two rules can agree on 94.3% of labels and differ in log loss by under 1%, while on another task they agree on 99.7% of labels and differ in log loss by 28%.
