# Closing material for Chapter 5: How Far More Data Goes

# From Section 5.1 (episode 4): A Learning Curve Fitted Early Promises More Than More Data Delivers

## In practice


- **Do not trust a two-parameter power-law forecast from a pilot study.** In every fit here, simulated and real, it promised a lower error than the model reached. Expect it to be optimistic, and do not report it as an estimate.
- **Plot the local log-log slope.** Compute the slope between neighbouring training sizes. If it is shrinking, there is a floor ahead and a straight-line fit will overshoot it.
- **Refit with the largest pilot size left out.** If the forecast at your target size moves by more than the improvement you are hoping for, the curve cannot tell you what the extra data will buy.
- **Distrust a fitted floor on a curve that is still falling.** It can land above what the model will reach or below the Bayes error, and it moves with the fit range. It is reliable mainly where the curve has already bent, and there you hardly need it.
- **Choose the model at the size you will train on.** The best model at pilot size need not be the best at full size. Check the ranking at the largest size you can afford to test.

## Limits of the evidence


- **These are small models on small data.** The scaling laws used to plan large neural-network runs are fitted over many orders of magnitude, far into the straight part of the curve, and have predicted well there. The failure here is specific to fitting early, which is exactly where a pilot study fits.
- **Least squares on the error scale weights the early points.** The large errors at small $n$ dominate an unweighted fit. Fitting on log error, or weighting by each point's variance, changes the numbers; it does not remove the curvature, so it does not change the direction of the bias.
- **The task was chosen to have a known floor.** Real problems have one too, but nobody computes it, which is the point: the only check on a fitted floor is the Bayes error, and in practice it is unavailable.
- **Few training sets at the largest sizes.** The 25,600-row errors average three training sets, and the digits' 1,200-image errors come from one. Their noise is small next to the gaps quoted, but not zero.

## Key points


1. A power law $a\,n^{-b}$ fitted to a pilot study predicted too low an error at the target size in all 18 fits here, simulated and real.
2. On a task with a known Bayes error of 0.268, 4 of 9 such predictions were below it: errors no classifier can reach.
3. Learning curves flatten towards a floor; the local log-log slope shrinks overall, if not at every step, so a straight line fitted early overshoots.
4. The floored form $a\,n^{-b} + c$ worked for logistic regression and, from 1,600 rows, for 15-NN, and failed for the boosted trees, still falling fastest: their fitted floor went from 0.330 (above what they reach) to 0.219 (below Bayes) with the fit range.
5. The best model at pilot size need not be the best at full size. On the digits, logistic regression went from best at 50 images to worst at 1,200.

## Exercises


1. If you have a learning curve from a pilot study, refit it twice: once on all the sizes you have, once leaving out the largest. If the prediction at your target size moves by more than the improvement you are hoping for, the curve is not telling you what the extra data will buy.
2. Plot the local slope between neighbouring sizes. If it is still steepening or constant, you are in the part of the curve a power law describes; if it is shrinking, there is a floor ahead, and a two-parameter fit will overshoot it.
3. Before committing to a model, check the ranking at the largest size you can afford to test, not at the pilot size. Which of your candidate models is the most constrained, and would you expect it to lead or trail as the data grow?
4. Explain why, on a stretch of learning curve with no visible bend, the floor $c$ in $a\,n^{-b} + c$ is poorly determined by the data.

## Further reading


- Viering and Loog, "The shape of learning curves: a review", *IEEE TPAMI* (2023): a review of the shapes learning curves take and of how they are modelled.
- Hestness et al., "Deep learning scaling is predictable, empirically" (2017): power-law scaling of error with data, fitted over many orders of magnitude in deep learning.
- Cortes et al., "Learning curves: asymptotic values and rate of convergence", *NeurIPS* (1993): the source of the $a\,n^{-b} + c$ form with an asymptotic floor.

# From Section 5.2 (episode 30): A Power Law Fitted on Small Models Cannot See the Floor

## In practice


- **Hold back more than one size when validating a scaling law.** A single correct prediction can come from cancelling errors. Fit on some sizes, check on several larger ones, and plot the residuals across the whole ladder.
- **Read the residuals, not the headline.** If they curve rather than scatter about zero, the functional form is wrong, and extrapolation will drift further the further it reaches.
- **Compare misses with the fitted models' scatter.** A miss no bigger than the scatter about the fitted points is not yet evidence of a bend; one well beyond it is.
- **Fit the law to training loss as well.** If training loss misses in the same direction, the cause is not mainly overfitting but something that limits learning, plausibly data or training steps.
- **Track data seen per parameter.** When it falls far below the level at which the small models of the ladder were trained, a law in parameter count alone may be misspecified; scale data and steps with the model, or expect a floor the small models cannot reveal.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One budget for every model.** The laws used to plan large models are fitted to models each trained with enough data and compute for their size, or along a compute-optimal frontier. This ladder holds the budget fixed on purpose, which is the setting in which a law in $N$ alone is misspecified. A ladder with data growing alongside the models would be expected to bend later, or not at all in this range; that was not run.
- **Small models, one seed each.** The models run from four thousand to two million non-embedding parameters, each trained once, and the run-to-run spread was not measured. The sixth model's agreement with the line, to a fraction of a percent, may be partly luck, and so may the seventh's miss, which is about the size of the scatter of the fitted models. The eighth's miss is more than twice that scatter, which seed noise of that size would be unlikely to produce.
- **One learning rate.** Every model used 3e-3. Larger models often want smaller rates, and part of the shortfall at the top of the ladder may be a rate that suits the small models better.
- **Character-level Shakespeare.** A million characters of one author is a narrow distribution with a low ceiling on what any model can learn from it. Kaplan's laws were measured on billions of tokens of web text, where the floor is far away.

## Key points


1. A power law fitted to five small models predicted the next size to +0.04%. Scaling laws do predict, over a short reach.
2. Over a longer reach they were optimistic: +3.4% for a model 5.2 times the largest fitted, +8.2% for one 12 times. A law fitted to three models hit the largest by cancelling two errors, and one fitted to seven missed it by +6.2%. Judge a law by its residuals, not by one hit.
3. Training loss missed the same way, so the cause was not mainly overfitting. With a fixed budget, characters per parameter fell from 955 to 2.3, consistent with the largest models being limited by data and steps rather than size; the budget itself was not varied.
4. A floor fitted to the small models was 0.000; fitted to all eight, 0.86. Extrapolate a fitted law only as far as the conditions that produced it still hold.

## Exercises


1. Take three or four sizes of a model you can train cheaply, each with the same data and the same number of steps, and fit $L = aN^{-b}$ to their validation loss. Before training the next size up, write down the prediction. Then train it, and a size beyond.
2. If the second prediction in Exercise 1 misses by more than the first, compute the training tokens per parameter for each model, and identify where the curve leaves the line. The point where it does is usually where that number stops being large.
3. Explain why fitting $aN^{-b} + c$ to models that all lie on a straight line on log-log axes cannot recover the floor $c$, and what that implies for planning a model larger than any in the pilot ladder.

## Further reading


- Karpathy's char-rnn collection, tinyshakespeare: the million characters of Shakespeare used to train the ladder in this section.
- Kaplan et al., "Scaling laws for neural language models" (2020): the power laws in model size, data and compute for language models trained on web text, measured where neither data nor training limits the model.
- Hoffmann et al., "Training compute-optimal large language models", *NeurIPS* (2022): how to divide a fixed compute budget between model size and training tokens, and the finding that compute-optimal models see on the order of twenty tokens per parameter.
- Section 5.1 of this book: learning curves fitted early, and the same failure of a floor that only the later points can reveal.

# From Section 5.3 (episode 68): More Data Cures the Noise, Not the Design

## In practice


- **Classify each problem as noise or design** before deciding whether a large data set has dealt with it, using the whole-population test above.
- **Retire noise problems only relative to complexity.** A selection leak, an encoding leak or tuning optimism shrinks with rows only if the number of features, categories and settings does not grow with them.
- **Do not let a large sample excuse a biased one.** For selective labels, ask who never enters the data and how their outcomes could be measured, for example by approving a random sample.
- **Count independent units, not rows.** Where rows come from people, sessions or sites, validate with folds that keep each unit together, and grow the number of units.
- **Expect criteria such as the BIC to grow more confident, not more correct,** when the model family is wrong.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Compact versions.** Each problem was rebuilt in a small simulation. The rates at which the real versions shrink depend on the number of features, categories and settings, which grow with data in real projects.
- **Fixed complexity.** With 1,000 noise features held fixed, the selection leak shrank. If the number of features grows with the data, as it often does, the leak need not shrink at all.
- **Ten data sets per size.** Enough to separate shrinking from staying; not enough to pin the third digit of any value.
- **Seven problems of many.** Problems such as drift (Section 20.1) and feedback loops (Section 13.3) depend on time, not sample size, and were not on the list.

## Key points


1. Four problems shrank with sixteen times the data: the standard error (2.8 to 0.7 points), tuning optimism (3.7 to 0.1), the selection leak (24.7 to 7.1) and the encoding leak (43 to 14).
2. Two did not: selective labels stayed 45 points wrong and row-level cross-validation with 20 people stayed 16 points optimistic.
3. The BIC's count of Gaussians for one uniform square grew from 4 to 8.
4. Noise averages away; the design of the data and the assumptions of the model do not.
5. With a large data set, spend your attention on who is in the data, how rows depend on each other and what the model assumes, not on error bars.

## Exercises


1. For a model trained on a large data set, list the problems from this book you would have worried about with a small one. Mark each as noise (an estimate fitted to the sample's accidents) or design (who is in the sample, how rows relate, what the model assumes).
2. Retire the noise problems if your sample is large relative to your features and settings. For each design problem, name the data you would need to measure it: more people rather than more rows, outcomes for the refused, a period after the drift.
3. Apply the whole-population test to a target encoding of a category whose number of levels grows with the data. Explain whether its leak behaves like a noise problem.
