# Closing material for Chapter 20: Monitoring and Retraining

# From Section 20.1 (episode 61): An Alarm on the Inputs Cannot See the Outcome

## In practice


- **Treat an input alarm as a reason to look, not as evidence of harm.** Before escalating, check labelled performance; a moved input can leave accuracy unchanged or improve it.
- **Do not read silence as health.** Input alarms, score alarms and importance-weighted accuracy estimates cannot detect a change in $p(y \mid x)$.
- **Budget for labelled outcomes**, however few and late: a regular labelled sample, or delayed outcomes joined back to predictions. A change in the relation shows up nowhere else.
- **Correct for the number of features watched** (test each at $\alpha / m$, accepting that small real shifts will be missed), and **fix the batch size or alarm on effect size**, since on large batches a p-value alarm fires on shifts too small to matter.
- **Compare mean predicted probability with the observed rate** whenever outcomes arrive; under label shift, accuracy can hold while the probabilities are wrong.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Five inputs, one model.** Real monitors watch hundreds of features and use other tests, such as population stability indices and embedding distances. Their behaviour with sample size and with many features is the same in kind as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test's.
- **Shifts chosen to separate the cases.** Real drift usually mixes a moving input distribution with a changing relation, and an input alarm will then often fire for the wrong reason at the right time. That coincidence is not a mechanism to rely on.
- **A correctly specified model.** Section 15.3 showed that a misspecified model does lose accuracy under covariate shift, so an input alarm is more informative for such a model. It still cannot see a change in the relation.
- **Labels assumed absent.** Where labels arrive quickly, monitoring the outcome directly makes most of this unnecessary.

## Key points


1. An input alarm fired on every batch when an input moved a standard deviation, and accuracy rose from 0.771 to 0.801.
2. When the relation between inputs and outcome reversed and the inputs stayed put, input and score alarms fired no more than with no drift, a label-free accuracy estimate said 0.77, and accuracy was 0.47.
3. With 20 inputs tested separately, 63% of batches raised an alarm with nothing wrong; with large batches, a shift too small to matter raised it 74% of the time.
4. Treat input drift as a reason to look, not as evidence of harm. Budget for labelled outcomes, however few and late, because a change in the relation shows up nowhere else.

## Exercises


1. List the alarms your monitoring raised in the last quarter and, for each, whether the model's measured performance on labelled outcomes changed afterwards. Then list the last two occasions the model's performance did fall and whether any input alarm preceded them.
2. Treat the two lists from Exercise 1 as measuring your monitor's precision and recall for the thing you care about. If either is low, explain why the missing ingredient is outcome data rather than a better test on the inputs.
3. Explain why an alarm on a model's predicted scores cannot detect a change in $p(y \mid x)$ when the inputs' distribution is unchanged, and why an importance-weighted accuracy estimate fails in the same case.

# From Section 20.2 (episode 64): How Much History to Retrain On Depends on How Fast the World Moves

## In practice


- **Do not assume shorter is safer.** In a stable world a short window only adds noise; with 60 rows a period the last period alone cost 0.049 of accuracy against all history.
- **Measure how fast your relation changes** from performance on labelled outcomes over time, and choose the window from that, not from a habit or a schedule.
- **Backtest the window choice on your own history**, retraining at each past date only on data whose labels would have been available then. Re-run the backtest periodically, since it reflects the past pace of change, not the future one.
- **Know your label delay and plan around it.** Under change it eats much of what retraining buys and lengthens every recovery. Shorten it where you can, for instance with faster investigation of outcomes.
- **Keep a never-retrained baseline** in your evaluation. If it holds up, retraining is buying little; if it collapses, the world is moving.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Logistic model, logistic world.** The model was correctly specified in every period, so its only problems were noise and staleness. A misspecified model adds a third: more data of the wrong kind cannot fix it.
- **Fixed windows.** Weighting rows by age, or retraining only when monitored performance drops, sit between the policies measured here; their best settings depend on the drift rate in the same way.
- **Constant volume.** Sixty rows a period made short windows noisy. With far more rows per period, short windows lose less in a stable world.
- **Six streams per cell.** Differences of under a point between neighbouring windows are within the noise; the ordering of the best policies across worlds was not.

## Key points


1. In a stable world, retraining on the last period alone cost 0.049 of accuracy against all history, and never retraining cost nothing.
2. The best window was all history, the last 16 periods, the last 4 and the last 4 across four worlds; no fixed choice was best everywhere. Window length is a bias-variance knob.
3. A backtest chose the right window when the pace of change was steady and the wrong one when it was not.
4. Labels eight periods late took 43% of retraining's gain under fast drift and added 16 periods to the recovery after a jump.
5. Choose a retraining window by measuring how fast your relation changes, from performance on labelled outcomes over time. Plan around your label delay; shorten it where you can, because it adds to every period a changed model spends wrong.

## Exercises


1. Take a model you retrain and backtest three windows on your own history: all of it, the last year, the last quarter. For each, retrain at every past retraining date using only data whose labels would have been available then, and score the next period.
2. Interpret the result of Exercise 1. If all of history wins, explain why frequent retraining buys little; if the shortest window wins, explain why the label delay becomes the number to reduce.
3. Explain, in terms of bias and variance, why the best window is longer under slow drift than under fast drift, and how the number of rows per period would shift it.
