# Closing material for Chapter 2: What Cross-Validation Estimates

# From Section 2.1 (episode 2): Cross-Validation Estimates the Error of a Model You Did Not Train

## In practice


- **Name the target.** When you report a CV score, describe it as the average performance of the training procedure on samples of this size, not as the performance of the deployed model.
- **Use CV to choose procedures.** Comparing algorithms, feature sets or hyperparameter settings is a question about $\mathrm{Err}$, and CV is the right tool; the pairing from Section 1.1 applies, since both procedures are scored on the same rows.
- **Hold data out to judge a model you will ship.** If the deployment decision depends on the specific fitted model being good, score it once on data it never saw, and accept that the estimate is noisy and the model slightly weaker.
- **Distrust the naive CV interval.** Its per-row standard error ignores the correlation between folds, and its nominal 90% covered neither target at 90% here. If the width matters, look at nested CV.
- **Do not read meaning into a good CV score for one particular sample.** Here, a better-than-usual CV score said nothing about the fitted model in simulation, and on the digits it went with slightly worse models.

## Limits of the evidence


- **The zero correlation is for this model and this size.** Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani show that it holds for linear models broadly. With very many rows per feature, the fitted model's error varies little from one training set to the next, so $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ and $\mathrm{Err}$ move closer together and the distinction matters less. Other model classes were not measured here. At 100 rows and 20 features it matters a lot.
- **The digits test set is a proxy for the truth.** It is a sample of 797 images, so each model's score on it is its true accuracy plus an error of order 0.008, shared in part between models because they face the same images, but not identical. The sign of the correlation survived the switch to a fixed test set; its size should be read loosely.
- **Squared error is skewed.** Part of the interval undercoverage, for every estimator in the table, comes from using a normal interval for a mean of skewed losses. The comparison between estimators is fair; the absolute coverages are a little worse than a better interval would give.

## Key points


1. "The error of your model" can mean the error of the model you fitted ($\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$) or the average error of models fitted to data like yours ($\mathrm{Err}$). They are different numbers.
2. On simulated least squares, the 10-fold CV estimate's correlation with $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ is -0.01. CV estimates $\mathrm{Err}$.
3. CV's naive 90% interval covers $\mathrm{Err}_{XY}$ 80% of the time and $\mathrm{Err}$ 83%: partly because the per-row losses are not independent, partly because they are skewed and CV's centre sits above $\mathrm{Err}$.
4. On real digits the correlation is -0.21: training samples that cross-validated well generalised slightly worse.
5. To evaluate a model you will ship, hold data out. To compare procedures, cross-validate. Say which one you did.

## Exercises


1. Find a report, ideally your own, that quotes a cross-validated score as the expected performance of a deployed model. Rewrite the sentence so it says what was measured: the average performance of the training procedure on samples of that size.
2. For the same report, if the deployment decision depended on the specific fitted model being good, check whether a held-out score exists, and if not, what it would cost in rows and in model quality to get one.
3. If you have two cross-validated scores that differ by less than their fold-to-fold spread, decide which question you were trying to answer. For choosing a procedure, the CV comparison is the right one, and Section 1.1's pairing applies to it. Explain why, for choosing a fitted model, it is not the right comparison at all.
4. Explain why scoring each training draw on its own complement, rather than on a fixed disjoint test set, would confound the CV score with the difficulty of the test set.

## Further reading


- Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani, "Cross-validation: what does it estimate and how well does it do it?", *JASA* (2023): the proof of the linear-model result and nested CV for intervals of honest width.
- Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, *The Elements of Statistical Learning* (2009), section 7.12: an illustration of the weak correlation between CV and the conditional error.
- Alpaydin and Kaynak, UCI Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits, as bundled with scikit-learn: the real dataset used here.

# From Section 2.2 (episode 44): Cross-Validation Folds Are Not Independent Samples, and the t-Test Assumes They Are

## In practice


- **Stop running a plain paired t-test on cross-validation fold scores,** and never on repeated folds with $J - 1$ degrees of freedom. Its p-values are too small.
- **Use the corrected resampled t-test on repeated cross-validation** when data are scarce: multiply the variance of the differences by $1/J + n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$. Of the honest tests here it was the most powerful.
- **Use McNemar's test on a large held-out set** when there are enough rows to set one aside.
- **Know which question you are answering.** A test on one data set says, at best, which model is better on that data set. Claims about algorithms in general need variation between data sets.
- **Report the size of the difference with its p-value.** A significant difference can be too small to matter; Section 1.1's error bars show directly how large a gap the data can resolve.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One task, small data.** Three hundred rows and shallow trees, where variation from one data set to another is large compared with the differences between algorithms. With many more rows the variation between data sets shrinks; whether the naive tests' excess shrinks with it was not measured here.
- **Unstable learners.** Decision trees change a lot when the data change, which makes the dependence between folds matter. Stable learners such as regularised linear models change less from fold to fold, and the naive tests' excess may be smaller for them; that was not measured here.
- **One comparison.** Comparing many models at once, or one model across many data sets, raises further problems: multiple testing, and, for many data sets, the rank-based tests Demšar recommended in 2006.
- **Significance is not size.** None of these tests says whether a difference is large enough to matter, which Section 1.1's error bars address more directly.

## Key points


1. With two algorithms of exactly equal expected accuracy, the paired t-test on ten folds declared a difference in 14.2% of data sets, and on ten repetitions of ten folds in 58.2%.
2. Fold scores share training rows, and repeated folds share the data set; neither carries the variation between data sets that the comparison is about.
3. The corrected resampled t-test, 5x2cv and McNemar's test held their 5% level; of the three, the corrected test detected a genuine difference of 0.043 most often (30%).
4. A naive test's high rejection rate under a real difference is not power; it is the same overconfidence.
5. Compare algorithms with the corrected resampled t-test on repeated cross-validation, or McNemar's test on a large held-out set, and report the size of the difference alongside its p-value.

## Exercises


1. Take two models you compared with cross-validation. Recompute the comparison three ways: the paired t-test on the fold differences, the same with ten repetitions of the folds, and the corrected resampled t-test, which multiplies the variance of the differences by $(1/J + n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}})$ for $J$ differences.
2. Then shuffle the labels and rerun all three, twenty times. Count how often each finds a "significant" difference between two models that both know nothing.
3. Explain why adding more repetitions of cross-validation drives the naive t-test's p-value towards zero whenever the data set's mean difference is not exactly zero, but leaves the corrected test's variance bounded below.

## Further reading


- Dietterich, "Approximate statistical tests for comparing supervised classification learning algorithms", *Neural Computation* (1998): the 5x2cv test, McNemar's test for classifiers, and the distinction between comparing models and comparing algorithms.
- Nadeau and Bengio, "Inference for the generalization error", *Machine Learning* (2003): the corrected resampled t-test.
- Bouckaert and Frank, "Evaluating the replicability of significance tests for comparing learning algorithms", *PAKDD* (2004): why ten repetitions of ten folds with the correction is a sound default.
- Bengio and Grandvalet, "No unbiased estimator of the variance of K-fold cross-validation", *JMLR* (2004): the impossibility result behind the failure of the naive test.

# From Section 2.3 (episode 36): Shuffling a Time Series Is Safe Until the Errors Remember

## In practice


- **Choose the scheme from the residuals.** Compute the autocorrelation of the out-of-fold residuals, in time order, at a short, a medium and a long lag. If it is near zero, the features carry the memory and shuffled folds are valid.
- **When the residuals are autocorrelated, use contiguous blocks.** Their gap from shuffled folds measures how much a slow unseen component flatters the shuffled score.
- **Do not treat forward splitting as unbiased.** Its folds train on half the data the final model sees, on average, so it tends to be pessimistic wherever more data helps.
- **Expect rough estimates when the residuals barely move.** With very persistent residuals, no split of one window predicted the future error well; widen the reported uncertainty rather than trusting any one scheme.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Simulated series with known structure.** Real series have seasonality, structural breaks and regime changes, each a slow component of the kind that makes shuffling optimistic. The settings here isolate the mechanism; they do not say how much of it a given real series has.
- **One horizon.** The future error was measured over the 250 steps after training, refitting once. A model refitted every step, or scored at a longer horizon, has a different target, and the comparison between schemes would shift.
- **No gaps between folds.** Blocked schemes are often run with a gap of a few steps on each side of the test block, to stop lagged features from reaching across the boundary. With two lags the boundary effect is small; with long lag windows it is not.
- **Means over twenty series.** Every estimate here is an average. On a single series the spread of each scheme is wide, and a single forward split, the most common choice in practice, is the noisiest of all.

## Key points


1. Shuffled folds are valid when the features carry the series' memory. On a stationary autoregression with its lags as features they were within 3% of the future error for all three models.
2. What cross-validation needs is uncorrelated errors given the features, not independent rows.
3. Shuffled folds are optimistic when something unseen changes slowly: 13% at residual persistence 0.9, 22% at 0.99, and 25% with a drifting level and a time index.
4. Forward splitting trains on less data and was 11% to 20% pessimistic for the drifting forests and at persistence 0.99. Contiguous blocks were the closest scheme in five of the seven hard rows.
5. Check the residuals of a shuffled-fold model for autocorrelation. If they have none, shuffling is fine; if they do, use blocks, and expect any estimate to be rough when the residuals barely move.

## Exercises


1. Take a time-series model you have cross-validated and compute the autocorrelation of its out-of-fold residuals at lags 1, 10 and 100, in time order.
2. If the autocorrelations in Exercise 1 are near zero, rerun with shuffled five-fold cross-validation and compare it with your forward estimate. Explain why the shuffled number is likely the more precise of the two.
3. If the autocorrelations are not near zero, compare shuffled folds with contiguous blocks. Explain why the gap between them is a direct measure of how much the slow component is flattering the shuffled score.
4. Explain in words why adding a time index as a feature made shuffled folds more optimistic for the forest on the drifting level, in terms of what a test row's neighbours in time reveal about its level.

## Further reading


- Bergmeir and Benítez, "On the use of cross-validation for time series predictor evaluation", *Information Sciences* (2012): an empirical comparison of cross-validation schemes on real series, favouring blocked folds.
- Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo, "A note on the validity of cross-validation for evaluating autoregressive time series prediction", *Computational Statistics & Data Analysis* (2018): the proof that shuffled folds are valid for autoregressions with uncorrelated errors.
- Roberts et al., "Cross-validation strategies for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical, or phylogenetic structure", *Ecography* (2017): a broad treatment of blocked cross-validation for dependent data of every kind.

# From Section 2.4 (episode 37): Rows From the Same People Are Not New Data

## In practice


- **Name the unit of deployment before choosing folds.** Ask what the new data will be new in (people, accounts, devices, sites, hospitals) and group the folds by that identifier, for example with `GroupKFold`.
- **Run both kinds of cross-validation and report the gap.** The difference between row-level and grouped scores is the part of the model's apparent skill that comes from recognising sources it has already seen.
- **Count sources, not rows.** Compute the intra-cluster correlation of the target within each source and apply Kish's formula. Quote the effective sample size alongside the row count.
- **Do not choose between data sets by row-level scores.** In this section, row-level scores rose as the data came from fewer people while true accuracy fell.
- **Spend collection effort on new sources.** When between-source variation is large, a row from a new person is worth far more than another row from a known one.
- **With very few sources, use leave-one-source-out folds** and state the uncertainty plainly.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Strong subject effects, by construction.** The signature and the propensity both have a standard deviation of 2, large enough that a person's rows are easy to recognise. Where differences between people are small, the gap between the two kinds of cross-validation shrinks towards the one-row-per-person case, where it vanishes.
- **Effects that do not transfer at all.** Here nothing about a person helps with a new one. In practice some person-level traits are measured, and a model that uses them generalises better; the problem is the part that is not measured.
- **Twenty subjects is few.** Subject-level cross-validation with 20 groups trains on 16 of them per fold, and its estimates are noisy from data set to data set. With very few subjects, leave-one-subject-out folds and an honest statement of the uncertainty are the best available.
- **Balanced subjects.** Every subject contributed the same number of rows. With a few heavy contributors, row-level scores are dominated by them, and the distortion can be worse.

## Key points


1. Rows from the same person share what makes that person different. With 2,000 rows from 20 people, row-level cross-validation scored five-nearest-neighbours at 0.825; on new people it scored 0.685.
2. As the same rows came from fewer people, row-level scores went up, from 0.737 to above 0.81, while true accuracy fell at every step, so row-level folds favoured exactly the data sets that generalise worst.
3. With 20 subjects, subject-level folds matched the truth to within 0.023, slightly pessimistic.
4. 6,400 rows from 20 people trained logistic regression to 0.788, about what 100 rows from 100 people gave. Extra rows from the same people added far less than their count, the pattern Kish's design effect describes for survey means.
5. Count people, not rows, and split the folds by whatever the deployment will be new in.

## Exercises


1. For a data set with an identifier for the source of each row (person, account, device, site), count the rows per identifier and compute the intra-class correlation of your target within identifiers.
2. Cross-validate your model twice, once with shuffled rows and once with folds grouped by the identifier. Interpret the difference between the two scores as the share of apparent skill that comes from recognising sources already seen.
3. Plug the correlation from Exercise 1 and your rows per identifier into Kish's formula. How many independent rows do you really have?
4. Describe a deployment in which row-level cross-validation would be the right choice, and explain why subject-level folds would then be too pessimistic.
