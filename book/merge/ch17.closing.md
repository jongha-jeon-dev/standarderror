# Closing material for Chapter 17: Clusters

# From Section 17.1 (episode 32): k-Means Finds k Pieces Whether or Not There Are Clusters

## In practice


- **Compare every clustering score with structureless data.** Draw each column uniformly between its minimum and maximum, independently of the others, cluster that reference the same way, and report the real score beside the reference score. A silhouette is only evidence of clusters if it clearly beats the reference.
- **Do not compare silhouettes across data sets as a measure of how clustered each is.** In this section the handwritten digits scored less than half what uniform noise did.
- **Ask whether the clusters you expect are round and of similar spread.** If they could be of very different sizes or elongated, k-means will slice them by its own criterion; consider a method with other assumptions and check its assumptions too.
- **Use several starts and look at what changes.** Rerun with different seeds and count how many rows change cluster. Report the clustering as unstable if many do.
- **Do not treat the lowest within-cluster sum of squares as the best account of the data.** It answers k-means' question, which may not be yours.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Two dimensions.** The shapes were drawn in the plane so they could be seen. In many dimensions the same failures happen and cannot be seen, which is the argument for checking clusters against a null reference rather than by eye.
- **Other algorithms draw other shapes.** Gaussian mixtures allow ellipses of different sizes, and density-based methods such as DBSCAN are designed to follow groups of any shape and to leave sparse regions unclustered. They were not run here, and each has its own assumptions and failures; Sections 17.2 and 17.3 measure them.
- **Raw pixels for the digits.** Clustering the digits after scaling or on learned features would change both the silhouette and the ARI. The comparison with the uniform square is about what the score means, not about the best possible clustering of handwriting.
- **Silhouette only.** The other common rule, the elbow of the within-cluster sum of squares plotted against $k$, has the same blind spot: the sum falls as $k$ grows on any data, noise included.

## Key points


1. k-means returns $k$ convex pieces of similar spread on any data. Here it recovered separated round blobs (ARI 1.00) and nothing else.
2. The silhouette rates the tidiness of the pieces, not the presence of groups. Uniform noise scored 0.40 at its chosen $k$; the digits never passed 0.19.
3. Real groups of the wrong shape are cut wrongly: ARI 0.09 for one large group with two small ones, 0.00 for two long parallel ones, with the silhouette picking 8 to 10 clusters for the first.
4. Single starts on the digits ranged from ARI 0.57 to 0.68, and the lowest sum of squares did not pick the start that best matched the classes.
5. Compare any clustering score with its value on structureless data of the same range, use several starts, and check that the cluster shapes the algorithm assumes are plausible for the data.

## Exercises


1. Take a clustering you have used and rerun it on a reference data set in which each column is drawn uniformly between that column's minimum and maximum, independently of the others. Compute the same quality score on both. If the reference scores close to the real data, the clusters you reported are mostly the algorithm's partition of space.
2. Rerun k-means on the same data with ten different single starts and count how many rows change cluster between runs.
3. Explain, using the within-cluster sum of squares, why k-means spends its centres on a large diffuse group rather than on two small tight ones.
4. Explain why the smallest attainable within-cluster sum of squares can only fall as $k$ grows, and why this makes the elbow rule unable to say that there are no clusters.

## Further reading


- MacQueen, "Some methods for classification and analysis of multivariate observations" (1967): the original description of k-means.
- Rousseeuw, "Silhouettes", *Journal of Computational and Applied Mathematics* (1987): the definition of the silhouette and its use for choosing the number of clusters.
- Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, "Estimating the number of clusters in a data set via the gap statistic", *JRSS B* (2001): comparing a clustering criterion with its value on uniform reference data.
- Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits, UCI Machine Learning Repository: the digits data, as bundled with scikit-learn.

# From Section 17.2 (episode 57): The BIC Counts Gaussians, Not Clusters

## In practice


- **Read the BIC's k as a number of components, not clusters.** Report it as "the number of Gaussian components of this covariance shape the data support", and say which covariance shape was allowed.
- **Check whether the chosen k grows with the sample.** Refit on a subsample; if the chosen k falls, the extra components were describing shape, not groups.
- **For a number of clusters, use a criterion that charges for overlap, or merge.** Compute ICL alongside the BIC, or merge the BIC's components hierarchically into clusters, and report both counts if they differ.
- **Do not treat a decisive BIC margin as evidence of clusters.** With thousands of points, margins far above 10 arise from non-Gaussian shape alone.
- **Fit several EM starts per k.** Raise `n_init` above its default of one, use k-means initialisation, and check that the best likelihood repeats before reading the BIC curve.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Two-dimensional shapes.** In more dimensions a non-Gaussian cluster can depart from Gaussian shape in more ways, so the number of components the BIC can justify plausibly grows faster. That was not measured beyond the digits.
- **ICL is not a cure.** It penalises overlap, so it under-counts clusters that genuinely overlap, which is where mixture models are most useful. On the digits it chose the same $k$ as the BIC with both covariance shapes.
- **k up to 20, three starts each.** More starts can only raise each $k$'s likelihood, which could move the chosen $k$ by a component or two; that was not measured.
- **Other mixtures.** Mixtures of t or skewed distributions describe heavy-tailed or asymmetric clusters with fewer components, but they move the problem to whatever shape they assume.

## Key points


1. For one cluster that was not Gaussian, the BIC chose more components the more data it had: 2 to 18 for a square as the points grew from 200 to 20,000. For a Gaussian cluster it chose one throughout.
2. From 1,000 points on these choices were decisive by Kass and Raftery's scale, so a decisive BIC margin is not evidence of separate clusters.
3. Three separated squares got 9 components from the BIC and 3 from ICL; the digits got 11 or 28 depending on the covariance shape allowed.
4. With one start per fit, an initialisation from random responsibilities never reached the likelihood that k-means initialisation reached 90% of the time.
5. Read the BIC's k as the number of components of the allowed shape. For a number of clusters, use a criterion that charges for overlap or merge components afterwards, and fit several starts per k.

## Exercises


1. Fit your mixture model at the k the BIC chose, then subsample your data to a quarter and choose k again. If the chosen k falls with the sample, the extra components were describing shape that only a large sample can afford to describe.
2. In the same model, find the pairs of components that overlap most, for example those sharing the most points with split responsibilities, and decide whether each pair is one group.
3. Explain, using the form of the BIC, why the number of components chosen for a uniform square must keep growing with $n$, while for a single Gaussian cluster it need not.
4. Explain why restricting a mixture to diagonal covariances raised the number of components the BIC chose on the digits.

## Further reading


- Schwarz, "Estimating the dimension of a model", *Annals of Statistics* (1978): the original derivation of the BIC.
- Fraley and Raftery, "Model-based clustering, discriminant analysis, and density estimation", *JASA* (2002): the framework that made the BIC the standard choice of k for Gaussian mixtures.
- Kass and Raftery, "Bayes factors", *JASA* (1995): the link between BIC differences and Bayes factors, and the scale for reading them.
- Biernacki, Celeux and Govaert, "Assessing a mixture model for clustering with the integrated completed likelihood", *IEEE TPAMI* (2000): the ICL criterion and its charge for overlapping components.
- Baudry, Raftery, Celeux, Lo and Gottardo, "Combining mixture components for clustering", *JCGS* (2010): merging the BIC's components into clusters.

# From Section 17.3 (episode 58): DBSCAN's Radius Is a Density, and It Gets Only One

## In practice


- **Treat `eps` as a declared density.** Choosing a radius, together with `min_samples`, is choosing how dense a region must be to count as a cluster. Report both with every result, and never rely on scikit-learn's default radius.
- **Sweep the radius and watch what changes.** Run at the chosen radius, half of it and double it, and record the number of clusters, the size of the largest and the noise share. If they change a lot, the clustering is a statement about the radius.
- **Do not trust the k-distance knee by itself.** It missed the good window once on the moons and on the digits landed just past the point where every class merged. Use it as a starting point for the sweep.
- **Check the largest cluster and the noise share.** Apply Schubert and colleagues' rules: a largest cluster above 20 to 50% of the clustered points or noise outside 1 to 30% calls for another look. Remember that a passing noise share can still hide a discarded cluster.
- **When clusters may differ in density, use a method that does not need one radius.** HDBSCAN handled a large density contrast better than any single radius, though not perfectly; its minimum cluster size is then the setting to vary.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Two dimensions, Gaussian clusters.** The density-contrast problem is cleanest in two dimensions; how it changes in more dimensions was not measured beyond the digits.
- **One `min_samples`.** Five is scikit-learn's default; Ester and colleagues suggested four for two dimensions. Raising it smooths the density estimate and moves the good window; it does not give one radius two densities.
- **One knee rule.** The knee was found by a simple geometric rule. People reading the curve by eye will pick other points, and the moons' window was narrow enough that a small difference mattered.
- **HDBSCAN's own setting.** Its minimum cluster size plays the part the radius played, with a gentler effect here. It was not swept.

## Key points


1. At the right radius DBSCAN separated two half-moons where k-means scored 0.25. Radii scoring 0.95 or more spanned a factor of 2.9, and in this draw the k-distance knee fell just outside them (in five other draws, reported in Section 22.2, inside).
2. The radius and `min_samples` act as one density threshold: raising one shifts the good range of the other.
3. With one cluster eight times the spread of its neighbours, no radius scored 0.9 in any of six draws; the best in the first called 77% of the wide cluster noise. HDBSCAN scored 0.82 to 0.89.
4. On the digits the knee's radius put 96% of the images in one cluster and scored 0.00; the best radius, chosen with the labels, scored 0.71.
5. Treat `eps` as the density you declare a cluster to have. Sweep it and look at what changes, check the largest cluster and the noise share, and when clusters may differ in density use a method that does not need one radius.

## Exercises


1. Run DBSCAN on your data at the radius you used, at half of it and at double, and record the number of clusters, the size of the largest and the share of points called noise each time.
2. In the runs from Exercise 1, find which clusters split or merge first as the radius changes. Explain why those are the places where the data's densities differ.
3. Explain why scoring noise points as one extra cluster, rather than as singletons, can make a radius that discards a whole cluster look good.
4. Using the definition of a core point, explain why raising `min_samples` moves the window of good radii to larger values.

## Further reading


- Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu, "A density-based algorithm for discovering clusters in large spatial databases with noise", *KDD* (1996): the original DBSCAN algorithm and the k-distance heuristic for its radius.
- Schubert, Sander, Ester, Kriegel and Xu, "DBSCAN revisited, revisited", *ACM TODS* (2017): a reassessment of DBSCAN with practical rules of thumb for checking its results.
- Campello, Moulavi and Sander, "Density-based clustering based on hierarchical density estimates", *PAKDD* (2013): HDBSCAN, which replaces the single radius with a hierarchy over all densities.

# From Section 17.4 (episode 60): The Most Stable k Is Not the Number of Clusters

## In practice


- **Test any rule for choosing k on data with no clusters first.** A single Gaussian cloud and a uniform square of the same scale as your data are a minimum. If the rule picks k greater than one there, its choice on your data is not evidence of clusters.
- **Report stability as a property of the method's answer.** "k-means at k = 4 is reproducible" is what stability shows; "the data contain four groups" is not.
- **Compare with a null that has no structure.** Use a method that can answer $k = 1$, such as the gap statistic, or rerun the whole procedure on data with the structure destroyed, for example by shuffling each column independently.
- **Look for more than one stable k.** When two values of k are both nearly perfectly stable, the data may have a hierarchy; report both levels instead of the coarser one alone.
- **Check the choice across resampling seeds.** On the digits the most stable k moved with the seed; report the range of k that are indistinguishable, not a single winner.

## Limits of the evidence


- **k-means only.** Stability was measured for k-means, whose objective has unique optima on these shapes. Other algorithms have other objectives and other kinds of stability; the argument of Ben-David and colleagues applies to any algorithm whose answer converges to a unique optimum.
- **One version of each method.** Stability has variants that compare to the full-data clustering, use bootstrap samples or normalise by a null; the gap statistic has variants in its reference distribution. Some of them can answer $k = 1$; this one cannot.
- **Two-dimensional shapes.** Four shapes in two dimensions isolate the mechanism. Whether higher-dimensional data tend toward unique or ambiguous optima depends on the data, and the digits are one example.
- **k from 2 to 8.** On the uniform square stability rose again from $k = 7$ to $k = 8$ while staying below its value at 4. A wider range was not tried, and could in principle find a more stable k for any shape.

## Key points


1. Stability chose $k = 2$ for one elongated Gaussian (0.92) and $k = 4$ for a uniform square (0.94); neither has clusters, and stability cannot answer one.
2. Stability measures whether the clustering objective has a unique optimum, which a clusterless shape can have; reproducibility is not evidence that groups exist.
3. It chose correctly for three separated blobs and chose the coarser of two right answers for two pairs of blobs.
4. The gap statistic said one cluster where there were none and three where there were three, and on the digits found no stopping point up to 15; stability chose 10 or 9 there, depending on the resampling seed.
5. Test any rule for choosing k on data with no clusters first. Report reproducibility as evidence about the method's answer, and compare against a null with no structure before claiming the structure exists.

## Exercises


1. Take a clustering you have reported and fit the same method, with the same k, to data with no clusters but the same spread: shuffle each column independently, which keeps every feature's distribution and destroys the structure between them.
2. Compare the shuffled clustering from Exercise 1 with the real one on stability and on whatever validity index you used. If the shuffled data score about as well, the index was measuring the method, not the data.
3. Explain why a perfectly round Gaussian cloud would give low stability at $k = 2$, while an elongated one gives high stability, although neither contains clusters.
4. Explain why stability can never choose $k = 1$, and why the gap statistic can.

## Further reading


- Ben-Hur, Elisseeff and Guyon, "A stability based method for discovering structure in clustered data", *Pacific Symposium on Biocomputing* (2002): the resampling-stability method and its rule based on the distribution of agreements.
- Ben-David, von Luxburg and Pál, "A sober look at clustering stability", *COLT* (2006): the result that stability reflects the uniqueness of the objective's optimum, not the presence of clusters.
- von Luxburg, "Clustering stability: an overview", *Foundations and Trends in Machine Learning* (2010): a survey of stability methods, their variants and what they can and cannot show.
- Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, "Estimating the number of clusters in a data set via the gap statistic", *JRSS B* (2001): the gap statistic and its stopping rule against a uniform reference.
