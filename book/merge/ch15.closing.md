# Closing material for Chapter 15: Missing, Selected and Shifted Data

# From Section 15.1 (episode 38): A Missing Value Is Information, Until the Process Behind It Changes

## In practice


- **Ask why each value is missing** before choosing an imputer. Find out, for each column with gaps, who or what decides whether it is recorded.
- **Fit the model with and without missingness indicators** and compare cross-validated scores. A small difference is consistent with gaps close to random; a large gain means the model depends on the collection process.
- **Do not judge an imputer by its reconstruction error alone.** Here the worst imputation with an indicator beat the best imputation without one when large values went missing.
- **Fit imputers inside the folds**, as with every preprocessing step.
- **Stress-test an indicator-dependent model** by scoring it with the gaps redistributed at random, which imitates a change in collection policy. If performance collapses, document the dependence and monitor the policy.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Artificial mechanisms.** The three masks are clean versions of what real data mix together. Real missingness is usually partly random, partly value-dependent and partly decided by someone, in proportions nobody wrote down.
- **Thirty correlated features.** Breast-cancer measurements are redundant, which is why iterative imputation does so well at random. With weakly related features, every imputation is closer to the mean, and the differences shrink.
- **One rate.** Thirty percent of cells, everywhere. Real gaps concentrate in a few columns and a few rows, and dropping such columns or rows is sometimes the better choice.
- **Small data.** With 569 tumours, the differences between strategies at random are about two hundredths of log loss; under the informative mechanisms they are several times larger, which is the point.

## Key points


1. Missing at random, the best imputation gave the best model: iterative imputation, error 0.44 standard deviations, log loss 0.091; the mean, 1.01, log loss 0.113. Indicators added nothing.
2. Imputation accuracy and predictive quality are different objectives: 5-NN imputation recovered values better than the mean and still gave a slightly worse model.
3. When large values went missing, the indicator mattered more than the imputation: 0.121 with mean imputation and indicators, 0.143 with iterative imputation alone.
4. When the gaps followed the diagnosis, the indicator model scored 0.999, above the complete data, and 0.817 once the gaps became random.
5. Ask why each value is missing before choosing a strategy, and treat a large gain from missingness indicators as a dependence on the process that collected the data.

## Exercises


1. Take a model trained on data with missing values. Fit it twice, once with imputation alone and once with missingness indicators added, and compare the cross-validated scores.
2. If the indicators help substantially, list the features whose missingness matters most and, for each, find out who or what decides whether it is recorded.
3. Score the indicator model on data where those gaps are redistributed at random, which is what a change in that decision would look like. Compare the drop with the gain from Exercise 1.
4. Explain why mean imputation is worse than iterative imputation as a reconstruction but better as a basis for prediction when large values are the ones missing, provided an indicator is added.

## Further reading


- Rubin, "Inference and missing data", *Biometrika* (1976): the original classification of missing-data mechanisms.
- Josse, Prost, Scornet and Varoquaux, "On the consistency of supervised learning with missing values" (2019): when simple imputation suffices for prediction, and why trees can treat missingness as a branch.
- Agniel, Kohane and Weber, "Biases in electronic health record data due to processes within the healthcare system", *BMJ* (2018): evidence that the timing of laboratory tests can predict outcomes better than their results.

# From Section 15.2 (episode 39): The Applicants You Refused Are the Data You Do Not Have

## In practice


- **List every input to the decision that produced your labels**, and mark the ones the model does not see. If the list has unmarked entries only, expect selection to cost only misspecification; otherwise expect miscalibration on the refused.
- **Do not trust AUC on accepted cases as a measure for everyone.** In this section it was too low in one world and too high in another.
- **Check calibration, not only ranking.** Selection on unseen information left the AUC intact while the predicted default rate for the refused was less than a third of the real one.
- **Prefer models that extrapolate sensibly** when they will be applied beyond the accepted region; trees cannot predict beyond the range they were trained on, and a misspecified linear model extrapolated badly too.
- **Budget for a random exploration sample.** A few per cent of decisions made at random gives outcomes from outside the policy's approvals, enough here to match a model trained on everyone. Use it to train the model, or at least to check it; recalibrating on it repaired the accepted-only model only where its shape was right.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One officer, one unobserved variable.** Real decisions use many unrecorded signals, some correlated with the recorded features. The size of the bias depends on how much the decision used that the model cannot see, which is rarely known.
- **Outcomes are clean.** A loan's outcome takes years and depends on the terms offered, which the policy also chose. The same structure holds for treatments and recommendations, with their own versions of these complications.
- **Random approvals of 5%.** Fewer would give noisier estimates. In practice exploration is often stratified, approving at random only near the decision boundary, which costs less and reaches less of the refused region.
- **Static.** Each new model sets the next policy, which selects the next training data. Feedback over several rounds can compound the bias measured here, and is not simulated.

## Key points


1. Selection on the features the model sees costs only misspecification: a correctly specified model predicted 0.453 for refused applicants who defaulted at 0.432; boosted trees, which cannot extrapolate, 0.337.
2. Selection on what the model cannot see miscalibrates it without warning: 0.137 predicted for a group defaulting at 0.486, with its AUC intact.
3. Part of that loss cannot be repaired by any model of the observed features: even a model trained on everyone predicted 0.394.
4. AUC on accepted applicants was 0.673 against 0.770 on everyone in one world, and 0.627 against 0.559 in another.
5. A small randomly approved sample recovered what a model trained on everyone would know. Find out what information the past decision used, and buy some outcomes from outside its approvals.

## Exercises


1. For a model trained on outcomes that exist only because of an earlier decision, write down every input to that decision and mark the ones your model does not see.
2. Compare the model's mean prediction with the observed outcome rate in the slice of approved cases nearest the decision boundary. Explain why, if the earlier decision used information you lack, that slice is where the gap first shows, and why its size bounds how wrong the model may be beyond the boundary.
3. Explain why selection on the observed features left a correctly specified model calibrated on the refused, while selection on $u$ did not.
4. Explain why recalibrating on random approvals repaired the accepted-only model in the straight world but not in the curved one.

## Further reading


- Heckman, "Sample selection bias as a specification error", *Econometrica* (1979): the classic treatment of selection bias as an omitted-variable problem.
- Lakkaraju et al., "The selective labels problem", *KDD* (2017): evaluating predictive models when outcomes are observed only for cases a past decision selected.
- Hand and Henley, "Can reject inference ever work?", *IMA Journal of Mathematics Applied in Business and Industry* (1993): why inferring the outcomes of refused applicants needs information from outside the accepted data.
- Section 15.3 of this book: covariate shift and why it charges only for misspecification.

# From Section 15.3 (episode 29): Covariate Shift Charges for Misspecification

## In practice


- **Separate the shift from the model.** When inputs drift, ask whether $P(y \mid x)$ has plausibly stayed fixed; if so, the cost of the drift is the model's misspecification, and the first remedy to try is a more flexible model.
- **Compare a flexible model on the shifted rows before reweighting.** If its accuracy holds where the current model's falls, the current model was approximating, and that, not the shift, is the problem.
- **Choose the metric before reweighting.** Importance weighting optimises the loss it is given. With a misspecified model it can lower log loss while lowering accuracy; report both.
- **Always compute the effective sample size of the weights.** If it is a few percent of the sample, the fit rests on a handful of rows, its variance is large, and the test population sits where the training data are thin.
- **Check for drift in $P(y \mid x)$ with labelled data from the new population.** No reweighting can correct a change in the rule itself.

## Limits of the evidence


- **The weights were exact.** Both densities were known. In practice the ratio is estimated, with a classifier trained to tell training inputs from test inputs or with a density-ratio method, and estimated weights add their own variance, worst exactly where the true weights are largest.
- **Only the inputs moved.** If $P(y \mid x)$ changes as well, the setting is *concept shift* and none of this applies: the Bayes rule itself moves, and no reweighting of old labels can learn the new one. In real drift the two usually come together, and telling them apart needs labelled data from the new population.
- **One dimension.** In one dimension the flexible model sees the whole range it will be tested on, up to the tail. In many dimensions a shift can move the inputs to combinations no training row came near, and every model extrapolates there. The lesson that misspecification is what the shift charges for still holds; a flexible model just runs out of support sooner.
- **A periodic rule.** The wave was chosen so that a line would fail. Where the true rule is close to linear over both ranges, a linear model loses little to the shift and weighting has nothing to correct.

## Key points


1. Under covariate shift the best possible accuracy need not change. Here the Bayes accuracy stayed between 0.832 and 0.839 while the inputs moved two standard deviations.
2. What the shift costs is the model's misspecification, exposed. A flexible model stayed within 0.018 of Bayes up to a shift of 1.5; a linear one fell from 0.816 to 0.536.
3. Importance weighting refits the model for the test inputs. It lowered log loss at every non-zero shift and moved towards the best linear model for the test distribution (within 0.048 of its accuracy), which at a shift of 1 was a worse classifier (0.680 against 0.758).
4. The weights shrink the sample: at a shift of 2 the effective sample was 6% of 500 rows.
5. Before reweighting, check whether a more flexible model makes the shift harmless, and check the effective sample size of the weights before trusting what they produce.

## Exercises


1. Take a model in production and split its recent scoring data by some input that has drifted: newer customers, a new region, a new device. Train a classifier to tell training inputs from recent ones, turn its probabilities into weights, and compute their effective sample size. If it is a few percent, explain why no weighting will rescue the model on the recent population.
2. Refit the model from Exercise 1 with a more flexible model, and once labels arrive for the recent rows, compare how far each model's accuracy falls there.
3. Explain why, with a misspecified model, importance weighting can lower log loss and lower accuracy at the same time, and why this could not happen with a correctly specified model fitted to enough data.

## Further reading


- Shimodaira, "Improving predictive inference under covariate shift by weighting the log-likelihood function", *Journal of Statistical Planning and Inference* (2000): the original analysis of importance-weighted likelihood under covariate shift and why it matters most for misspecified models.
- Sugiyama, Krauledat and Müller, "Covariate shift adaptation by importance weighted cross validation", *JMLR* (2007): extends importance weighting to model selection under covariate shift.
- Kong, "A note on importance sampling using standardized weights" (1992): the source of the effective sample size used in this section.
