# Closing material for Chapter 22: The Book, Measured

# From Section 22.1 (episode 65): Four Simulated Verdicts, Retried on Real Data

## In practice


- **Trust a simulation for whether and why a method misbehaves.** All four verdicts carried over in direction, at least in part.
- **Do not quote a simulation's effect size as a forecast for your data.** Re-measure how much on data like yours; every size here shrank.
- **Expect effects to depend on difficulty and rarity.** Tuning optimism and threshold costs were small on an easy task; probability inflation was smaller at a 10% than at a 2% positive rate.
- **Still apply the safe remedies**: report probabilities from an unresampled or recalibrated model, choose thresholds from costs, and select models on the cost you will incur, not on AUC.
- **Write up a failed replication.** A direction that fails to hold on your data is the more important finding.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Two bundled data sets.** The digits and the breast-cancer data are clean, small and easy. Most real problems are harder, which on the evidence here would move the replications towards the simulations' larger effects, not away from them.
- **Four verdicts of many.** These were chosen because they could be re-run with the original chapters' own code. Verdicts about time, people and selection (Sections 2.3, 2.4 and 15.2) need data with that structure, which the bundled sets lack.
- **Twenty splits of a small data set.** The splits share most of their rows, so the twenty results are not independent, and their spread understates how a new data set of the same kind could differ.
- **The same choices on both sides.** The replications reuse the original chapters' choices of models and settings; an independent replication would choose its own.

## Key points


1. Resampling for a rare class left the AUC unchanged on real digits and inflated the probabilities 1.6 to 1.9 times, against eleven in the simulation at a rarer prevalence.
2. Tuning 64 settings on 200 real tumours was 0.005 optimistic and bought nothing over the default; the simulation's optimism was 0.023.
3. A cost-derived threshold cut every model's cost on real data, but 0.5 was not the disaster the simulation found; the best-AUC model was the cheapest in 6 of 20 splits.
4. Directions replicated; sizes shrank on easy, less imbalanced data.
5. Trust a simulation for whether and why a method misbehaves. Re-measure how much on data like yours before quoting a number.

## Exercises


1. Pick one verdict from this book that matters for your work, such as resampling, tuning optimism, the cost-derived threshold or a leakage check, and re-run it on your own data with that chapter's code.
2. For the replication in Exercise 1, note whether the direction holds and how the size compares. Explain why a direction that fails to replicate is the more important finding, and the one worth writing up.
3. Explain why probability inflation from balancing should be smaller for a 10% class than for a 2% class, and why the AUC is unaffected in both cases.

## Further reading


- Morris, White and Crowther, "Using simulation studies to evaluate statistical methods", *Statistics in Medicine* (2019): how to design and report simulation studies, and what their results do and do not cover.
- Open Science Collaboration, "Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science", *Science* (2015): a large-scale replication in which effects shrank to about half their original size.

# From Section 22.2 (episode 66): The Book, Rerun With Five More Seeds

## In practice


- **Rerun single-draw results before they become conclusions.** A model comparison, feature ranking, tuned setting or cluster count from one split or one initialisation should be rerun on a few other seeds, varying both the split and the model's own randomness.
- **Report the range, not the draw**, and choose words the whole range supports.
- **Watch the margin.** Before writing "above 0.9", "none" or "most", compare the distance to that threshold with the spread across seeds. If they are comparable, the word is not supported.
- **Set automated checks from the range.** If you pin results in tests, take thresholds from several seeds, so that the check fails on a real change rather than an ordinary draw.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Twelve of many.** These were the verdicts that rested on one draw and whose functions take a seed. Verdicts averaged inside their chapter were not rerun, on the reasoning that their spread was already measured; some may still depend on choices other than the seed.
- **Five new seeds.** Enough to catch a verdict that fails often, not one that fails one time in twenty.
- **Seeds change different things.** For some functions the seed draws a new data set, for others only a new split or a new model initialisation; claims about real data cannot be given new data this way.
- **The same author checking the same code.** A rerun cannot find a mistake in the code itself, only in how much one draw was trusted.

## Key points


1. A pinned assertion protects a number against drift in the code, not against its having been a lucky draw.
2. Of twelve single-draw verdicts, seven held on five of five new seeds and two on four of five, where a threshold in the prose was tighter than the evidence.
3. Three did not hold as written: a heuristic's radius that missed in one draw and hit in five (Section 17.3), a share that was "most" in one split and 33% to 53% in others (Section 14.4), and an AUC threshold crossed in three of five reruns (Section 18.2).
4. The broken verdicts were observations close to the words chosen for them; the ones that held were averages or very large effects.
5. Before writing a number into a claim, rerun it with a few seeds, write down the range, and choose words the range supports.

## Exercises


1. Take a result you have reported from a single run: a model comparison, a feature ranking, a tuned setting. Rerun it with three other seeds, changing the data split and the model's randomness.
2. If the conclusion survives, report the range with it. If it does not, the original was a draw: rewrite the sentence about it before anyone else reads it.
3. Explain why a verdict averaged over many draws, or a very large effect, could safely rest on the evidence of one build, while a value just under a round threshold could not.

## Further reading


- Bouthillier, Laurent and Vincent, "Unreproducible research is reproducible", *ICML* (2019): why results reported from one seed fail to reproduce under another.
- Gelman and Loken, "The garden of forking paths" (2013): how results come to depend on choices made during an analysis, the subject of Section 4.2.
- Sections 1.1, 1.2 and 19.1 of this book: the error bar on a test score, the variation between networks that differ only in their seed, and a benchmark score as a sample of questions.

# From Section 22.3 (episode 70): Sixty-Nine Sentences, Measured, and One Held as Written

## In practice


- **Ask where a sentence was measured** before acting on it, and whether your setting resembles that one.
- **Measure the rules your team repeats.** Rules for reading a number or acting on it, such as which score is better or where the threshold sits, are the sentences that broke most often here, so test those first.
- **Rewrite conditional truths as conditions.** When a rule holds only in some settings, state which, rather than keeping the unconditional version.
- **Pin your numbers, and rerun them.** Automated checks keep reported numbers from going stale; rerunning on new seeds keeps them from being lucky. Neither replaces the other.

## Limits of the evidence


- **The sentences were chosen to break.** Each was picked because it is stated as fact and could be tested on a laptop. The count of failures says how often such sentences stop short, not how often the curriculum is wrong.
- **One author's classification.** The line between "failed" and "held under conditions" is a judgement. A different reader would move a handful of chapters, and the code lists every call so that they can.
- **Small data and simulations.** Most chapters used data sets of hundreds to a few thousand rows, or simulations built to show an effect. Sections 22.1 and 5.3 measured how far such findings travel; the answer was: in direction, usually; in size, not always.
- **Assertions pin what was measured.** They cannot catch a mistake in the code that produced a number, only a change in it.

## Key points


1. Of sixty-nine sentences, 38 failed as stated, 30 held under conditions and 1 held as written: fit every preprocessing step inside the folds. Most of the curriculum is true somewhere; the work is finding where.
2. The failures clustered in three parts: how a score is read, how a prediction becomes a decision, and how a language model is graded.
3. The broken sentences fall into four families: a score answering a narrower question than the one asked, a method reporting on the model rather than the data, rows that are not what they seem, and a prediction mistaken for a decision.
4. 936 assertions kept the numbers from going stale, and five extra seeds showed that three of twelve single-draw verdicts were luckier than the words chosen for them.
5. Before acting on a sentence about machine learning, ask what setting it was measured in, and measure it in yours.

## Exercises


1. Pick a sentence your team repeats about its models: a rule about thresholds, features, retraining or evaluation. Write it down exactly as people say it.
2. Design the smallest measurement that could show the sentence from Exercise 1 false in your setting, run it, and classify the result by this section's rule. If it held, write down the conditions you tested; if it held only under some, rewrite the sentence so that it says which.
3. Choose one verdict from the family "a method reports on the model or its assumptions, not on the data", and explain what question the method actually answers and what question it is usually asked.
