# Closing material for Chapter 8: Boosting, Averaging and Stacking

# From Section 8.1 (episode 14): Boosting Overfits Its Probabilities Long Before Its Accuracy

## In practice


- **Stop on the metric the output will be judged by.** If the probabilities are used, as risks, rankings or inputs to a cost calculation, early-stop on validation log loss or another *proper scoring rule*, a score that is optimised in expectation by reporting the true probabilities (the Brier score is another). Do not stop on accuracy.
- **Plot both curves.** For any boosted model, plot staged validation error and staged validation log loss against the number of rounds. If they bottom out far apart, the choice of stopping metric decides which model you ship.
- **Treat the learning rate as a speed, not a safeguard.** A smaller rate moves the best round later in proportion and widens the window around it, but the model still passes its best. Going below 0.1 bought nothing in the best log loss here.
- **Expect label noise to make matters worse.** With noisy labels, long runs cost accuracy as well, and probabilities much more.
- **Check calibration before using a boosted model's probabilities**, and recalibrate on held-out data if needed; Section 11.3 measures how many rows recalibration requires.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One dataset.** Breast cancer is easy, with a test error under 5%. On harder data, where many test rows sit near the boundary, the error can be expected to turn up sooner and further, as it did here once labels were flipped. That the ordering, log loss first and error later, carries over to other data is a reasonable expectation, not something measured here.
- **Probabilities can be repaired afterwards.** Calibrating a booster on held-out data, by *Platt scaling* (fitting a logistic regression to its scores) or *isotonic regression* (fitting a monotone step function), improved its log loss substantially in Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana's comparison, though the boosting they studied distorted probabilities towards the middle rather than towards 0 and 1, and recalibration was not run here. It needs a held-out set, and it repairs the symptom, not the training.
- **Checkpoints, not every round.** The curves are sampled at eight round counts, and the best log loss quoted is the lowest of those checkpoints on the curve averaged over splits. The best round is computed from every round, per split, and its median is reported. The two need not coincide: at a learning rate of 0.1 the best checkpoint is 30 rounds and the median best round 46.
- **Other boosters regularise more by default.** Libraries with leaf-weight penalties, row subsampling and minimum leaf sizes would be expected to reach the same place more slowly; none was run here. scikit-learn's classic implementation, with its defaults, is the plainest version of the algorithm.

## Key points


1. With clean labels, the test error of gradient boosting never turned up from round 30 to round 2,000, ending at 0.046.
2. Test log loss was lowest within the first few dozen rounds and 2.6 times worse at 2,000. The model's decisions held; its confidence ran away.
3. Each tenfold cut in the learning rate moved the best round about tenfold later. Cutting it from 1 to 0.1 lowered the best log loss; cutting it from 0.1 to 0.01 left it where it was. Shrinkage widens the window; it does not remove the need to stop.
4. With 10% label noise the error rose by under two points and the log loss more than tripled.
5. Stop boosting on the metric the output will be judged by. If the probabilities are used, that is log loss or another proper score, not accuracy.

## Exercises


1. Take a boosted model you have in use and plot its staged validation error and staged validation log loss against the number of rounds on the same axes. Mark the round your early stopping chose and the round at which each curve is lowest.
2. If the two curves bottom out far apart, find out which one your stopping rule watched. If the model's probabilities are used downstream, refit with stopping on log loss and compare the calibration of the two models on held-out data.
3. Explain, using the definitions of error rate and log loss, how a model's test log loss can rise steadily while its test error stays flat. Which test rows drive the rise?
4. Explain why a tenfold smaller learning rate should move the best round about tenfold later, and why it need not lower the best achievable log loss.

## Further reading


- Friedman, "Greedy function approximation: a gradient boosting machine", *Annals of Statistics* (2001): the gradient boosting algorithm and the case for shrinkage.
- Schapire, Freund, Bartlett and Lee, "Boosting the margin", *Annals of Statistics* (1998): the margin explanation of why boosting's error keeps falling.
- Mease and Wyner, "Evidence contrary to the statistical view of boosting", *Journal of Machine Learning Research* (2008): boosted classifiers that keep improving in error long after they should overfit.
- Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, "Predicting good probabilities with supervised learning", *ICML* (2005): how well different learners' probabilities are calibrated, and recalibration by Platt scaling and isotonic regression.

# From Section 8.2 (episode 41): An Average of Models Beats the Average Model, Not the Best One

## In practice


- **Compare an ensemble with its best member** on the same held-out rows, not only with the members' mean. An average that does not beat its best member is cost without gain.
- **Check each member's confidence.** Compute the share of predictions each makes with probability above 0.99. A member certain about most rows will dominate wherever it disagrees.
- **Calibrate members before averaging probabilities,** especially single trees and naive Bayes. Calibration removes overconfidence; it does not make a weak member strong.
- **Measure diversity directly:** the share of rows on which each pair of members disagrees. Averaging helps when members of similar quality disagree, and hurts when the disagreement comes from a much weaker member.
- **Do not cite the ambiguity decomposition as a promise of gains over the best model.** It guarantees improvement over the mean member for squared error, as convexity does for log loss; nothing is guaranteed for accuracy.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One data set where a single model is excellent.** Nearest neighbours alone scores 0.98 on the digits, leaving little room for any combination to gain. Harder tasks with more balanced members may leave more room; that was not measured here.
- **Unweighted averages.** Every member counted equally. Weighted averages and stacking are the obvious refinements, and their failure modes are the subject of Section 8.3.
- **A deliberately bad member.** A fully grown single tree is not something anyone would put in an ensemble on purpose; it is here because its certainty makes the mechanism visible. Any overconfident member, such as an unregularised boosted model late in training, does the same in a milder form.
- **Five splits.** The counts of combinations above and below their best member come from one set of five half splits and could shift with others; the tree's capture of every pair it joined with a less certain model follows from its probabilities and would not.

## Key points


1. Of 57 averaged combinations of six classifiers on the digits, 11 beat their best member and 8 fell below their members' mean accuracy.
2. Averaging probabilities weights each model by its confidence. A tree certain about every digit won all 819 disagreements with the SVM, the pair scored 0.825, and only one of the 31 combinations containing the tree, the one pairing it with naive Bayes, beat its best member.
3. The guarantee is against the mean member: for squared error, the average's error is the mean member's minus their spread, exactly. On diabetes that still left it behind ridge regression alone.
4. Log loss has a similar guarantee by convexity; accuracy has none.
5. Average models of similar quality that make different mistakes, calibrate them first, and compare the average with the best single model before adopting it.

## Exercises


1. Take an ensemble you use and score each member alone on held-out data, alongside the ensemble. Is the ensemble better than its best member, or only than the members' mean?
2. For the same ensemble, compute for every pair of members the share of rows on which they disagree, and for every member the share of predictions made with probability above 0.99. If the ensemble does not beat its best member, look for a member much weaker or much more confident than the rest, and rescore without it.
3. Derive the ambiguity decomposition from the expansion $f_m - y = (f_m - \bar f) + (\bar f - y)$, and explain why it says nothing about the best member.

## Further reading


- Krogh and Vedelsby, "Neural network ensembles, cross validation, and active learning", *NeurIPS* (1995): the ambiguity decomposition of an ensemble's squared error.
- Dietterich, "Ensemble methods in machine learning" (2000): why ensembles work, in terms of accurate and diverse members.
- Kuncheva, *Combining Pattern Classifiers* (2004): a book-length treatment of combination rules and diversity.

# From Section 8.3 (episode 42): Stacking Learns to Pick the Best Model, Given Honest Predictions

## In practice


- **Fit the meta-model on out-of-fold predictions only.** In-sample predictions reward memorisation; the meta-model will hand its decisions to whichever base model fitted its training rows most closely.
- **Print the meta-model's weights.** If they concentrate on one model, the stack is selecting, and that model alone is the baseline to beat. A large weight on 1-NN, a deep tree or a deep forest can be a sign of leaked in-sample predictions.
- **Keep two baselines.** Score the stack, the base model with the best validation score and a plain average on the same held-out data. Keep the stack only if it beats both.
- **Be sceptical of stacks on small data.** With few rows, the noise in the weights can cost more than the combination gains; a plain average or a single validated model may do better.
- **Budget for the cost.** Count the extra fits, and keep a test set that neither the base models nor the meta-model ever saw.

## Limits of the evidence


- **One task with one dominant model.** The SVM's lead from 300 rows on is large, which makes selection the right answer. With members of equal quality and complementary errors, the out-of-fold stack has more to gain and the average is harder to beat.
- **A linear meta-model on probabilities.** Meta-models on log-odds, with non-negativity constraints on the weights (as Breiman recommended), or with the original features as extra inputs, behave differently; the in-sample trap is the same for all of them.
- **Few repetitions at large sizes.** There are three data sets at 3,000 rows, and differences of a thousandth there are within noise. The in-sample stack's exact match to 1-NN is not.
- **Accuracy and log loss only.** A stack tuned for AUC or for a cost-weighted metric would choose differently, by the same mechanism.

## Key points


1. Fitted on in-sample predictions, the meta-model gave its largest weight to the model that had memorised the most: 1-NN (weight +12.9), whose accuracy it reproduced exactly, or the forest when 1-NN was absent.
2. Fitted on out-of-fold predictions, it chose the SVM and matched it to within 0.0035 from 300 rows on, never more than 0.001 above it; at 100 rows it lost 0.013 to the best member.
3. A plain average beat every member at 100 rows when no member was certain, and trailed the best member once the weak members fell behind.
4. Honest stacking on this task was selection; whether it combines depends on members that are good in different places.
5. Fit stacks on out-of-fold predictions only, and keep a stack only if it beats both the best single model and the plain average on data neither has seen.

## Exercises


1. Take a stacked model and print the meta-model's weights. Refit the meta-model on in-sample base predictions instead of out-of-fold ones and print them again. Which base model gains weight, and why?
2. Score three things on a held-out set: the stack, the base model with the best validation score, and a plain average of the base models. If the stack's weights are concentrated on one model, compare it with that model alone; if it is not better than the average, its weights are not worth their estimation error.
3. Explain why a 25-nearest-neighbour model cannot win the in-sample meta-model's trust the way 1-NN does, and which other common models can.

## Further reading


- Wolpert, "Stacked generalization", *Neural Networks* (1992): the original proposal of fitting a model on other models' predictions.
- Breiman, "Stacked regressions", *Machine Learning* (1996): the case for cross-validated level-one data and non-negative weights.
- Smith and Wallis, "A simple explanation of the forecast combination puzzle", *Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics* (2009): why simple averages of forecasts often beat estimated optimal weights.
