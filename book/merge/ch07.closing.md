# Closing material for Chapter 7: Trees and Forests

# From Section 7.1 (episode 11): Resample the Rows and the Tree Tells a Different Story

## In practice


- **Refit before you read.** Before presenting a tree as an explanation, refit it on a few dozen bootstrap resamples of its training data and count how often its root feature and its top-level features recur.
- **Check the margin at the root.** List the best split on each feature at the root and the gain of each as a share of the best. If several are within a few per cent of one another, the root is decided by the sample, and the printed tree should say so.
- **Report competing features and threshold ranges.** For each of the top splits, give the features that win across resamples and how often, and quote thresholds as ranges rather than single values.
- **Measure prediction disagreement on held-out rows.** Compute the share of held-out rows on which some resampled tree disagrees with the original. Those rows are where the tree's explanation is weakest; flag them rather than explaining them confidently.
- **Do not read importance or irrelevance off one tree.** A root feature is not thereby the most important variable, and an absent feature is not thereby irrelevant; a correlated feature may have taken its place.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Deeper trees are unlikely to be better.** Depth 3 is the readable case. Grown without a depth limit, a tree on these 398 rows has 16 leaves and depth 6. Deeper trees were not refitted on the resamples here, but every split below the top is chosen on the rows the splits above it selected, so they can be expected to inherit the instability above them.
- **Some data have one dominant split.** When one feature separates the classes far better than any other, the root is stable and the instability starts further down. The size of the gap between the best and second-best split at the root is the thing to check, and on data with many correlated measurements it is usually small.
- **Bagging is the fix for prediction, not for reading.** *Bagging* (bootstrap aggregating) fits a tree to each of many bootstrap resamples and averages their predictions. Averaging many such trees should give predictions that move far less between resamples than any one tree's; that was Breiman's point in 1996, and it was not measured here. Bagging also gives up the single readable tree. Sections 14.1 and 14.2 measure what is left of the reading once trees are averaged.
- **Unbiased split selection is unlikely to settle the contest.** *Conditional inference trees* choose the split variable by a significance test of association with the outcome, rather than by the raw impurity decrease. That corrects a bias that Section 14.1 measures, the tendency of impurity-based selection to favour features with many possible split points. But with five candidates within 5% of one another, the resample would still be expected to decide. Conditional inference trees were not fitted here.

## Key points


1. Two hundred bootstrap refits of a depth-3 tree on breast cancer scored 0.914 ± 0.015 on the same held-out tumours: the accuracy is a property of the data.
2. The root feature matched the full-data tree's in 50% of the refits, and the top three levels came out as 194 different feature sets in 200 resamples, none of them the full-data tree's (3 of 200 on another split, in Section 22.2): the structure is a property of the sample.
3. The root moves because the candidates are close. Here the best splits on five correlated size and concavity features were within 5% of one another, and greedy selection keeps only the winner.
4. 36% of held-out tumours got a different diagnosis from at least one tree. Instability in the rules is instability in the predictions near the boundary, where the diagnosis matters most.
5. Before reading a tree, refit it on a few dozen bootstrap resamples and report how often its root and its top-level features recur.

## Exercises


1. Take a tree you have shown to someone as an explanation. Refit it on 50 bootstrap resamples of its training data and count how often its root feature, its root threshold (to the precision you quoted) and its second-level features reappear. If the root survives in fewer than four resamples out of five, say so when you present the tree, or present the set of competing roots instead of one.
2. For the same tree, compute on your held-out rows the share of resampled trees that agree with the original tree's prediction. Which rows have the lowest agreement, and how does the original tree explain its prediction on them?
3. Explain why a stable test accuracy across refits is compatible with a different root feature in half of them. Your answer should say which rows contribute to the difference between two trees' accuracies.

## Further reading


- Breiman, "Bagging predictors", *Machine Learning* (1996): introduces bagging and the idea of unstable procedures, of which trees are the standard example.
- Breiman, "Statistical modeling: the two cultures", *Statistical Science* (2001): the Rashomon effect, many models of similar accuracy that tell different stories.
- Hothorn, Hornik and Zeileis, "Unbiased recursive partitioning", *Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics* (2006): conditional inference trees, which select splits with a significance test.

# From Section 7.2 (episode 13): Out-of-Bag Error Is Free, Pessimistic When Small and Blind to Copies

## In practice


- **Use OOB error only once the forest has a few hundred trees.** Below that, it describes a smaller forest than the one you fitted, and in scikit-learn it also counts rows with no vote as the first class.
- **Check the warning.** If scikit-learn warns that some rows have no out-of-bag prediction, the OOB error is inflated by bookkeeping; add trees before reading it.
- **Do not compare a small forest's OOB error with another model's cross-validated error.** The forest is handicapped. Score every candidate the same way.
- **Do not tune on OOB error and report the winner's OOB error.** Keep a held-out set for the final number.
- **Look for copies before trusting it.** Count exact duplicate rows, and identify any grouping variable such as a patient, customer or device. With copies or groups, replace OOB error with grouped cross-validation or a held-out set split by group.

## Limits of the evidence


- **OOB error is not free for tuning.** Each OOB error is an honest estimate for one forest. Choosing the best of many hyperparameter settings by their OOB errors reuses the same rows to select and to score, and the winner's OOB error is optimistic, as any validation score reused for selection is.
- **Gradient boosting has no equivalent.** Its trees are fitted in sequence, each to the residuals of all the earlier ones, so no tree is independent of a row it did not sample. Subsampled boosting reports an "out-of-bag improvement", which is a different and much rougher quantity.
- **Two datasets, one model setting.** Both datasets are easy and both forests use scikit-learn's defaults. The crossover to an honest estimate came between 100 and 500 trees here; it plausibly depends on how much a single tree's error exceeds the forest's.
- **Other libraries treat the no-vote rows differently.** The artefact measured above is scikit-learn's. A library that drops rows without an OOB vote, or reports them separately, would show less pessimism at 5 trees, and the same smaller-forest effect.
- **Duplicates are the extreme case.** Near-duplicates, such as augmented copies with small shifts or repeated visits by one patient, should leak less than exact copies but in the same direction; only exact copies were measured here.

## Key points


1. With 500 trees, out-of-bag error was within 0.004 of the held-out error on both datasets, about as close as five-fold cross-validation, for one fit instead of six.
2. With 5 trees it was 2.7 and 2.8 times the held-out error. Each row's OOB vote comes from about 37% of the forest, so a small forest is judged as an even smaller one; and scikit-learn scores rows with no vote as the first class, which supplied 46% and 33% of those errors.
3. Use OOB error once the forest has a few hundred trees, and do not compare a small forest's OOB error with another model's cross-validated one.
4. With duplicated rows, OOB error was 0 and the held-out error 0.027. With exact copies OOB error is not an estimate of anything; on grouped data, expect it to be optimistic.
5. Selecting hyperparameters by OOB error makes the winner's OOB error optimistic, like any reused validation score.

## Exercises


1. Fit your forest with `oob_score=True` at 10, 50 and 500 trees and compare each OOB error with the error on a held-out set. Note where the two meet, and whether scikit-learn warned about rows without an out-of-bag prediction at the smallest size.
2. Look for copies in your training data: count exact duplicate rows, and if there is a grouping variable, such as a patient, a customer or a device, refit with grouped cross-validation and compare with the OOB error. If the grouped estimate is noticeably higher, the OOB number you have been reporting was measuring memory.
3. Explain, using the probability that a row is out of bag for a given tree, why the OOB error of a forest of $T$ trees describes a forest of roughly $T/e$ trees, and why this matters at 5 trees but not at 500.
4. Explain why duplicating every training row drives the OOB error to zero but leaves the held-out error unchanged, and what this implies for a dataset with several rows per patient.

## Further reading


- Breiman, "Out-of-bag estimation", technical report (1996): the original proposal of the out-of-bag estimate for bagged predictors.
- Breiman, "Random forests", *Machine Learning* (2001): random forests, with out-of-bag error as their built-in estimate.
- Janitza and Hornung, "On the overestimation of random forest's out-of-bag error", *PLOS ONE* (2018): a further pessimism of OOB error in small samples.

# From Section 7.3 (episode 15): A Forest Cannot Predict Beyond the Largest Value It Has Seen

## In practice


- **Check the ranges before deploying a tree ensemble.** For each input, compare the range in the scoring data with the range in the training data, and count the rows that fall outside it. Pay most attention to inputs that drift, such as dates, sizes and prices.
- **Check the predictions against the label range.** If the outcome you expect is above the largest training label, a forest cannot produce it. Plot predictions against the drifting input and look for a flat line.
- **Validate on a split by the drifting variable.** Train on the lower part of its range, or the earlier dates, and test on the rest, alongside a random split of the same sizes. Prefer the model that does well on the split that resembles deployment.
- **Detrend before fitting trees when the trend's shape is known.** Fit the trend with a model that can extrapolate it and fit the ensemble to the residuals; add the two predictions back together. For time series, differencing or detrending does the same job.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Time is the common case.** A forest given a date or a time index as a feature is extrapolating on every future prediction. Trends, inflation and growth all live past the last training date, and a forest on raw levels will predict last year's level. Differencing or detrending first is the same fix as the hybrid above.
- **Extrapolation in one feature is not extrapolation in all.** A row can be inside the range of every feature separately and still far from any training row, in a corner of the joint space that no one sampled. Trees are flat there too, and no per-feature range check will catch it.
- **Linear models extrapolate confidently, not correctly.** The linear model here was right beyond the range because the truth was linear in $x$. Had the trend bent, it would have extrapolated the wrong line with equal conviction. The trees' ceiling is a known, bounded failure; the line's is not. Section 15.3 measures what a misspecified linear model costs when its inputs shift.
- **One simulation, one real split.** The size of the ratio between errors beyond and inside the range depends on how far past the edge one tests and how steep the trend is. The ceiling does not.

## Key points


1. A tree's prediction is an average of training labels, so a forest cannot predict beyond the range of the labels it saw. Past the edge of its training range this forest went flat at 2.87, under a largest label of 3.58.
2. Boosted trees go flat too: each tree is constant in an input beyond its last threshold, so their sum is as well.
3. Inside the training range the trees were the best single models (0.12 against 0.51 for linear regression); between 1.5 and 2 the forest's error was 22 times larger.
4. Fitting the trend first and the forest to the residuals was best everywhere: 0.11 inside, 0.12 beyond.
5. On diabetes, a forest that edged a random split lost by 40% on the highest-bmi patients and under-predicted them systematically.
6. Before deploying a tree ensemble, check where its inputs will fall relative to the training data, especially any input that drifts, such as time.

## Exercises


1. Sort your training data by the feature most likely to drift, such as a date, a size or a price, and train on the first 80% and test on the last 20%. Compare your tree ensemble with a linear model on that split and on a random split of the same sizes. If the ensemble's advantage shrinks or reverses, plot its predictions against that feature on the test rows and look for the flat line.
2. Then try the hybrid: regress the outcome on the drifting feature alone, fit the ensemble to the residuals, and add the two back together. Compare it with the plain ensemble on both splits.
3. Explain why a random forest's predictions cannot exceed its largest training label, and why gradient boosting, whose prediction is a sum rather than an average, is still flat beyond the training range.
4. Describe a row that lies inside the training range of every feature but is still an extrapolation for a tree ensemble. Why does a per-feature range check miss it?
