# Closing material for Chapter 19: Grading a Language Model

# From Section 19.1 (episode 51): A Benchmark Is a Sample of Questions

## In practice


- **Report a comparison as a difference with a paired interval.** Export per-question results for both models on the same questions and report the mean difference with its paired standard error, not two headline scores with separate error bars.
- **Count the disagreements.** The questions only one model got right, in each direction, carry all the evidence; McNemar's exact test is the test on them.
- **Treat leaderboard positions inside each other's intervals as ties.** A correct interval for each score says nothing about the correctness of the ranks.
- **Cluster by question.** When a question is asked under several prompts, samples or option orders, compute the standard error from per-question averages or with a cluster-robust estimator. Do the same for families of near-duplicate questions written from one template.

## Limits of the evidence


- **A pool, not a population.** The truth here is a fixed pool of 2,500 problems from five tasks. A real benchmark is a sample from a population of questions nobody has written down, and its questions are often chosen rather than drawn, which adds a bias that no error bar describes.
- **Exact answers.** Grading was by exact match, so every error bar here is pure question sampling. Graders that disagree with one another add their own variance.
- **Models of one family.** All ten models were trained on the same lines, and six are the main model or copies of it, so their errors are more correlated than those of models from different developers. The more correlated the errors, the more pairing gains; for unrelated models the gain is smaller, but the paired test is still the right one.
- **Questions drawn independently.** Benchmarks often contain families of near-duplicate questions written from one template, which cluster in the same way as the repeated prompts here.

## Key points


1. The models' 95% intervals covered their true scores about 95% of the time on average at every benchmark size; one score's error bar is right.
2. The ranking is not: on 100 questions the best of ten models ranked first in only 50% of benchmarks, and seven close models came out in true order in 0.4%.
3. On 300 shared questions the paired test detected a real 0.038 gap in 60% of benchmarks and the unpaired one in 2.0%; the paired standard error matched the actual spread.
4. Five prompts per question gave a true standard error 2.1 times the naive one, and the cluster-robust standard error matched it.
5. Report a comparison as a difference with a paired interval, cluster rows by question, and treat leaderboard positions inside each other's intervals as ties.

## Exercises


1. Take two models you have compared on a benchmark, export the per-question right-or-wrong results for both, and count the questions only one of them got right, in each direction.
2. Suppose there are thirty such disagreements, split 18 to 12. Explain why the honest summary is that the benchmark cannot tell the models apart, whatever the two headline scores say, and why the questions both models got right do not enter the argument.
3. Explain why seven models with correct individual intervals are almost never ranked correctly as a whole on 100 questions, while the best of them is ranked first half the time.
4. A colleague evaluates a model with three prompt templates per question and reports a standard error computed over all rows. Describe how you would recompute it, and when their figure would be close to right.

## Further reading


- McNemar, "Note on the sampling error of the difference between correlated proportions or percentages", *Psychometrika* (1947): the original paired test on discordant counts.
- Dietterich, "Approximate statistical tests for comparing supervised classification learning algorithms", *Neural Computation* (1998): a comparison of tests for classifier differences, and the background to Section 2.2.
- Miller, "Adding error bars to evals" (Anthropic, 2024): standard errors, paired differences and clustered questions for language-model evaluations.
- Madaan et al., "Quantifying variance in evaluation benchmarks" (2024): how much benchmark scores vary, and what that means for comparing models.

# From Section 19.2 (episode 52): A Leaked Benchmark Shows in the Set, Not in the Item

## In practice


- **Keep a fresh set.** For any benchmark you rely on, write or generate problems in its style that have never been published, and hold them back. They are the only contamination test that needs nothing from the model's developer.
- **Compare whole sets, not items.** Test whether the benchmark's mean score or mean log-likelihood differs from the fresh set's, with a standard error, rather than scanning items for suspicious ones.
- **Do not read a clean per-item scan as evidence that nothing leaked.** At two insertions the model-alone detector here scored an AUC of 0.545, close to chance, although the benchmark score was already above every control's.
- **Check that the fresh set matches.** Confirm on a model that cannot have seen either set that the fresh problems are as hard as the benchmark's; otherwise a difference in difficulty will masquerade as contamination, or hide it.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Verbatim contamination.** Every leaked line was the exact problem and answer. Real contamination includes paraphrases, translations and solutions without the question, which can raise scores while leaving even less trace in the likelihood of the exact item.
- **A small model and a short exposure.** A large model memorises single examples more readily, so its per-item signal may be stronger, and a benchmark seen across many training epochs leaves more trace than one inserted into a single extra pass.
- **Arithmetic answers.** The answers are a few characters long, which gives a per-item likelihood little to work with. Longer answers carry more evidence per item, which helps per-item detectors.
- **A fresh set that matches.** The set-level test is only as good as the match between the fresh problems and the benchmark. Before any training, the leaked set here was already slightly easier than the fresh set, which the controls absorb; an evaluator without controls needs the fresh set to be drawn the way the benchmark was.

## Key points


1. With each benchmark problem inserted into training 32 times, the benchmark score rose from a control mean of 0.551 to 0.800, while accuracy on fresh problems of the same kinds stayed within 0.513 to 0.542.
2. Runs differing only in seed scored 0.503 to 0.577 on the same benchmark; a single run is a sample.
3. At 32 insertions the contaminated model's likelihood identified leaked problems with an AUC of 0.621, catching 3% of them at 5% false flags; with the clean model as a reference, 0.745 and 24%. Item-to-item variation in difficulty swamps the leak.
4. Compared as sets with fresh problems from the same source, every contaminated run from two insertions on stood out from all five controls.
5. Test for contamination by keeping a fresh set the model cannot have seen and comparing whole sets, and do not read a clean per-item scan as evidence that nothing leaked.

## Exercises


1. For a benchmark you report, write or generate fifty problems in its style that have never been published. Score your model on them and on fifty of the benchmark's, and compute the difference with its standard error.
2. If the gap from Exercise 1 is bigger than the two sets' sampling error, explain why that is a reason to stop reporting the benchmark number on its own. If there is no gap, explain why that is weaker evidence than it looks unless the new problems match the old ones in difficulty, and how you would check the match on a model that cannot have seen either set.
3. Explain in your own words why a shift in average log-likelihood that is small compared with the spread between items can be nearly invisible to a per-item threshold yet clear in a comparison of set means.
4. The fine-tuned runs here lost ground on fresh problems while gaining on the leaked ones. Describe what an evaluator comparing the contaminated model only with its predecessor would conclude, and what extra run would have let them separate the two effects.

## Further reading


- Yeom, Giacomelli, Fredrikson and Jha, "Privacy risk in machine learning: analyzing the connection to overfitting", *CSF* (2018): the loss-threshold membership inference attack and its link to overfitting.
- Carlini et al., "Membership inference attacks from first principles", *IEEE S&P* (2022): calibrating each example's likelihood against reference models, and evaluating detectors at low false-positive rates.
- Shi et al., "Detecting pretraining data from large language models", *ICLR* (2024): the Min-K% probability score for detecting whether a text was in a language model's training data.
- Zhang et al., "A careful examination of large language model performance on grade school arithmetic" (2024): new problems written in the style of GSM8k, used to measure how much models' scores depend on having seen the benchmark.

# From Section 19.3 (episode 53): The Prompt Is Part of the Score

## In practice


- **Report the prompt with the score.** A score without its context, examples and format is not reproducible, and on these models the context alone moved a score by about a third.
- **Report a range over reasonable prompts.** Score under several variants a colleague would call equivalent, and report the spread alongside the headline number.
- **Compare models under the same prompts, and check the ranking under each.** If the ranking flips between equivalent prompts, the comparison is a comparison of prompts.
- **Choose a prompt on items you will not report.** Split the problems; select on one set and report on the other. The correlation of prompts' scores across the two sets says whether the selection found something real.
- **Do not read a context effect as understanding.** A score that changes with the examples does not show that the model used their content.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Context, not instructions.** These models cannot follow instructions, so the prompt variations here are positions and examples. For instruction-following models, wording and format are larger levers, and the variations multiply.
- **Two models, one family.** The two models share their training data and architecture. Models from different families respond to the same prompt change differently, which makes ranking changes more likely, not less.
- **Small sets.** The prompt search used 150 problems per set, so a difference of 0.04 between two prompts' scores is about one standard error of either score, and less than two standard errors of the paired difference. That is the condition under which selection does the most damage, and also the usual one.
- **Greedy decoding.** The models answered with their most likely output. Sampling with a temperature adds another source of run-to-run variation, which was not measured.

## Key points


1. With nothing in front of each problem the main model multiplied correctly 0.500 of the time; with twelve ordinary lines, 0.735.
2. The context changed the gap between models, not only their levels: the twin's lead on three-digit carries was 0.127 under one context and 0.012 under another, and the main model's lead on multiplication ranged from 0.050 to 0.168.
3. Examples with wrong answers changed no score by more than 0.005; a context's effect is not evidence that the model read it.
4. The best of sixteen prompts on one set of 150 multiplications scored below the prompts' average on a second set. Where the prompts really differed, the choice survived the second set.
5. Report a score with its prompt and a range over reasonable prompts, compare models under the same prompts, and choose a prompt on items you will not report.

## Exercises


1. Take the evaluation prompt you use and write four variants that a colleague would call equivalent: different separators, a different instruction wording, the examples in a different order, and no examples. Score two models you are comparing under all five on the same items.
2. If the ranking from Exercise 1 holds under every variant, report it with the range of scores. If it flips, explain why the comparison is then a comparison of prompts rather than of models.
3. You tried ten prompts and kept the best. Describe how you would find out, with data you already have or could cheaply collect, whether its advantage is real or the search's luck.
4. Explain why a context change that lowered every model's score by the same amount would be harmless to a leaderboard, and why the changes in this section were not.

## Further reading


- Sclar, Choi, Tsvetkov and Suhr, "Quantifying language models' sensitivity to spurious features in prompt design", *ICLR* (2024): how formatting alone moves few-shot accuracy, and the case for reporting a range over formats.
- Min et al., "Rethinking the role of demonstrations", *EMNLP* (2022): evidence that the labels in in-context demonstrations matter less than their format and input distribution.
- Zhao, Wallace, Feng, Klein and Singh, "Calibrate before use", *ICML* (2021): how few-shot accuracy depends on the choice and order of examples in the prompt.

# From Section 19.4 (episode 54): Multiple Choice Grades the Wrong Answers You Offered

## In practice


- **Treat a multiple-choice score as accuracy against those particular distractors.** State what the distractors are, and how they were made, whenever the number is reported.
- **Report generation where the use is generation.** If the model will be asked to produce answers, measure it producing answers; a high multiple-choice score does not show it can.
- **Test the distractors.** Rescore a sample of items with plausible near-miss options. A large drop means the original score was measuring how easy the wrong answers were to reject.
- **Check rankings under both rules.** If two models' order changes between generation and multiple choice, the comparison depends on the rule, and the rule should match the use.
- **State the normalisation.** Report whether option log-likelihoods were divided by length, and in what units.

## Limits of the evidence


- **Arithmetic, not language.** Real multiple-choice benchmarks such as MMLU (Hendrycks and colleagues, 2021) have options written by people, and are often scored by the letter the model picks rather than by the likelihood of the answer text. Scoring the letter is a third rule, with its own failure modes, and it was not measured.
- **Small models.** Large models can be asked to choose explicitly and may reason before answering, and the gap between recognising and producing may be smaller for them. The mechanism shown here, a partial answer that still beats unrelated options, is not specific to small models, though how often it operates in a large one was not measured.
- **Distractors of three invented kinds.** A benchmark's distractors are usually chosen to be plausible to people, which is closer to the near-miss column than to the random one; how close is a property of each benchmark.
- **200 problems per cell.** Differences of a few points between models under near-miss multiple choice sit within about one standard error; the large gaps between scoring rules do not.

## Key points


1. Three models that generated no four-digit sum correctly picked it from four options 0.955, 0.980 and 0.790 of the time against random distractors.
2. A model that produces the right leading digits can win against random options without being able to finish the answer; multiple choice measures something closer to recognition than to production.
3. The main model's score on the same sums was 0.955, 0.395 or 0.315 depending only on the kind of wrong options offered.
4. The main model's 0.105 lead over its twin in generating multiplications disappeared under near-miss multiple choice; against random options every model scored at least 0.99 on multiplication and mixed operations.
5. Treat a multiple-choice score as accuracy against those particular distractors. Report generation where the use is generation, and state the distractors and the normalisation along with the number.

## Exercises


1. For a multiple-choice benchmark you rely on, take fifty items and replace the wrong options with ones written to be plausible near misses, keeping the right answer. Score your model on both versions.
2. Interpret the result of Exercise 1. If the score barely moves, what does that say about the original distractors? If it drops a lot, explain why comparisons with other models on the original benchmark are comparisons of how easily each rejects those particular wrong answers.
3. Using the formula for an option's log-likelihood, explain why a model that generates a truncated answer prefers the shorter option among `10411`, `1041`, `110411` and `910411`, and why the same model can still prefer `10411` among random five-digit numbers.
4. Explain why length normalisation can matter for options that are sentences of different lengths but did nothing for most option sets in this section.

## Further reading


- Gao et al., *A framework for few-shot language model evaluation* (the lm-evaluation-harness, 2021 onward): the standard harness, which reports multiple-choice accuracy with and without length normalisation.
- Hendrycks et al., "Measuring massive multitask language understanding", *ICLR* (2021): the MMLU benchmark, a widely used multiple-choice test with options written by people.
- Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo, "Are emergent abilities of large language models a mirage?", *NeurIPS* (2023): how the choice of metric can create or hide apparent jumps in ability.

# From Section 19.5 (episode 55): Every Judge Ranked Itself First

## In practice


- **Do not let a model grade itself or a close relative.** Self-preference survived a threshold calibrated on the truth in every case measured here.
- **Check any judge against a random sample graded by people,** and compute the judge's pass rate and the people's separately for each system being compared. A gap that differs between systems means the judge's ranking is partly a ranking of resemblance to the judge.
- **Use the labelled sample to correct the estimate, not only to decide whether to trust the judge.** Report the prediction-powered estimate, with its interval, alongside the judge's raw pass rate.
- **Prefer the tuned form of PPI.** It uses the judge only as far as the labels support; here, with a weak judge, its interval was no wider than the labels' alone, where plain PPI's was.
- **Distrust a narrow interval from a judge alone.** Its width reflects the number of judged answers, not the judge's error.

## Limits of the evidence


- **A likelihood judge.** Real judges are prompted, are often asked to compare two answers, and can reason before answering. Their biases include position and length as well as self-preference, and their sizes differ from these.
- **Exact answers.** Arithmetic made the truth free, which is what let every judge be checked. For open-ended outputs the "truth" is itself a human judgement with its own noise, and the labelled set in prediction-powered inference inherits that noise.
- **Three tiny models trained on the same data.** All three were trained on the same lines, and the main model and the twin differ only in their order, which is the case in which a judge is most likely to recognise a contestant. A judge from a different family might be biased differently or less, and that was not measured.
- **Fixed threshold.** A judge that outputs a score rather than a pass could be corrected by a calibration per model; that needs labels for each model, which is the prediction-powered recipe again.

## Key points


1. Each of three models, judging all three models' answers by its own likelihood with a threshold calibrated on the truth, ranked itself first on every task: 9 of 9.
2. The weakest model, right on 1.3% of three-digit carries, passed 73% of its own answers. A model's own greedy answers, right or wrong, look likely to it.
3. Judging the other two models, the strong judges always put a strong model above the far weaker one; the weak judge misordered the two close strong models on carries.
4. With 40 labelled answers, prediction-powered inference removed the bias and covered the truth in about 94% of draws; with a weak judge the plain version was wider than labels alone, and the tuned version was not.
5. Do not let a model grade itself or a close relative. Check any judge against a small random sample graded by people, and use that sample to correct its estimate, not only to decide whether to trust it.

## Exercises


1. If you use a model to grade outputs, have people grade a random 50 of the same outputs, and compute the judge's pass rate and the people's on those 50, separately for each system being compared.
2. Suppose the gap between the judge's and the people's pass rates in Exercise 1 differs between systems. Explain why the judge's ranking is then partly a ranking of how much each system resembles the judge, and compute the prediction-powered estimate from the 50 labels as the number to report alongside it.
3. Using the variance of the prediction-powered estimate, explain why a judge that often disagrees with the labels can make the interval wider than the labels alone, and how estimating the judge's weight avoids this.
4. The judge's threshold was chosen so that its overall pass rate was correct. Explain why that does not prevent a ranking error between two models, using the main model's verdicts on itself and the twin on three-digit carries.

## Further reading


- Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", *NeurIPS* (2023): agreement between model judges and human preferences, the position and length biases of judges, and tentative signs of self-preference.
- Panickssery, Bowman and Feng, "LLM evaluators recognize and favor their own generations", *NeurIPS* (2024): evidence that self-preference in large models rises with their ability to recognise their own outputs.
- Angelopoulos, Bates, Fannjiang, Jordan and Zrnic, "Prediction-powered inference", *Science* (2023): the method of combining many model predictions with a few labels to obtain valid confidence intervals.
- Angelopoulos, Duchi and Zrnic, "PPI++: efficient prediction-powered inference" (2023): the tuned version, which weights the predictions so that a poor predictor cannot make the interval worse than the labels alone.
