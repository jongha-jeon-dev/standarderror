# A Prediction Interval Is Right on Average and Wrong Where It Matters

*A simulated regression whose noise grows sixteenfold across the input's range. A boosted model plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covered 90.8% of new outcomes with training residuals and 93.3% with held-out ones; split conformal prediction covered 95.1%, as its guarantee promises. In the noisiest tenth of inputs those bands covered 70.5%, 76.3% and 80.9%. Conformalised quantile regression covered 95.0% overall and 93.2% to 96.0% in every tenth, with a band 21% narrower. On the diabetes data at 90%, split conformal covered 97.2% of the patients with the lowest predictions and 86.3% with the highest; quantile regression trained on 250 patients did no better.*

## A band around every prediction

Most of this series has been about classifiers. A regression's prediction is a number, and anyone acting on it needs to know how far off it might be: a delivery estimate, a price, a dose. The standard answer is a band of fixed width around every prediction, plus or minus 1.96 times the standard deviation of the residuals, read as the range that will contain the outcome 95% of the time: *a prediction plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covers 95% of new outcomes*.

The test is a regression where the truth is known: y equals sin x plus x/2 plus noise, with x spread evenly from 0 to 10 and the noise's standard deviation rising from 0.2 at the left to 3.2 at the right. Such heteroscedasticity is common, since larger quantities usually vary more: a large claim is less predictable than a small one, a long delivery than a short one. A boosted model fits the curve on 2,000 rows, and each band is checked on 20,000 fresh ones, overall and in each tenth of the input's range. Everything is averaged over 20 data sets.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from standarderror.ml import deploy

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X, y = deploy._hetero(2000, rng)        # noise sd 0.2 + 0.3 x, x in [0, 10]
Xc, yc = deploy._hetero(1000, rng)      # calibration rows
Xt, yt = deploy._hetero(20000, rng)     # fresh rows
model = GradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(X, y)
q = np.quantile(np.abs(yc - model.predict(Xc)), 0.95 * (1 + 1 / 1000))
inside = np.abs(yt - model.predict(Xt)) <= q   # split conformal
print(f"overall {inside.mean():.3f}; x < 1: {inside[Xt[:, 0] < 1].mean():.3f}"
      f"; x > 9: {inside[Xt[:, 0] > 9].mean():.3f}")
```

```text
overall 0.969; x < 1: 1.000; x > 9: 0.864
```

## Right on average

Built from the training residuals, the band covered 90.8% of new outcomes: the model had fitted some of the training noise, so its residuals understated the error on new rows. Built from residuals on 1,000 held-out rows, it covered 93.3%: closer, but the residuals' standard deviation is the wrong summary of a distribution whose spread varies, because the 95% point of a mixture of narrow and wide noise is not 1.96 of its standard deviations.

Split conformal prediction, set out by Vovk and colleagues and by Lei and colleagues for regression, makes one change: instead of a standard deviation it takes the 95% quantile of the absolute held-out residuals, with a small correction for the number of rows. That has a guarantee that needs no model to be right: if the new rows are exchangeable with the calibration rows, the band covers at least 95% of them on average. It delivered: 95.1%, with a standard deviation of 0.008 across the 20 data sets, about what a 1,000-row calibration set allows. On the question the sentence asks, the conformal band is the honest version of the textbook one.

## Wrong where it matters

The guarantee is about the average over all inputs, and a band of one width cannot be right everywhere when the noise is not. In the quietest tenth of inputs every fixed-width band covered more than 99% of outcomes, wider than it needed to be. In the noisiest tenth the training-residual band covered 70.5%, the held-out band 76.3%, a straight line with a held-out band 80.2% and split conformal **80.9%**. One outcome in five fell outside a "95%" interval exactly where the outcomes were largest and least predictable, which in many applications is where the stakes are. The straight line, which cannot follow the curve, had larger residuals everywhere and therefore a wider band, 7.91 against 7.72, and covered the noisiest tenth better than boosting with the same recipe (80.2% against 76.3%): the worse model looked safer there only because it was less sure of itself everywhere.

The Coverage series on this site opened with the same point: coverage is a promise about averages. Barber, Candès, Ramdas and Tibshirani proved in 2021 that no method can guarantee coverage at every input without assumptions, which is why the repair has to bring one.

Conformalised quantile regression, from Romano, Patterson and Candès in 2019, brings the assumption that the noise's spread can be learned. Two boosted models estimate the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of y given x, so the band widens where the data are noisier, and a conformal step on held-out rows corrects the whole band by however much it misses. It covered 95.0% overall and between 93.2% and 96.0% in every tenth, with an average width of 6.76 against split conformal's 8.54: 21% narrower and right almost everywhere, because the width went where the noise was.

## The diabetes data

The replication uses the 442 diabetes patients, with disease progression after a year as the outcome, at a 90% level: 250 patients to fit, 100 to calibrate, 92 to check, over 100 random splits.

```python
d = deploy.intervals_diabetes(reps=100)
for k in ("split conformal", "CQR"):
    v = d[k]
    print(f"{k:15s} {v['coverage']:.3f} overall; thirds by prediction "
          f"{v['low']:.3f} {v['middle']:.3f} {v['high']:.3f}")
```

```text
split conformal 0.909 overall; thirds by prediction 0.972 0.892 0.863
CQR             0.904 overall; thirds by prediction 0.944 0.916 0.852
```

Split conformal around a linear model covered 90.9% overall, on target. Split by the predicted value into thirds, it covered 97.2% of the patients predicted to progress least and 86.3% of those predicted to progress most: the same pattern, presumably because progression varies more among the patients with more of it. Conformalised quantile regression, with shallow boosted trees on 250 patients, covered 94.4% and 85.2%, no better, with a band 11% wider. Learning how the spread varies takes data, and 250 patients with ten variables were not enough to learn it better than a constant. The repair that worked on 2,000 rows did not transfer to 250.

## Where this breaks

**One shape of noise.** The simulation's noise grows smoothly with one input; real heteroscedasticity can depend on several inputs or on unmeasured ones, which no model of the spread can follow.

**Exchangeability.** Every conformal guarantee assumes the new rows are like the calibration rows. Under the drift of episode 61 the guarantee lapses, even on average.

**Thirds of the predictions.** Coverage by predicted value is one way to slice; slices by any variable can show different gaps, and with enough slices some will miss by chance.

**One quantile model.** Other ways to let the band vary, such as dividing residuals by a fitted spread, behave similarly in kind. They all need the spread to be learnable.

## What to keep

1. A band of plus or minus 1.96 residual standard deviations covered 90.8% with training residuals and 93.3% with held-out ones; split conformal delivered its promised 95.1%.

2. In the noisiest tenth of inputs every fixed-width band covered 70% to 81%, and in the quietest more than 99%.

3. Conformalised quantile regression covered 93.2% to 96.0% in every tenth with a 21% narrower band, on 2,000 rows; on 250 diabetes patients it did no better than a fixed band.

4. Check an interval's coverage in slices that matter, not only overall. Use a held-out or conformal calibration, and let the width vary only where there are enough data to learn how.
