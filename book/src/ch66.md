# The Series, Rerun With Five More Seeds

*Twelve verdicts from episodes 3 to 59 that came from a single random draw, each rerun with the episode's own code on seeds 1 to 5. Seven held on all five. Two held on four: in one split 3 of 200 bootstrap trees reproduced the full tree's top levels where episode 11 said none did, and in one simulation the only informative principal component scored 0.889 against episode 31's threshold of 0.9. Three did not hold as written: the k-distance knee scored 0.80 to 0.96 on the half-moons instead of 0.57 (episode 58); a benign background changed the top feature for 33% to 53% of tumours, not most (18); grouped anomalies' AUC was 0.18 to 0.24, not always under the 0.2 written down, though far below chance (59). Those three episodes now say so.*

## Turning the method on itself

Every episode of this series ends its build with a list of assertions: the numbers the prose quotes, and the qualitative claims it makes, checked against the measurement so that the build fails if either drifts. That guards against a stale number. It does not guard against a lucky one. Many verdicts were averaged over repeated draws, but some came from a single split, a single simulated data set or a single random initialisation, and the assertion pinned what that one draw happened to show. The sentence under test is the series' own working assumption: *a verdict measured once, and pinned so the build fails if it drifts, will hold on the next draw*.

The last arc turns the series' methods on the series. This episode reruns it; the next four measure how choices compound, which problems more data cure, what a whole project looks like when every check is run, and what, in the end, survived.

Twelve verdicts qualified: each rested on one draw, and each episode's function takes a seed. Every one was rerun with seeds 1 to 5, using the episode's own code and the conditions its assertions state. Seed 0 reproduces every published value, so the comparison is only between draws.

```python
from standarderror.ml import course

ep, verdict, what, check = next(c for c in course.CLAIMS if c[0] == 58)
print(verdict)
for seed in range(3):
    r = check(seed)
    print(f"seed {seed}: {what} {r['value']:.3f}, held: {r['held']}")
```

```text
the k-distance knee falls below the half-moons' good radii
seed 0: knee's score 0.573, held: True
seed 1: knee's score 0.960, held: False
seed 2: knee's score 0.798, held: False
```

## What held

Seven verdicts held on all five new seeds: episodes 3, 5, 33, 40, 48, 56, 57. Some held with the same number to three decimals, such as the BIC choosing 9 components for three squares every time and t-SNE drawing a cluster ten times wider at 0.997 to 1.015 times the narrow one's size. Others moved and stayed far from their threshold: dropping the test result inflated the treatment's apparent effect 1.62 to 1.67-fold against a claim of more than 1.6, and a training-fitted encoding ranked its own labels at 0.773 to 0.781.

Two held on four of five. Episode 11 said that none of 200 bootstrap trees reproduced the full-data tree's top three levels; in one of the new splits, 3 did. Episode 31 said only the tenth principal component predicted, at accuracy above 0.9; in one new simulation it scored 0.889. Both points survive in substance: three trees in 200 is still almost none, and 0.889 is still the only component that predicts. The thresholds written into the prose were tighter than the evidence.

## What did not hold as written

Three verdicts did not survive in the form the episodes stated.

Episode 58 found that the radius at the knee of the k-distance plot fell just below the half-moons' good radii and scored 0.57. On five other draws of the moons it fell inside them every time and scored 0.80 to 0.96. The published miss was this one draw's: the knee sits close to the lower edge of the window, and which side it lands on depends on the noise. The episode's broader point, that the heuristic picked a wrong radius on the digits, stands; the moons no longer illustrate it.

Episode 18 found that explaining tumours against benign ones instead of all of them changed the top feature for 58% of tumours, and wrote "most". On five other splits the share was 33% to 53%. A large share, but "most" was this split's.

Episode 59 asserted that grouped anomalies scored an AUC below 0.2 on the k-th-neighbour distance whenever k was smaller than the group. On the new seeds the worst AUC ranged from 0.18 to 0.24: always far below chance, the episode's real claim, but above the 0.2 its assertion wrote down in three of five.

All three episodes have been revised to say so, with the range from this rerun, and the syllabus rows with them.

***7 of 12** held on every new seed.*

## What the failures have in common

The verdicts that broke were not the surprising ones. Each was a correct observation whose number sat close to the word the prose attached to it: a knee just below a window, a share just above a half, an AUC just under a round threshold. Bouthillier, Laurent and Vincent made the same observation about deep-learning papers in 2019: results reported from one seed often fail to reproduce under another, not because the method is wrong but because the margin was smaller than the run-to-run variation. Episode 20 measured that variation for networks; this episode measured it for the series' own claims.

The verdicts that held had one of two protections: they were averages over many draws inside the episode, or the effect was far larger than any plausible variation, such as nine components for three squares or a tenfold size difference drawn as none. A single draw is enough evidence for a large effect and not enough for a precise number, and a pinned assertion is only as good as the margin between the measured value and the threshold it checks.

The cost of finding this was small. The twelve reruns took about six minutes of computer time, less than writing any one of the episodes they checked, and they changed three of them. The practical version is a habit rather than a procedure: whenever a number is about to become a word, such as most, none, always or below a round threshold, rerun the calculation with a few seeds first and see whether the word survives the range.

## Where this breaks

**Twelve of many.** These were the verdicts that rested on one draw and whose functions take a seed. Verdicts averaged inside their episode were not rerun, on the reasoning that their spread was already measured; some may still depend on choices other than the seed.

**Five new seeds.** Enough to catch a verdict that fails often, not one that fails one time in twenty.

**Seeds change different things.** For some functions the seed draws a new data set, for others only a new split of the same data or a new model initialisation; real-data claims cannot be given new data this way.

**The same author checking the same code.** A rerun cannot find a mistake in the code itself, only in how much one draw was trusted.

## What to keep

1. Of twelve single-draw verdicts, 7 held on five of five new seeds and 2 on four of five, where a threshold in the prose was tighter than the evidence.

2. Three did not hold as written: a heuristic's radius that missed in one draw and hit in five (episode 58), a share that was "most" in one split and 33% to 53% in others (18), and an AUC threshold crossed in three of five reruns (59).

3. The broken verdicts were observations close to the words chosen for them; the ones that held were averages or very large effects.

4. Before writing a number into a claim, rerun it with a few seeds. Write down the range, and choose words the range supports.
